Triply-Periodic Minimal Surfaces

Blow a soap film across a wire frame and it pulls itself as tight as it can. Some of those taut films close up on themselves and repeat forever, tiling space in every direction like a crystal, and they divide what is left into two separate labyrinths that interlock and never meet. The gyroid is the famous one. It turns up in butterfly wings, in block copolymers, and in the stiffest lattices anyone has managed to 3-D print.

Pick one and it is built here, cell by cell, from a short trigonometric formula whose zero set traces the surface.

What you are looking at

A minimal surface is one that cannot be made smaller by nudging it: pinch it anywhere and whatever you save in one direction you pay for in the other. Soap films do this for you. A triply-periodic one keeps doing it forever, repeating in all three directions, and the result cuts space into two labyrinths that thread through each other without ever touching. The two sides are shaded differently here, so the colour tells you which labyrinth you are looking into.

Schwarz found the first examples in the 1880s, by hand. Alan Schoen found twelve more at NASA in 1970, including the gyroid, which he worked out existed before anyone could draw it. Most of these have no formula you can write down in closed form.

So where does the shape come from?

From a trick. Take a short sum of sines and cosines and ask where it comes to exactly nothing. That set of points is a surface, and for the right sum it sits very close to the minimal surface you wanted. The gyroid's is three terms long:

sin x cos y + sin y cos z + sin z cos x = 0

These are called nodal surfaces, and the catch is in the word close: a nodal surface is an approximation, accurate enough to design a lattice with and not the same object a mathematician means by the gyroid. The page labels every surface that is one. The formulas are all published, and the sources are named below.

Level moves the target from zero to something else. The surface stops being minimal when you do — but it stays smooth and periodic, one labyrinth swells while the other shrinks, and this is exactly the knob a materials scientist turns to set the volume fraction of a gyroid lattice.

How it is built

The formula is sampled on a grid, every little cube of that grid is cut into six tetrahedra, and wherever the formula changes sign along an edge the crossing point is found. Joining those gives the surface. Working in tetrahedra rather than cubes is what makes the result watertight without any special cases, and crossings are identified by the grid edge they sit on rather than by position, so the same point found from two directions is the same point and the surface never splits.

Only one cell is ever meshed. The formulas repeat exactly, so every other cell holds a copy, and the block is built by translating that one and welding the seams — the identical thing the generator does, measured there at some forty times faster than meshing the whole volume.

Printing one

These are the lattices people actually print: a gyroid at a few cells across is strong in every direction at once and has no flat faces to peel. The surface here is a sheet with no thickness, so Download STL walls it to the thickness you choose and scales it to the size you ask for. A wall of a millimetre or two on a 150 mm block is the usual range; thinner than that needs resin.
