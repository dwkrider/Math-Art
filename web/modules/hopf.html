The Hopf Fibration

The Hopf map takes a point on an ordinary sphere and hands you back a circle in space. Take a second point and you get a second circle, and the two of them are always linked, the way two links of a chain are, whichever points you happened to choose. If you could take every point at once, the circles would fill the whole of space, exactly one passing through each place, and none of them would ever cross another.

The small sphere below is the one you pick points on. Click it to add a point, or drag across it to lay down a whole curve of them, and the larger view shows you what those choices turn into out in space.

First, what a fibre bundle is

A fibre bundle is what you get when you hang a copy of one small space, called the fibre, over every point of another one, the base. The three below are about the simplest examples there are. You have met the first two already, even if not under that name, and the third is the one that explains why anybody bothered to give the idea a name.

The cylinder and the torus are what mathematicians call trivial bundles, which is a description rather than an insult: the whole thing is nothing more than the base and the fibre set side by side, and if you forgot about the fibres you would have lost nothing. The Möbius strip is different. Its fibres have been glued together with a half turn in them, and however you try to relabel the pieces the twist refuses to go away. That stubbornness is the interesting part, and it is the reason bundles get studied as something other than products. The Hopf fibration is the celebrated case. It hangs a circle over every point of a sphere, and the twisting is so thorough that you cannot pull any two of the circles apart.

What you are looking at

The base is an ordinary sphere. Over each point of it sits a circle, but the circle does not live in our space; it lives on the three-sphere, which is the set of all points at distance one from the origin in four dimensions. To get that onto a screen the page uses stereographic projection, the old map-maker's trick: stand at the north pole of the three-sphere and cast every point down onto the flat space beneath. This particular projection happens to send circles to circles, so each fibre arrives as a perfect ring, and the whole picture is really the shadow of something one dimension up. If you have ever watched a tesseract turning on a screen, it was the same projection doing the work.

One fibre needs a word of explanation. The point at the very bottom of the base sphere has a circle that passes through the pole we are projecting from, and a circle through the pole comes out of the projection as a straight line. That is the axis you can see running up through the middle of the picture. It is not a special kind of fibre, only a circle whose far side has been sent off to infinity. If you untick Axis fibre it goes away, along with the other fibres that the projection flings too far out to be worth drawing.

Each fibre is drawn in the colour of the base point it belongs to, with the hue going round the sphere and the lightness running from one pole to the other, so that you can always match a ring in one view with a point in the other. The Nested tori preset gives you the classic picture. Each circle of latitude on the base lifts to a torus; the tori sit one inside the next without touching, and between them they account for all of space. The circles lying on any one torus are its Villarceau circles, and these come in two families, which is what the Ruling control switches between. The second family belongs to the mirror-image fibration, a genuinely different map that happens to produce the same picture.

Some things to try

Two points. Choose Two fibres. The page works out the linking number of the two rings from the curves themselves, actually computing it rather than looking it up, and reports the answer under the view. It comes out as 1 whichever two points you pick. That number is the reason the family can never be combed flat, and it is essentially what Hopf proved in 1931: that here is a map from the three-sphere to the two-sphere which cannot be deformed down to a constant.

The latitude slider. Put a single ring of points on the base and slide it slowly from one pole to the other. The torus its fibres make begins as a thin sleeve wrapped round the axis, swells as the ring moves down, is perfectly balanced when the ring reaches the equator, and then closes back down onto the unit circle. It is worth knowing, while you watch this, that every one of those tori is flat in the geometry of the three-sphere, however bent it looks once it has been projected.

The flow. Press Play flow. What this does is rotate the three-sphere before the projection happens, which carries every fibre along onto the next one in the family while leaving the picture as a whole unchanged. The rings slide through one another and never collide. Of everything on the page this is the thing that persuades me the circles really do fill space, rather than merely looking as if they might.

Winding. Turn p or q up and the fibre stops being a Hopf circle and becomes a torus knot drawn on the same surface. When p and q have no common factor you get one knotted loop; when they share a factor it comes apart into a link of several.
