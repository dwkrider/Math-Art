The Hopf Fibration

Take an ordinary ball and put your finger on it. The Hopf map hands you back, for that one point, a circle out in space. Put your finger on a second point and you get a second circle, and the two circles are linked, the way two links of a chain are, whichever two points you chose. If you could touch every point at once, the circles would fill the whole of space, exactly one passing through each place, and no two of them would ever cross.

The small sphere below is the ball. Click it to add a point, or drag across it to lay down a whole curve of them, and the larger view shows what those points become out in space.

First, what a fibre bundle is

Take a strip of paper and tape its two short ends together. You have a cylinder. Do it again with a half twist in the strip and you have a Möbius strip. Bend a length of garden hose round until its ends meet and you have a torus, the surface of a doughnut. Each of these is a fibre bundle: a shape made by hanging a copy of one small space, the fibre, over every point of another, the base. For the cylinder the base is a circle and the fibre is a short line, one standing over each point of the circle. The three below are the simplest examples there are, and the third is the one that explains why anybody bothered to give the idea a name.

The cylinder and the torus are what mathematicians call trivial bundles. Trivial is a description, not an insult: the whole thing is nothing more than the base and the fibre set side by side, and if you forgot the fibres were there you would have lost nothing. The Möbius strip is different. Its fibres have been glued together with a half turn in them, and however you relabel the pieces the twist refuses to go away. Push it along the strip and it turns up somewhere else. That stubbornness is the interesting part, and it is the reason bundles are studied as something other than products. The Hopf fibration is the celebrated case. It hangs a circle over every point of a sphere, and the twisting is so thorough that no two of the circles can be pulled apart.

What you are looking at

The base is an ordinary sphere. Over each point of it sits a circle, but the circle does not live in three-dimensional space. It lives on the three-sphere, the set of all points at distance one from the origin in four dimensions, which is to an ordinary sphere what an ordinary sphere is to a circle. To get that onto a screen the page uses the old map-maker's trick of stereographic projection. Picture a glass globe with a lamp at its north pole and a sheet of paper laid flat beneath it. Every point of the globe casts a shadow onto the paper, and the whole globe, bar the pole itself, lands on the flat sheet. The page does the same thing one dimension up, casting the three-sphere down onto ordinary space. This particular projection happens to send circles to circles, so each fibre arrives as a perfect ring, and the whole picture is the shadow of something one dimension higher.

One fibre needs a word of explanation. The point at the very bottom of the base sphere has a circle that passes through the pole the page is projecting from, and a circle through the pole comes out of the projection as a straight line. That is the axis running up through the middle of the picture. It is not a special kind of fibre, only a circle whose far side has been sent off to infinity.

Its neighbours are worth watching too. A base point close to the south pole, but not quite on it, gives a circle that is merely enormous rather than infinite, and the picture keeps only the part of it that fits. Those are the fibres that sweep out of the view and never come back: they do close, far outside the frame. The count under the view says how many are being shown as arcs this way. Untick Axis fibre and they go, the straight one with them.

Each fibre is drawn in the colour of its base point, with the hue going round the sphere and the lightness running from one pole to the other, so a ring in one view can always be matched to a point in the other. Choose the Nested tori preset for the classic picture. Each circle of latitude on the base lifts to a torus; the tori sit one inside the next without touching, and between them they account for all of space. The circles lying on any one torus are its Villarceau circles. Cut a bagel with a single flat slice through its centre at just the right slant and the cut edge is not the two ovals you expect but two perfect circles, crossing each other. Those are Villarceau circles, and every torus carries two families of them, which is what the Ruling control switches between. The second family belongs to the mirror-image fibration, a genuinely different map that happens to produce the same picture.

Some things to try

Two points. Choose Two fibres. The page works out the linking number of the two rings, the count of how many times one threads through the other, and it works it out from the curves themselves rather than looking it up. The answer appears under the view. It is 1 whichever two points you pick. That number is the reason the family can never be combed flat, and it is essentially what Hopf proved in 1931: that here is a map from the three-sphere to the two-sphere which cannot be deformed down to a constant.

The latitude slider. Put a single ring of points on the base and slide it slowly from one pole to the other. Watch the torus its fibres make. It begins as a thin sleeve wrapped round the axis, swells as the ring moves down, is perfectly balanced when the ring reaches the equator, and then closes back down onto the unit circle. Every one of those tori is flat in the geometry of the three-sphere, however bent it looks once it has been projected, in the way that a sheet of paper rolled into a tube is still flat to anything living on it.

The flow. Press Play flow. This rotates the three-sphere before the projection happens, which carries every fibre along onto the next one in the family while leaving the picture as a whole unchanged. The rings slide through one another and never collide. Of everything on the page this is the best evidence that the circles really do fill space, rather than merely looking as if they might.

Winding. Turn either winding number up and the fibre stops being a Hopf circle and becomes a torus knot drawn on the same surface. The two numbers count how many times the loop winds round the torus in each of its two directions. When they have no common factor you get one knotted loop; when they share a factor it comes apart into a link of several.
