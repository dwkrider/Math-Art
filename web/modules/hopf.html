The Hopf Fibration

Pick a point on a sphere. The Hopf map turns it into a circle in space. Pick another point and you get another circle — and the two are linked, like two rings of a chain, no matter which points you chose. Pick every point and the circles fill the whole of space, one through each place, never crossing.

Click the small sphere below to choose points; drag across it to draw a curve of them. What appears beside it is what those choices look like out in space.

First, what a fibre bundle is

A fibre bundle is a space built by hanging one small space — the fibre — over every point of another — the base. Two of them you already know, and one shows why they are worth a name at all.

The first two are trivial: the total space is nothing more than the base and the fibre side by side, and you lose nothing by forgetting the fibres. The Möbius strip is not. Its fibres are glued with a half turn, and no relabelling makes that twist go away — which is what makes a bundle worth studying rather than just a product. The Hopf fibration is the famous one: circles over a sphere, twisted so thoroughly that no two of them can be pulled apart.

What you are looking at

The base is an ordinary sphere. Over each point of it sits a circle, living not in our space but on the three-sphere — the set of points at distance one from the origin in four dimensions. To draw that on a screen it is projected down by stereographic projection: stand at the north pole of the three-sphere and cast every point onto the flat space below it. Circles stay circles, so each fibre arrives as a perfect ring, and the whole picture is the shadow of something one dimension up. If you have seen a rotating tesseract, it is the same projection.

One fibre is special. The point at the very bottom of the base sphere sends its circle through the projection pole, and a circle through the pole opens out into a straight line: that is the axis running through the middle of the picture. It is not a different kind of fibre — it is a circle whose far side has been sent to infinity. Turn Axis fibre off and it disappears with the other fibres the projection throws too far out.

Every fibre is drawn in the colour of the point it comes from — hue around the sphere, lightness from pole to pole — which is how the two views stay tied together. Choose Nested tori and you see the classic picture: each circle of latitude on the base lifts to a torus, the tori nest inside one another without touching, and together they fill space. The circles lying on each torus are its Villarceau circles, and the Ruling control switches between the two families they come in — the second being the mirror fibration, which is a different map with the same picture.

Things worth trying

Two points. Choose Two fibres. The page computes the linking number of the two rings from the curves themselves and reports it under the view. It is 1, for any two points you pick — that number is the reason the family cannot be combed flat, and it is what Hopf proved in 1931: a map from the three-sphere to the two-sphere that cannot be deformed to a constant.

The latitude slider. Put one ring of points on the base and slide it from pole to pole. The torus its fibres make starts as a thin sleeve round the axis, swells until at the equator it is perfectly balanced, then closes down onto the unit circle. Every one of those tori is flat in the geometry of the three-sphere, however bent it looks here.

The flow. Press Play flow. This rotates the three-sphere before projecting, which slides every fibre along the family while keeping the whole picture identical to itself — the fibres move through one another without ever colliding. It is the clearest evidence that these circles genuinely fill space.

Winding. Raise p or q and the fibre stops being the Hopf circle and becomes a torus knot drawn on the same surface. With p and q coprime you get a single knotted loop; sharing a factor breaks it into a link of several.
