Dirac's Belt Trick

A solid sits in the middle of a cage, with a belt running from each of its faces out to the bars. Drag the turn slider below to 360°, one full revolution, or press the 360° button. The solid is back exactly where it started. The belts are not. They are wound round it, and you can slide them about as much as you like without ever getting them straight; the only ways out are to turn the solid back, cut a belt, or pass one belt through another. Now keep going. Somewhere in the second revolution the belts work themselves loose, and by 720° everything is exactly as it was.

So one turn and no turn look the same to the solid, and the belts can tell the difference. That is a fact about the geometry of rotation itself, nothing to do with belts in particular, and it is the same fact that lets the quantum state of an electron come back as its own negative after it has been turned once round. In 1975 two groups saw that sign, by turning one beam of neutrons and comparing it with another they had left alone.

What the belts are counting

Look only at the solid and a turn of 360° is indistinguishable from no turn at all; every face is back where it was. The belts remember how it got there, though not in full. They do not count the turns. They only record whether the count is odd or even. Roger Penrose describes exactly this arrangement, a book tied to a pile of books by a belt, and says the belt “keeps track of the parity of the number of 2π rotations … rather than totting up the entire number.”

There is a tidy mathematical way to put this. Follow the solid from its starting position, through its turning, and back to where it began. What you have traced is a loop in the space of rotations, a path that ends where it started. A loop that turns once cannot be shrunk, by any sequence of small changes, down to the loop that does nothing at all. A loop that turns twice can be, and the belts are that shrinking made visible. Watch any one belt through the whole cycle: its end at the solid makes the full double turn, its end at the cage stays put, and every point in between traces a loop somewhere part-way between the two. Topologists write the fact as π1(SO(3)) = ℤ/2, which says that loops in the space of rotations come in exactly two kinds, up to deformation. Rotations carry a hidden two-valued label, so every orientation of the solid exists in two versions, and the belts tell you which one you are in. A rotation matrix throws that label away. The unit quaternions keep it, because each rotation appears among them twice, as a number and as its negative.

Why it matters

Every particle of spin ½, which includes the electron, the proton and the neutron, is described in quantum mechanics by a wave that turns into its own negative when the particle is rotated through 360° and only comes back to itself after 720°. An object that behaves like this is called a spinor, as opposed to a vector, which is anything you could draw as an arrow and which a full turn leaves exactly as it was. On first hearing a spinor sounds absurd, since a full turn ought to change nothing. The belt trick is the loophole. The geometry of rotation has room for exactly one bit of memory, even or odd, and a spinor is simply something that carries that bit.

You cannot see the sign of a single wave. The flip only shows itself when a wave that has been turned is compared with one that has not, and in 1975 two groups managed exactly that comparison. Samuel Werner's group in the United States and Helmut Rauch's in Vienna each split a beam of neutrons in a silicon-crystal interferometer, sent one half through a magnetic field so that the neutrons' spins precessed, and brought the halves back together. As the field was turned up the interference pattern swung back and forth. It repeated itself not when the spins had been turned through 360° but when they had been turned through 720°. Two full turns, which is just what the belts ask for.

The idea goes further than single particles. Richard Feynman, in his 1986 lecture in Dirac's memory, picked up an argument from David Finkelstein. Join two particles by a ribbon and swap them over, and you will find a full turn has gone into the ribbon. So exchanging two spin-½ particles ought to flip the sign as well, and that is the root of Pauli's exclusion principle, the rule that no two electrons can occupy the same state. Feynman was careful to offer this as a way of seeing it rather than as a proof, but the principle it points at is why atoms have shells and why solid matter holds itself up instead of collapsing.

What the trick does not show

The belts are not a picture of an electron. There is nothing tied to an electron, and Penrose is careful to call the belt an “imaginary flexible attachment.” What the trick does show is that a spin-½ particle is possible, because turning through 360° is not quite the same as doing nothing while turning through 720° is. Whether electrons, protons and neutrons really are particles of that kind is a question about nature rather than geometry. It is the neutron experiments, not the belts, that answer it.

Where it comes from

Paul Dirac used the trick in his lectures for years, to make the predicted behaviour of spin ½ feel a little less outrageous. His own version had no belt in it at all. He used a pair of scissors with string threaded through the handles and tied off to a chair, and it was Max Newman who published the proof, in 1942, that an odd number of turns leaves a tangle and an even number does not. The same thing turns up as the plate trick, or the cup trick, and you can do it with no equipment but your arm. Balance a cup on your palm and turn it once, keeping it upright. Your arm is now twisted up under it. Turn it once more the same way and your arm comes straight again. Feynman did this with a cup in that 1986 lecture, and dancers in Indonesia and the Philippines have been doing it with candles and plates for a great deal longer. Engineers make use of it too. It is how a cable can be run to a platform that spins for ever without the cable ever winding itself up.
