Scherk-Collins Sculptures

Hold a saddle in your hands — a surface that curves up one way and down the other, like a crisp or a mountain pass. Stack a column of them, each turned a little from the one below, and you have a shape Scherk wrote down in 1835. Bend that column round until its ends meet and you have the form the sculptor Brent Collins carved in wood, and that Carlo Séquin turned into a program.

This is that program's geometry, running in your browser. Change the number of arms on each saddle, the number of storeys, how far the stack twists on its way round, and watch the sculpture reassemble.

Where the shape comes from

Dip a wire frame in soapy water and the film that forms pulls itself as tight as it can. The surfaces that result are called minimal, and they have a property worth holding on to: at every point the surface curves upward in one direction by exactly as much as it curves downward in the direction square to it. A saddle is the simplest thing that can do that.

In 1835 Heinrich Scherk found a minimal surface that repeats forever upwards — a tower of saddles, each one turned from the last. Written in coordinates it is sin z = sinh x · sinh y, and you can slice it at any height and get an exact formula for the cross-section, which is what this page draws. The tower has holes in it: the places where the surface, cut off at a finite width, has nothing left to join.

What the controls do

Arms per saddle is how many ways each saddle branches. Two gives the ordinary saddle; three gives the monkey-saddle, with a dip for the tail as well as for the legs; more gives a flower. Storeys stacks them. Flange width is how far out the surface is cut off, and it is the one with a threshold hiding in it: narrow the flange past about 0.78 and the holes break open, which changes what kind of surface the sculpture is rather than merely how it looks.

Warp bends the tower round a circle — 360° brings the ends together — and Twist turns the stack as it goes. Those two have to agree if the ends are to meet cleanly, and the line under the sculpture says whether they do. Séquin's condition is that the twist, plus the rotation the storeys make on their own, comes to a whole number of branch widths; everything else leaves a step where the ends butt together.

The sculpture you land on at the top of the page, the Hyperbolic Hexagon, is Brent Collins': six storeys of two-armed saddles, bent into a closed ring. Collins carved these by hand in wood before the program existed, and Séquin wrote the program to explore the family around them.

Printing one

The surface here has no thickness — it is a mathematical sheet, and a slicer would make nothing of it. Download STL therefore gives it a wall, offsetting the sheet to either side by half the thickness you ask for and closing the rim, then scales the result so its longest side is the size you ask for. The file is binary STL in millimetres.

Two honest warnings. A thin wall on a wide sculpture is fragile in anything but resin, and a high Detail makes a large file without making a better print past about 8. Also, the add-on builds its thickness differently, with rims shaped to the design; this page offsets the mid-surface instead. The shape is the same, the edges are not identical.
