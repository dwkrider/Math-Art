# Isohedral Tiling

![Isohedral Tiling](../images/isohedral.png)

## Overview

Add an isohedral (Escher-style) tiling.

A tiling is **isohedral** when its symmetry group acts transitively on the tiles: every tile is equivalent to every other under a symmetry of the pattern, so the whole plane is one tile repeated. Grünbaum and Shephard classified these into 81 types, IH1–IH81; this generator implements a curated, coverage-verified selection spanning the principal wallpaper symmetries.

The interesting control is **edge deformation**. A prototile's boundary edges come in matched pairs related by a group isometry, and if both edges of a pair carry the same profile, the deformed tile still tiles the plane with no gaps. That is precisely the mechanism behind M. C. Escher's interlocking lizards, birds and fish — the tile can be bent into almost any silhouette and will still interlock, because the deformation is applied to the symmetry, not to the shape.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Isohedral Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling.** The 13 entries are named by their **wallpaper group** — the kind of symmetry that repeats the tile: pure translation (*p1*), rotations (*p2*, *p4*, *p3*, *p6*), glides (*pg*, *pgg*), and the mirror-bearing groups (*cmm*, *p4g*, *p4m*, *p6m*, *p3m1*, *p31m*). The group decides both the tile's default polygon shape and how much of it you can deform.
3. **Drive the shape** with **Edge Shape** — the signature control. At 0 the tile is a straight-edged polygon; pushing it toward $\pm1$ bends the deformable edges into Escher-like interlocking curves, and the sign flips which way each bulge points.
4. **Mind the rigid types.** For the three pure kaleidoscopes — *p4m*, *p6m*, *p3m1* — every edge lies on a mirror line and cannot move, so the operator **hides Edge Shape** and shows an info note instead. This is a fact about those groups, not a fault: reach for another type if you want deformation.
5. **Set size and colour.** **Cells X** / **Cells Y** give the patch extent (1–30 each); **Color By** defaults to *By Tile Type*, colouring each tile by its **symmetry orbit** (which coset of the group produced it), with *By Sides* and *Uniform* also available.
6. **Shape the output.** **Margin** opens grout lines; **Relief Height** extrudes flat tiles into prisms above 0; **Trim Boundary** clips the patch to a clean rectangle; **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own object. The header line reports the tiling's vertex and face counts (or tile count when separated).

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Escher's famous prints of interlocking lizards start from an ordinary shape — a square, a triangle, a rhombus — and then bend its edges into a creature's outline. The magic is a bookkeeping rule: whatever bump you push *out* of one side of the tile, you must push *in* on the side that will butt against it, so the two always fit like jigsaw pieces. Follow that rule and the bent tile keeps paving the plane with no gaps, no matter how wild the outline becomes. The pattern's own symmetry — the way copies are rotated, slid or mirrored to repeat — is what tells you which edge is partnered with which, so it does the bookkeeping for you. This generator lets you dial that bending with a single slider.

**The prototile is a fundamental domain.** Each isohedral type begins from one polygon $P$ that is a *fundamental domain* of a wallpaper group $G$: applying every symmetry in the group to $P$ covers the plane exactly once, $\lbrace g(P) : g \in G\rbrace$, each point landing in precisely one image. Because $G$ acts transitively on those images — some group element carries any tile to any other — every tile is literally the same tile in a different pose, which is what "isohedral" asserts. In the code, $G$ is presented as a lattice of translations $\lbrace i\,b_1 + j\,b_2\rbrace$ times a short list of **cosets** (one isometry per point-group element), and the fundamental domain is the small polygon `proto`.

**Matched edges.** The boundary of $P$ splits into edges that come in partnered pairs. If the group element $g$ carries edge $e$ onto edge $e'$, then $e' = g(e)$, and $e,e'$ are the two sides of a single seam shared with one neighbour. The tiling therefore survives an arbitrary deformation of $e$ **as long as its partner receives the transported deformation**:

$$e \mapsto \varphi(e), \qquad e' \mapsto g\big(\varphi(e)\big).$$

Geometrically this is exact conservation: whatever area a bulge $\varphi$ adds on one side of a seam, the very same isometry $g$ removes from the neighbour across it, so adjacency and total area are untouched and no gap can open. That is the entire content of "Escherization" — the silhouette becomes as free as you like while the tiling property is preserved, because the constraint lives in the *group relation between edges*, not in the outline. The generator discovers the pairing automatically: for each edge it searches the neighbouring isometries for the unique one that shares that edge, distinguishing an orientation-preserving neighbour (a rotation or translation, where the shared edge is the *reversed* image) from an orientation-reversing one (a glide or reflection, where the flip of handedness makes the *forward* image align). A single wiggle profile — zero at both endpoints, so vertices never move — is then applied to one edge of each pair and transported to its partner.

**The rigid exception.** One kind of edge cannot move at all. An edge lying *on a mirror line* is fixed pointwise by that reflection, $g(e) = e$, so the matching condition collapses to $\varphi(e) = g(\varphi(e))$: the profile would have to equal its own mirror image about the very line it sits on, and the only curve symmetric about a line it lies on is the straight line itself. Consequently the pure kaleidoscope tilings — *p4m*, *p6m*, *p3m1*, whose fundamental triangle has all three sides on mirrors — are **rigid**: the shape control can move nothing, which is why the operator hides it for exactly those three. This is a theorem about the groups, not a gap in the implementation; knowing it saves you from reaching for a slider and concluding it is broken. Every other type here keeps at least one deformable edge pair, which is precisely why the lively Escher tiles come from the groups built on rotations and glides rather than from the most mirror-symmetric ones.

## References

- Branko Grunbaum & G. C. Shephard, "Tilings and Patterns" (W. H. Freeman, 1987) -- the classification of the 81 isohedral tiling types IH1..IH81.
- M. C. Escher -- the interlocking-figure ("Escherization") tradition of symmetry-matched edge deformation.
- Craig S. Kaplan & David H. Salesin, "Escherization" (SIGGRAPH 2000); Kaplan, "Introductory Tiling Theory for Computer Graphics" (2009) and the "Tactile" isohedral-tiling library -- algorithmic edge deformation.
