# Leaf

![Leaf](../images/leaf.png)

## Overview

A leaf as solid, solidifiable geometry — a simple blade or a compound leaf — where the blade is **grown, not drawn**: instead of fitting a curve to a leaf outline, the margin is advanced by a growth model and the silhouette is whatever that produces (cordate, elliptic, lanceolate, obovate, ovate, reniform, rose). A traced outline reproduces one leaf; a marginal-growth model explains why leaves fall into these families at all, and why nudging one growth rate carries a shape continuously into its neighbour.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Plants & Growth ▸ Leaf*.
2. **Pick the Mode.** **Simple** grows one blade; **Compound** grows a rachis (a central stalk) bearing many leaflets, each leaflet itself a small blade.
3. **Choose the Shape and growth.** **Shape** selects one of seven blade families (Cordate, Elliptic, Lanceolate, Obovate, Ovate, Reniform, Rose leaflet). **Growth Steps** is how many rounds of marginal growth to run — more steps refine the same silhouette rather than changing it — and **Outline Points** how finely the finished margin is sampled.
4. **Dial the mode-specific controls.** *Compound* mode adds leaflet grading: leave **Use Table Row** on to take the apical delay and elongation rate from a **Table 5.2 Row** preset, or turn it off to set **Apical Delay** (plastochrons between leaflet pairs) and **Elongation Rate** by hand; **Plastochrons**, **Leaflet Angle** and **Leaflet Size** finish the rachis. *Simple* mode instead offers **Venation** — turn it on to grow a vein network into the blade, choosing **Venation Type** (Open/dichotomous or Closed/reticulate) and tuning **Auxin Sources**, **Vein Step**, **Kill Radius**, **Influence Radius**, **Vein Radius** and **Seed**.
5. **Set the scale and read the output.** **Scale** fits the leaf to a 2 m cube. The blade is emitted as a closed, bilaterally symmetric, triangulated mesh ready for a Solidify modifier; when venation is on, the veins come as a *second* bevelled-curve object so they can carry their own material and be solidified independently. The operator reports the blade's vertex and face counts.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A leaf is not stamped out from a template — it grows outward from its rim, the way frost creeps across a cold window or dough swells over the edge of a tin. Along the leaf's margin sits a band of dividing cells; wherever that band grows fast the blade bulges outward, wherever it grows slowly the edge is left behind, and the final silhouette is simply the record of where growth was fast and where it was slow. Widen early and then stop and you get a rounded, broad-based leaf; keep growing hardest near the tip and you get a long, narrow one. So the seven shapes here are not seven drawings — they are seven settings of a handful of growth rates. The technical name for that growing rim is the *marginal meristem*, and everything below is the bookkeeping of how fast each part of it advances.

**Marginal growth, precisely.** Only one half of the margin is grown and then mirrored, because a leaf is bilaterally symmetric by construction and growing both halves independently would only introduce numerical asymmetry. The half-margin starts as a single segment from base to apex and is refined over `steps` plastochrons. At each step, every edge elongates by a rate interpolated between a **basal** rate at the base and an **apical** rate at the apex,

$$\text{rate}(s) = \text{basal} + (\text{apical}-\text{basal})\,s,$$

and a new vertex is inserted at each edge's midpoint, pushed outward along the edge normal by a lateral bulge $\propto (\text{width}-1)\,\lVert\text{edge}\rVert\,\beta(s)$. The profile $\beta(s)=\sin(\pi s)$ is a single hump — widest in the middle — plus an optional **lobe** term concentrated near the base, and it is exactly that basal term that carries an ovate leaf into a cordate and then a reniform one. Then the whole margin elongates along the leaf axis (apex faster or slower than base, per the same two rates) and widens laterally by the width rate. That last lateral widening of *existing* material is not cosmetic: each inserted bulge is proportional to an edge that halves every step, so the bulges alone form a geometric series that converges almost immediately while the length keeps multiplying — without re-widening what is already there, every blade would collapse into a sliver. Finally the apex is tapered by a power law ($\text{tip}<1$ blunt, $\text{tip}>1$ drawn out to a point) and the whole blade scaled to a per-species width/length **ratio**. Each named shape is therefore just one setting of $(\text{basal},\text{apical},\text{width},\text{lobe},\text{tip},\text{ratio})$. The model follows ABOP sections 5.2–5.3 and the SIGGRAPH 1988 leaf work and reproduces the *qualitative* Table 5.1 series; `lsystem/leaves.py` states precisely what is and is not claimed as a literal reconstruction of Tables 5.1 and 5.2, worth reading before treating a given shape as exact.

**Compound leaves.** A rachis bears leaflet pairs, but with a twist that ties the whole leaf together from two numbers. The apex emits a new leaflet pair only every $D+1$ plastochrons — $D$ is the **apical delay** — while every internode already on the rachis keeps elongating by the **rate** $R$ at every step. So a leaflet formed early has been elongating the longest and ends up furthest from the tip on the longest internode, and the smooth grading from big basal leaflets to small distal ones is a *consequence* of $(D,R)$, not a separate taper knob: a large delay with a low rate gives many similar leaflets, a short delay with a high rate a few sharply graded ones. Each leaflet is the same blade, triangulated once in its own local frame and then placed by rotating and translating its vertices — never by rotating the outline first, because the triangulation splits the margin into left and right halves *by index*, which is only meaningful while the blade still straddles $x=0$; rotate first and the halves pair across the wrong axis, producing a butterfly.

**Venation** follows Runions et al. (SIGGRAPH 2005) by *space colonization*: auxin sources are scattered across the blade, and at each step every source pulls the nearest vein node within its **influence radius** so that a vein tip advances one **vein step** toward the resultant of the sources tugging on it, and a source is removed once a vein comes within the **kill radius** of it. The pattern therefore emerges from competition for space rather than from a branching rule — and the kill radius is the real fineness control, because it is what stops veins crowding into one another. Two modes decide whether veins may reconnect: **Open (dichotomous)** lets each source attract only its single nearest vein, so veins never rejoin and the result is a tree (ginkgo, conifers, most monocots); **Closed (reticulate)** lets each source attract every vein in its relative neighbourhood, so veins meet and close loops — the areoles of a dicot leaf. Vein thickness follows a pipe model, each vein as thick as its branches combined and tapering toward the tips.

**Triangulating a narrow blade.** One implementation detail is a real geometric decision rather than a coding one: the outline is symmetric about $x=0$, so it splits into a left and a right margin with equal point counts, and the mesh is built by pairing those margins across the midrib and quadding between consecutive pairs. This keeps every face well conditioned — unlike a fan about the centroid, which on a long narrow shape degenerates into sliver triangles at both tips, faces that are badly conditioned and visibly wrong the moment a Solidify modifier goes on top. At the two ends, where the left and right margins actually meet, the end band's quad has two coincident corners and is collapsed to a triangle, so no zero-area face — which is broken geometry, not a small face — survives into the mesh.
