# Leaf

## Overview

Add a leaf: a blade grown by marginal growth, simple or
compound, as a solidifiable mesh.

Simple blades and compound leaves as solid geometry. The blade is **grown rather than drawn**: instead of fitting a curve to a leaf outline, the margin is advanced by a growth model and the silhouette is whatever that produces — cordate, elliptic, lanceolate, obovate, ovate, reniform, rose.

The distinction matters because it is what connects shape to cause. A traced outline reproduces one leaf; a marginal-growth model explains why leaves fall into these families at all, and why varying one growth parameter carries a shape continuously into its neighbour.

## How it works

**Marginal growth.** The blade is generated by advancing its margin: points along the leaf edge move outward at rates that vary along the margin and decay with age. Where the rate is high the blade widens, where it is low the margin is left behind, and the accumulated history of those rates *is* the outline. Widening early and stopping gives an obovate leaf; growing longest at the base gives a cordate one.

The model follows ABOP sections 5.2–5.3 and the SIGGRAPH 1988 leaf work; `lsystem/leaves.py` states precisely what is and is not claimed to reproduce ABOP Tables 5.1 and 5.2, which is worth reading before treating a given shape as a literal reconstruction.

**Venation** follows Runions et al. (SIGGRAPH 2005): veins grow toward auxin sources scattered in the blade, each source captured and removed as a vein tip reaches it, so the branching pattern emerges from competition for space rather than from a branching rule. Open and closed modes decide whether veins may reconnect into loops — the difference between a dicot's reticulate venation and a simple dichotomous pattern.

**Compound leaves** repeat the blade as leaflets along a rachis, with the same growth model applied at both levels.

**One implementation detail worth stating**, because it is a real geometric decision rather than a coding one: the blade is fan-triangulated **about the midrib**, not about the centroid. A leaf is long and narrow, and a centroid fan on a narrow shape produces sliver triangles at both ends — degenerate, badly conditioned, and visibly wrong the moment a Solidify modifier goes on top. Fanning along the midrib keeps every triangle well shaped, which is what makes the output usable as solid geometry rather than only as a picture.
