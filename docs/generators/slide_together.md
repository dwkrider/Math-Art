# Slide-Together

![Slide-Together](../images/slide_together.png)

## Overview

Add one of Hart's slide-together models: identical slotted
polygons that interlock without glue.

A **slide-together** is one of **George Hart's** paper models (his "Slide-Togethers" and *Modular Kirigami*, Bridges 2007): many identical copies of a single regular polygon, each slit at fixed positions, that slide into one another and hold with no glue or fasteners. Every panel lies in a face plane of a symmetric solid, and where two panels cross they are cut with a pair of complementary slots so they pass halfway through each other and lock.

Hart described seven — 20 triangles, 30 squares, 12 pentagons, 12 decagons, 20 hexagons, 12 star decagons {10/3} and 12 pentagrams. This operator adds those seven and more found by sweeping the uniform polyhedra for the same behaviour, including the first *octahedral* models (six octagons or squares on the cube's axes), which are by far the easiest to build by hand.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Slide-Together*.
2. **Pick the Model.** The list opens on Hart's seven and continues with the extra placements found on uniform star polyhedra (great ditrigonal, icositruncated, and so on) and the octahedral models drawn from a solid's edges rather than its faces. Each entry names the polygon and the solid whose planes carry it.
3. **Set the fit with Panel Size and Turn.** **Panel Size** scales every panel about its own centre — larger panels overlap more deeply, which is what makes the model hold together; the built-in default is the largest size at which every panel still crosses the same number of neighbours. **Turn** rotates every panel in its own plane away from the angle it is built at.
4. **Cut and thicken.** **Slit Width** sets the width of the cut slots (0 leaves the panels uncut, showing the bare arrangement). **Thickness** gives the panels real depth via a Solidify modifier (0 leaves them flat faces). These controls are always shown — the model has no mode-specific gating.
5. **Choose the output.** **Colours** paints the panels in rotation, the way the paper models are made; **Separate Panels** emits one object per panel instead of a single mesh; **Scale** sizes the whole model.
6. **Read the report.** The operator reports the panel count (`N panels`) — 20, 30 or 12 depending on the model — so you can confirm the full symmetric set was built.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Think of joining two stiff cards without glue: cut a slot halfway into each from opposite edges, then slide one card's slot into the other's — they wedge together at the crossing, the way the cardboard dividers in a wine box or an egg carton grip each other. A slide-together does this many times over. One regular polygon is copied into every "face plane" of a symmetric solid — the twenty faces of an icosahedron, say — with every copy tilted the same way, and wherever two copies cross they are slotted into one another. Because each panel is trapped by all its neighbours at once, the whole assembly holds itself together with nothing but the slots. Everything below works out *where* two panels cross and *how deep* to cut each slit so the pieces come to rest halfway.

**Placement.** Each model puts one regular $\lbrace n/d\rbrace$ polygon in every plane of a symmetric family — the 20 face planes of the icosahedron, the 12 of the dodecahedron, the 30 two-fold planes an icosidodecahedron's edges pick out, or (for the newer models) the face and edge-cycle planes of a uniform star polyhedron. A panel sits *in* its plane, offset from the centre by $\hat n\,h$ along the plane's unit normal $\hat n$ at signed distance $h$ — **not** through the origin. That offset is the whole point: panels passing through the centre would all cross at that single point, and the number of crossings would not depend on how big the panels are.

**Where two panels cross.** Two panels with unit normals $n_i, n_j$ lie in planes that meet along a line whose direction is

$$ \hat d = \frac{n_i \times n_j}{\lVert n_i \times n_j\rVert}. $$

A point on that line, with $h_i = n_i\cdot p_i$ and $h_j = n_j\cdot p_j$ the two plane offsets and $d$ the *raw* (un-normalised) cross product, is

$$ o = \frac{(n_j \times d)\,h_i + (d \times n_i)\,h_j}{d\cdot d}. $$

The raw $d$ matters — the numerator is linear and the denominator quadratic in it, so feeding the unit vector would push the point off both planes by a factor of $\sin(\text{angle between them})$. Clipping this line against each polygon gives the stretch of it inside that panel; where the two stretches overlap is the **chord** along which the panels cross.

**The complementary slits.** Along that chord the joint is a pair of slits: each panel is cut from its own rim inward to the *midpoint* of the chord. Cutting the two panels from **opposite** ends is exactly what lets them pass through each other and stop halfway; cut both from the same end and the slits would overlap and the joint would fall apart. Each slit is spliced into the boundary walk as a thin rectangle whose width is **Slit Width**.

**Star panels.** A star polygon $\lbrace n/d\rbrace$ walked corner to corner is a self-crossing path, which is not a valid face, so the physical panel is its *outline*: a simple $2n$-gon alternating the outer radius $R$ with an inner radius where consecutive chords meet,

$$ \frac{r}{R} = \frac{\cos(\pi d/n)}{\cos\!\big(\pi (d-1)/n\big)}, $$

which is $1/\varphi^{2}$ for the pentagram, as it must be. A chord of a star panel enters and leaves the outline several times — once per arm — so each arm gets its own pair of slits; Hart's pentagram template accordingly shows *ten* slits per panel, not five.

**The defining invariant.** Every panel of a model must be slit *identically* — the same number of slots at the same radii — because the model is many copies of one physical template, and the symmetry that carries one panel onto another has to carry its slits along too. This is both the design constraint and the sharpest test of the code: it fails the instant a step stops being equivariant, so the in-plane orientation of each panel is read from the solid itself rather than invented from a fixed global axis. It also fixes the default **Panel Size** — the largest size at which every panel crosses the same number of neighbours; grow past it and the outer panels saw through neighbours they should miss, shrink below it and they float free.
