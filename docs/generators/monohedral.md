# Monohedral Tiling

![Monohedral Tiling](../images/monohedral.png)

## Overview

Add a monohedral tiling (single irregular prototile).

A **monohedral** tiling uses a single prototile — one shape, repeated — but drops the requirement that it be regular. That small relaxation opens a surprisingly deep problem. *Every* triangle tiles the plane, and so does *every* quadrilateral, convex or not. But only **3** convex hexagons tile, and exactly **15** convex pentagons.

The pentagon count is the interesting one. Reinhardt found the first 5 in 1918, Kershner added more in 1968, the amateur mathematician Marjorie Rice discovered several in the 1970s working in her kitchen with a notation of her own devising, and the list was only proved complete by Michael Rao's exhaustive computer search in 2017 — ninety-nine years after it was started.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Monohedral Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling.** The menu offers a representative of each principal family: *Triangle* and *Quadrilateral* (which tile without exception), the three tiling hexagon types as *Hexagon Type 1* and *Type 2*, and the classic pentagons *Pentagon Type 1*, *Type 2* and *Type 5*. Each entry builds one concrete member of its family, laid down by that type's known symmetry cell.
3. **Set the patch size** with **Cells X** and **Cells Y** — copies of the prototile's unit cell along each lattice direction (1–40 each).
4. **Choose the colouring** with **Color By** — here it defaults to *By Tile Type*, which paints each tile by its **orientation orbit** (which rotation, reflection or glide of the single prototile it is). With one shape in one colour a monohedral tiling can be almost unreadable, so orbit colouring is what exposes the pattern; *By Sides* and *Uniform* are also available.
5. **Shape the output.** **Margin** opens grout lines by insetting each tile; **Relief Height** extrudes flat tiles into prisms above 0; **Trim Boundary** clips the patch to a clean rectangle; **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own object under an empty.
6. **Read the report.** The header prints the tiling's name with its vertex and face counts (or tile count when separated), confirming the patch built at the requested size.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Cut one shape out of cardboard and ask whether copies of it — flipped and turned every which way — can pave a floor with no gaps and no overlaps. For triangles and four-sided shapes the answer is always yes, and there is a lovely reason: two copies of any triangle snap together into a parallelogram, and parallelograms obviously tile. Pentagons are the cruel case. Their five corners add up to an amount that does *not* divide evenly into the full turn a floor-corner needs, so the copies never fall into place automatically — they only work for pentagons with just the right angles and side lengths. Hunting down *every* such special pentagon took mathematicians almost exactly a century.

**Why every triangle and every quadrilateral works.** Take any triangle $T$ and rotate a copy by $180^\circ$ about the midpoint of one of its edges. The original and the copy fit together with no gap into a parallelogram — and parallelograms tile the plane by pure translation, so every triangle tiles. The half-turn is the whole idea, and it extends further than it first appears. For *any* quadrilateral $Q$, half-turns about the midpoints of its four edges surround it with copies, and the four interior angles — which for a simple quadrilateral sum to a full turn —

$$\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta = 2\pi ,$$

each arrive at the shared vertex exactly once, filling it perfectly. Nothing in that argument uses convexity, so it survives even a non-convex (dart) quadrilateral, which is why the quadrilateral case has no exceptions to catalogue: the tile plus one half-turn is a centrally symmetric hexagonal *dimer*, and that dimer tiles by translation. (The generator deliberately ships a dart-shaped quad, to make the point that the construction never needed the shape to be convex.)

**Why pentagons are hard.** A convex pentagon's interior angles sum to $3\pi$. Unlike the quadrilateral's $2\pi$, this does not divide the full turn at a vertex, so no single arrangement of pentagons is *forced* to close up — the angles have to be arranged deliberately, several pentagons contributing different corners to each vertex. Each of the 15 tiling types is therefore pinned down by its own private system of angle-and-edge equations. Type 1 asks only that two adjacent angles sum to $\pi$ (so a pair of sides runs parallel); later types demand interlocking conditions such as

$$2A + B = 2\pi,\qquad 2C + D = 2\pi,\qquad a = b = e,$$

binding angles and edge lengths together at once. Because every type solves a *different* system, there is no one mechanism to reason about — only a vast finite search over how the constraints can combine — and that is exactly why completeness of the list of 15 resisted proof from 1918 until Rao's computer search in 2017.

**The unit cell.** Whatever the family, the storage is uniform: two lattice vectors $b_1,b_2$ and a list of tile copies — each a specific rotation, reflection or glide of the one prototile — that together fill a single cell. The patch is that cell stamped along the lattice,

$$P=\bigcup_{i,j} T(i\,b_1+j\,b_2)\,(\text{cell copies}),$$

over an $n_x\times n_y$ run. Each copy carries a **type index** recording which orientation orbit it belongs to. Because these tilings are frequently *not* edge-to-edge (a pentagon's vertex may land partway along a neighbour's edge), the usual angle-sum-at-a-vertex check is meaningless here, so each cell is certified instead by dense **coverage sampling** — a grid of points, each offset by a small irrational amount, that must every one lie in exactly one tile. The type index also feeds the *By Tile Type* colour mode: it paints the prototile's dance of orientations across the plane, and since a single shape in a single colour can be nearly impossible to read, colouring by orbit is what makes the underlying symmetry legible.

## References

- Karl Reinhardt, "Uber die Zerlegung der Ebene in Polygone" (doctoral dissertation, 1918) -- the 3 convex hexagon types and first 5 convex pentagon types that tile the plane.
- Richard B. Kershner, "On paving the plane" (1968) -- further pentagon types.
- Marjorie Rice -- amateur discovery of additional pentagon types (1970s).
- Michael Rao, "Exhaustive search of convex pentagons which tile the plane" (2017) -- proof that exactly 15 convex pentagon types exist.
