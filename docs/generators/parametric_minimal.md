# Classic Minimal Surface

![Classic Minimal Surface](../images/parametric_minimal.png)

## Overview

This generator produces the classic gallery of parametric minimal surfaces — Enneper (of any order), the catenoid, helicoid, Henneberg, Catalan, Bour, Richmond, Scherk's doubly-periodic graph, plus the Weierstrass-representation surfaces Costa (genus 1) and Chen-Gackstatter, the Jorge-Meeks $k$-noid, and the catenoid–helicoid associate family. Each is meshed directly from its closed-form parametrization and fit to a 2 m cube, and can be emitted as a dense polygon mesh or as a single compact NURBS patch with correct periodic closure.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Minimal Surfaces*.
2. **Pick a Family, then a Surface.** The **Family** dropdown (Weber's taxonomy) — *Classical*, *Spheres (genus 0)*, *Tori (genus 1)*, *Higher Genus*, *Bjorling (curve-seeded)*, *Non-Orientable* — filters the **Surface** list beneath it, so you first choose the *kind* of minimal surface and then the specific member. The Variants gallery below is grouped the same way.
3. **Set the one shape knob that applies.** The panel shows only the control the chosen surface actually uses: **Order / Count** is the Enneper order, the helicoid's half-turn count, or the Jorge-Meeks end count $n\ (\ge 3)$; **Storeys** stacks fundamental domains of a saddle tower; **Associate Angle** runs the Bonnet family ($0$ = catenoid, $\pi/2$ = helicoid) or, for the Karcher saddle tower, the wing-clustering angle. **Domain Radius** sets how far the parameter disk reaches — for a $k$-noid, how close it creeps to the ends.
4. **Choose the output.** **Mesh** gives a dense polygon mesh at **Resolution U × V**; **NURBS** gives a single compact patch at **Control Points U × V** with the surface's true periodic seam closed correctly. NURBS is offered only for surfaces that have a single-patch form (assembled multi-piece surfaces stay mesh-only).
5. **Size it.** **Scale** multiplies the normalized size, where $1.0$ fits the surface to a 2 m cube centred on the origin.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A **minimal surface** is the shape a soap film pulls itself into on a bent wire loop: at every point it is as *un-bulged* as it can be, curving up in one direction exactly as much as it curves down in the crossing direction, so the two cancel and the film has zero **mean curvature** on average. That balance is why films look so tense and smooth. Remarkably, there is a machine — the **Weierstrass–Enneper representation** — that manufactures such a film from two complex-number "ingredients," and almost every famous minimal surface is just one clever choice of those ingredients. This generator is a gallery of those choices: Enneper's self-crossing flower, the catenoid (the film between two rings), the helicoid (the film of a spiral ramp), Costa's celebrated surface, and more. Each is evaluated straight from its formula onto a grid and turned into a mesh.

Concretely, each surface is realized by evaluating its standard parametrization on an $(n_u, n_v)$ grid, then centering and scaling the result so its largest extent is 2 units. The unifying formula behind the whole catalog is the **Weierstrass–Enneper representation**: from a *Gauss map* $g$ (essentially where each point's normal aims, seen on the sphere via stereographic projection) and a *height differential* $dh = h(z)\,dz$, the surface is

$$X = \operatorname{Re}\!\left[e^{i\theta}\!\int (\phi_1, \phi_2, \phi_3)\,dz\right],\qquad
\phi_1 = \tfrac12\!\left(\tfrac1g - g\right)h,\quad
\phi_2 = \tfrac{i}{2}\!\left(\tfrac1g + g\right)h,\quad
\phi_3 = h.$$

The point of writing it this way is that *any* holomorphic pair $(g, dh)$ yields a genuinely minimal surface — the analyticity does the work of enforcing zero mean curvature automatically, so building new minimal surfaces reduces to choosing $g$ and $dh$. Taking the real part of a complex integral is what turns the two flat ingredients into three real coordinates, and the phase factor $e^{i\theta}$ rotates through the **Bonnet associate family**: it bends the film without stretching it, carrying each surface through a continuum of isometric minimal surfaces.

**Elementary surfaces** are sampled from their explicit maps (the closed-form result of doing that integral). For example the **Enneper surface** of order $n$ uses the complex chart $z = u\,e^{iv}$ with

$$x = \operatorname{Re}\!\Big(z - \tfrac{z^{2n+1}}{2n+1}\Big),\quad
y = -\operatorname{Im}\!\Big(z + \tfrac{z^{2n+1}}{2n+1}\Big),\quad
w = \tfrac{2}{n+1}\operatorname{Re}\!\big(z^{n+1}\big),$$

so raising the order $n$ adds more petals to the same self-intersecting flower. The **catenoid** is $x = \cosh v \cos u,\; y = \cosh v \sin u,\; z = v$ — the profile of a hanging chain (a catenary) spun around an axis, the only minimal surface of revolution — and the **helicoid** is $x = v\cos u,\; y = v\sin u,\; z \propto u$, the ramp swept by a line screwing up an axis. Scherk's doubly-periodic surface is the graph $w = \ln(\cos u / \cos v)$, defined only where $\cos u$ and $\cos v$ share a sign, which is what carves it into its checkerboard of tunnels. The **Catenoid–Helicoid associate** makes the family link visible: the angle $\theta$ gives the catenoid at $\theta = 0$, the helicoid at $\theta = \pi/2$, and at every angle between a complete minimal surface isometric to both — you can literally watch one bend into the other without tearing.

**Weierstrass-representation surfaces** (Costa, Chen-Gackstatter) need a richer domain than a disk: they live on the square (lemniscatic) **torus** with half-periods $\omega_1 = \tfrac12,\ \omega_3 = \tfrac{i}{2}$, $\tau = i$. The reason is topological — these surfaces have genus, i.e. a handle, and a handle is a torus, so the parameter domain must itself be a torus. The module includes a small Weierstrass engine computing the elliptic functions $\wp$, $\wp'$ and $\zeta$ from the Jacobi theta functions ($\theta_1$ $q$-series, nome $q = e^{i\pi\tau}$; about a dozen terms reach $10^{-15}$); these are the natural "coordinates" on a torus, the way $\sin$ and $\cos$ are on a circle. Costa's surface (genus 1, three ends) uses the Gray/Nylander closed form built from $\zeta(z)$, $\zeta(z-\tfrac12)$, $\zeta(z-\tfrac{i}{2})$ and $\ln\big|(\wp - e_1)/(\wp + e_1)\big|$ — the $\zeta$ terms place a catenoidal and two planar ends at the torus's special points — while Chen-Gackstatter (genus 1, a single order-3 Enneper end, total curvature $-8\pi$) is built from $\wp$, $\wp'$, $\zeta$ with $g_2 = 4e_1^2$. Both are meshed **periodically** on the torus with small disks punctured out around the ends, so the only mesh boundaries are clean circular end rims rather than a torn edge.

The **Jorge-Meeks $k$-noid** (genus 0 with $n\ge 3$ catenoid ends) has no closed form, so it is integrated numerically. With Gauss map $g = z^{n-1}$ and height differential $dh = z^{n-1}/(z^n-1)^2\,dz$ — whose double poles at the $n$-th roots of unity are exactly the $n$ catenoid ends — the representation integral is evaluated **radially outward** from the pole-free base point $z = 0$, spanning both sides of $|z| = 1$, with disks around the ends removed. Integrating along rays from a safe centre is what keeps the numerics away from those poles.

Surfaces whose ends run to infinity are **trimmed** so they fit in view: a parameter-space validity mask gives clean circular rims where the geometry allows, and otherwise faces past a distance percentile (together with faces bridging an excluded end) are dropped, and the resulting staircase rim is smoothed by a boundary-only Laplacian pass — a cosmetic relaxation that leaves the interior minimal surface untouched. The body is then fit to the 2 m cube using an *inlier* reference, so a single runaway end cannot shrink the whole surface to a speck. **NURBS output** drops the duplicated periodic endpoint and builds a single patch with cyclic-U/V flags matching the surface's true periodicity, so the seam closes as a true wrap rather than a coincidental overlap.

## References

- Weierstrass-Enneper representation: K. Weierstrass (1866) and A. Enneper (1864); the catenoid (surface of Euler, 1744) was shown minimal by J. B. C. Meusnier (1776).
- Costa surface: C. J. Costa (1982); embeddedness by D. Hoffman and W. H. Meeks III (1985). Chen-Gackstatter: C. C. Chen and F. Gackstatter (1982). Jorge-Meeks k-noids: L. P. Jorge and W. H. Meeks III (1983).
- Triply-periodic families: H. A. Schwarz (P, D; Gesammelte Math. Abhandlungen, 1890), A. H. Schoen (gyroid, I-WP, F-RD; NASA TN D-5541, 1970), E. R. Neovius (1883). Cotangent-Laplacian area flow after U. Pinkall and K. Polthier (1993).
- Seifert surfaces: H. Seifert, "Ueber das Geschlecht von Knoten", Mathematische Annalen 110 (1934). Visualization of Seifert surfaces as disks joined by twisted bands after J. J. van Wijk and A. M. Cohen, IEEE TVCG 12(4) (2006).
