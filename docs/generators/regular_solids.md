# Regular Solids

![Regular Solids](../images/regular_solids.png)

## Overview

A complete *Add Regular Solid*, organised by family: the five **Platonic** solids, the four **Kepler–Poinsot** regular star polyhedra (built as their true intersecting faces), all thirteen **Archimedean** semiregular solids and all thirteen **Catalan** duals, uniform **prisms and antiprisms**, all ninety-two **Johnson** solids (J1–J92), and a set of **Conway-derived families** — Archimedean–Catalan hulls, propellor solids, truncated / rectified Archimedean, chamfered solids, dipyramids & trapezohedra, and truncated Catalan. On top of the shape sit generic stellation, six styles, coloring by face size, a handedness switch for the chiral solids, and a "congruent pieces" splitter that carves the shell into rotated identical parts for printing and reassembly.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Regular Solid*.
2. **Pick the Family, then the Solid.** Family sets the catalog; Solid lists its members — the five Platonic, the thirteen Archimedean and thirteen Catalan duals (by name), the four Kepler–Poinsot stars, prisms/antiprisms, the Johnson solids J1–J92, or one of the Conway-derived families (hulls, propellor, truncated / rectified Archimedean, chamfered, dipyramids & trapezohedra, truncated Catalan). For **Prisms & Antiprisms** a **Sides** slider sets the base $n$-gon.
3. **Leave Canonicalize on** for the Conway-derived families (Archimedean, Catalan, hulls, propellor, truncated / rectified Archimedean, chamfered, dipyramids & trapezohedra): it reshapes the solid to its canonical form — all edges tangent to one common sphere, every face exactly planar. It greys out, and the geometry is left untouched, for the families that are already exact (Platonic, Kepler, prisms, Johnson). **Canonical Iterations** sets how long that refinement runs.
4. **For a chiral solid** (the snubs and the chiral Catalans) **Handedness** picks which of the two mirror forms to build; it appears only when the current solid actually has two enantiomorphs.
5. **Turn on Stellated** to replace each face with a pyramid rising to where its edge-neighbours' face-planes meet (octahedron → stella octangula, dodecahedron → small stellated dodecahedron). The toggle greys out whenever those planes cannot meet in a point (cube, prisms, flat-capped elongated solids).
6. **Choose a Style** — Solid, Leonardo (open face-frames, with **Border**/**Thickness**), Struts, Ball and Stick (**Strut Radius**/**Node Radius**), Wireframe, or Face Segments (one thick plate per face, with **Depth**, **Bevel Gap**, **Explode** and **Separate Meshes**) — and a **Coloring** (by face size, or none).
7. **Split into Congruent Pieces** to carve the shell into $N$ identical, connected, rotated parts, each its own object for print-and-reassemble, with **Explode** pushing them apart so the cut shows.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A *polyhedron* is a solid with flat faces — a die, a soccer ball, a crystal, a pyramid. This one generator knows a whole museum of them, sorted into families: the five perfectly regular *Platonic* solids everyone meets first; the *Archimedean* solids, which mix two or three kinds of regular face (a soccer ball is one); their *Catalan* partners, made by trading every face for a corner and every corner for a face; the spiky *Kepler–Poinsot* stars; the *Johnson* solids, the complete grab-bag of everything else you can close up out of flat regular faces; and plain *prisms* and *antiprisms*, the drum-shaped ones. There is no single formula that spits them all out, so the generator keeps a different recipe for each family — and finishes several of them with a common "rounding" step that nudges the shape until it sits perfectly on a sphere. The rest of this section is one paragraph per recipe.

**Platonic, Archimedean, Catalan and the Conway-derived families.** These are all produced through **Conway operator notation** (see the Conway Operators generator), a little algebra of shape verbs applied to a seed. Each solid carries a notation string — the cuboctahedron is `aC` (**a**mbo cube), the truncated icosahedron `tI` (**t**runcate icosahedron), the rhombic triacontahedron `daD` (dual of ambo dodecahedron) — and the same alphabet, extended with `j` (join → hulls), `p` (propellor), `c` (chamfer) and duals of prisms, builds the extra families. Applying an operator gives the right *combinatorics* — the correct faces meeting the correct way — but not yet the right *coordinates*; the raw output is lopsided. George Hart's **canonicalization** fixes that by iterating two corrections until they stop changing anything:

- every edge's midpoint is pushed toward tangency with the unit sphere, and the whole solid is recentred on the average edge-tangent point — this equalises the edges and inflates the shape to its roundest position;
- every face is planarized by projecting its vertices onto their best-fit plane — this removes the buckling the first step introduces.

The two corrections pull against each other, and their fixed point is the **canonical form**: every edge tangent to one common sphere, every face exactly flat. That is the geometry that makes an Archimedean solid *look* Archimedean, and it is reached numerically rather than from a table of coordinates. Vertex/face counts are verified for all 26 Archimedean/Catalan solids (snub cube 24/38, truncated icosahedron 60/32, snub dodecahedron 60/92, …). The exact families below never go through this step — they are already correct — so canonicalization is scoped to the Conway-derived families and greyed out for the others.

**Prisms and antiprisms** need no relaxation because their coordinates are known in closed form. A regular $n$-gon has circumradius $r_n = \tfrac{1}{2\sin(\pi/n)}$ — the radius at which its edges come out unit length. For a **prism** that ring is copied to $z = \pm\tfrac12$, giving unit vertical edges as well. For an **antiprism** the top ring is rotated by half a step so the sides close up as triangles, and the two rings are set at height $h = \sqrt{1 - \big(2 r_n \sin\tfrac{\pi}{2n}\big)^2}$ — exactly the separation that makes those slant edges unit length too. The square-root form is just the Pythagorean leftover after the half-step twist eats part of the edge.

**Johnson solids J1–J92** are the complete set of convex solids whose faces are *all* regular but which are none of the above — not Platonic, Archimedean, or a prism/antiprism. There are exactly ninety-two (Johnson listed them in 1966; Zalgaller proved the list complete in 1969), and they are built here in three groups by how their geometry is pinned down:

- **J1–J48** are composed from exact unit-edge building blocks — pyramids, cupolas, and the pentagonal rotunda (sliced from a canonical icosidodecahedron) — stacked on a shared ring with optional prism or antiprism middles. A pyramid apex sits at height $\sqrt{1 - r_n^2}$ above its ring (again the Pythagorean leftover of a unit slant edge); a cupola rises by the height at which *its* slant edges are unit; an ortho vs. gyro pairing differs only by a whole-step twist of the bottom cap, which is why the two versions of a bicupola are genuinely different solids.
- **J49–J83** are the **augmented, diminished and gyrate** forms: a cap glued **onto** an exact convex base (the augmented prisms J49–J57 and augmented dodecahedron J58–J61), sliced **off** it (the diminished icosahedra J62–J64), or twisted in place — the whole gyrate/diminished rhombicosidodecahedron family J72–J83, made by removing and re-seating pentagonal cupola caps on a canonical rhombicosidodecahedron, plus the cupola-augmented truncated solids J65–J71.
- **J84–J92** are the nine **elementary** solids (snub disphenoid, sphenocorona, hebesphenomegacorona, …) that fit no cap-and-base scheme at all. Their coordinates have no tidy closed form, so an approximate model is **relaxed** until every edge is unit length — regular-face relaxation, the geometric analogue of letting a spring network settle.

All ninety-two are verified to machine precision — every edge unit length, Euler characteristic $\chi = V - E + F = 2$ — and the ortho/gyro pairings are confirmed by like-meets-like contact tests at the girdle.

**Kepler–Poinsot** star polyhedra are built as their *true self-intersecting faces*, not as convex approximations — the point of them is precisely that the faces pass through one another. The great dodecahedron $\lbrace5, \tfrac52\rbrace$ and great icosahedron $\lbrace3, \tfrac52\rbrace$ take their faces directly from the neighbour rings of an icosahedron. The small $\lbrace5/2, 5\rbrace$ and great $\lbrace5/2, 3\rbrace$ stellated dodecahedra are made of genuine pentagrams: each pentagram $\lbrace5/2\rbrace$ is star-triangulated by finding the five inner crossing points where the extended chords $i \to i{+}2$ meet, then filling the central pentagon and the five points as triangles — so the star renders as the outline it should, not as a filled pentagon. For the great stellated dodecahedron the five pentagram points are the *second* ring of each dodecahedron face (the vertices one step further out), so the face planes cut deep and interpenetrate into the spiky star.

**Generic stellation** (the Stellated option) extends each face outward until it meets its neighbours, replacing the face with a pyramid whose apex is the least-squares intersection of the planes of that face's edge-neighbours. For each neighbour plane $(\mathbf n_g, d_g)$ the apex $\mathbf a$ solves

$$\Big(\textstyle\sum_g \mathbf n_g \mathbf n_g^{\!\top}\Big)\,\mathbf a = \sum_g d_g\, \mathbf n_g,$$

the normal equations for "the point closest to lying on every neighbour plane at once", solved by Cramer's rule. When the neighbour planes are all parallel to a common axis (cube, prisms, flat-capped elongated Johnson solids) they never converge to a point, the $3\times3$ system is rank-deficient, and the option is disabled rather than allowed to blow up. The octahedron yields the stella octangula, the dodecahedron the small stellated dodecahedron, the icosahedron the small triambic icosahedron.

**Congruent Pieces.** To split a solid into identical rotated parts, the generator first recovers the solid's own **rotation group** *from the mesh*: it tries to align face 0's local frame (its normal plus an in-plane axis) with every same-size face at every cyclic offset, and keeps a candidate rotation only if it carries the *whole* set of face centroids onto itself — so the symmetry is discovered, not assumed. A **free subgroup** of order $N$ — one whose non-identity rotations fix no face — then guarantees $N$ pieces that are true rotated copies of one another. One piece is grown as a compact, connected fundamental domain by greedily annexing the adjacent unassigned face nearest the running centroid; the other pieces are its group translates. A final hill-climbing pass de-jags the partition by re-choosing which group copy of each face-orbit lands in which piece, minimizing cut perimeter plus a hinge-face penalty while keeping every piece edge-connected — the difference between a clean interlocking split and a ragged one. Icosahedron → 2/4/5/10 pieces, cube → 2/3/6, rhombic triacontahedron → 3/5, and so on. Because the domain search is not mirror-symmetric, the left-handed form of a chiral solid takes exactly the mirrored right-handed split.
