# Calabi-Yau Cross-Section

![Calabi-Yau Cross-Section](../images/calabi_yau.png)

## Overview

This is the object nearly every "Calabi-Yau manifold" picture actually shows. A
Calabi-Yau threefold has six real dimensions and cannot sit in ordinary space;
what gets drawn is a two-dimensional *cross-section* of one, projected from four
dimensions down to three. This generator builds Andrew Hanson's classic
parametrisation of that slice — a surface stitched together from $n \times n$
curved patches — and can also emit its boundary, which is a torus knot or link.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Calabi-Yau Cross-Section.
2. **Pick the Family.** *Fermat* builds the symmetric slice
   $z_1^{n} + z_2^{n} = 1$ from a single **Degree** $n$ (the famous quintic is
   $n = 5$). *Brieskorn* instead takes two independent exponents, **First Power**
   $p$ and **Second Power** $q$, for $z_1^{p} + z_2^{q} = 1$ — a more general
   shape whose boundary is the $(p, q)$ torus link.
3. **Choose the shadow.** The surface really lives in 4-D, so **Projection
   Angle** selects which 3-D shadow you see; turning it slides the
   self-intersections around, which is worth knowing because those crossings
   belong to the *projection*, not to the surface.
4. **Explore the family.** **Phase** walks along
   $z_1^{p} + z_2^{q} = e^{2\pi i\,\varphi}$, rotating the patches into one
   another and returning to the start at $\varphi = 1$. **Extent** sets how far
   the barbs run out toward the curve's points at infinity (the surface is
   unbounded; this is simply where it is cut off).
5. **Colour it** by *Patch*, *Symmetry*, or the discarded *Fourth Dimension* —
   the last paints on the one coordinate the 3-D projection threw away.
6. **Output.** Leave it a surface, or turn on **Boundary Link** to also emit the
   $(p, q)$ torus link as a bevelled curve, and **Thickness** to solidify the
   sheet for printing.

## How it works

Imagine trying to photograph a shape that lives in six dimensions. You can't —
so instead you take a thin two-dimensional slice of it, and even that slice
lives in four dimensions, so you photograph its *shadow* in three. That shadow,
tidied up and coloured, is the swirling patched surface everyone recognises as
"a Calabi-Yau." The trick that makes it drawable is an old one for spheres,
pushed into the complex numbers: an ordinary circle is all the points with
$\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, and Hanson found a complex version of $\cos$ and $\sin$
that traces the Calabi-Yau slice the same way. The surface comes out in a tidy
grid of $n \times n$ curved tiles, and its ragged edge, followed around, is a
torus knot.

**The slice.** The quintic threefold is the set of solutions to

$$z_0^{5} + z_1^{5} + z_2^{5} + z_3^{5} + z_4^{5} = 0 \quad \text{in } \mathbb{CP}^4.$$

Dehomogenising and holding two of the coordinates fixed collapses this six-real-
dimensional object to the complex curve $z_1^{5} + z_2^{5} = 1$ in
$\mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{R}^4$ — a genuine two-real-dimensional surface, the thing
we can actually build. The generator does this for any degree $n$ (Fermat) or
any pair $p, q$ (Brieskorn).

**Hanson's parametrisation.** Barr's superquadrics draw a sphere by raising
$\cos$ and $\sin$ to a power; Hanson complexifies the same idea. Writing the
complex hyperbolic functions

$$u_1(t, x) = \cosh(x + i t), \qquad u_2(t, x) = -\,i \sinh(x + i t),$$

which satisfy $u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1$, the $n^2$ maps

$$z_1 = e^{2\pi i k_1 / n}\, u_1^{2/n}, \qquad z_2 = e^{2\pi i k_2 / n}\, u_2^{2/n}$$

for $0 \le t \le \pi/2$ and $|x| \le x_{\max}$ each satisfy
$z_1^{n} + z_2^{n} = 1$ identically. The two integer phases $k_1, k_2$, running
over $0 \ldots n-1$, choose which branch of the fractional power you are on, and
the $n \times n$ combinations are exactly the tiles that piece together into the
whole surface. (One convention trap the code guards against: the factor $-i$ must
sit *inside* the $2/n$ power, as Hanson wrote it; putting it outside — a common
transcription error — only satisfies the equation for $n = 2$, and the code
asserts the residual $|z_1^{p} + z_2^{q} - e^{2\pi i \varphi}|$ stays near zero
across the whole grid to catch exactly that.)

**The projection.** The surface sits in $\mathbb{R}^4$ with coordinates
$(\mathrm{Re} z_1, \mathrm{Re} z_2, \mathrm{Im} z_1,
\mathrm{Im} z_2)$. It is dropped to 3-D by

$$(\mathrm{Re} z_1,\ \mathrm{Re} z_2,\ \cos\alpha\,\mathrm{Im} z_1 + \sin\alpha\,\mathrm{Im} z_2),$$

where $\alpha$ is the Projection Angle. The one direction thrown away,
$\sin\alpha\,\mathrm{Im} z_1 - \cos\alpha\,\mathrm{Im} z_2$, is kept
as a mesh attribute — it is the only surviving trace of the dimension the
projection discarded, and it is what the *Fourth Dimension* colouring shows.

**What the surface is, topologically.** The curve $z_1^{p} + z_2^{q} = 1$ is the
Milnor fibre of the Brieskorn–Pham singularity $z_1^{p} + z_2^{q}$ at the origin.
That means the truncated surface is a genus-minimising Seifert surface spanning
the $(p, q)$ **torus link**: it has Euler characteristic $1 - (p-1)(q-1)$,
$\gcd(p, q)$ boundary circles, and genus
$\bigl((p-1)(q-1) - \gcd(p, q) + 1\bigr)/2$. For $p = q = 5$ that is genus 6 with
five boundary circles; for $(p, q) = (2, 3)$ it is the once-punctured torus that
spans the trefoil. These counts are checked against the finished mesh in the
module's self-test, the same way the Seifert-surface generator checks its braid
closures — so the topology the page claims is the topology the mesh actually has.
