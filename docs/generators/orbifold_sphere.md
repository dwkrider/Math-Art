# Orbifold Symmetry Sphere

![Orbifold Symmetry Sphere](../images/orbifold_sphere.png)

## Overview

A sphere stamped with a comma-shaped motif repeated under a chosen spherical symmetry, so the arrangement of commas and their mirror images makes the symmetry type visible — after the "comma symmetry spheres" of Henry Segerman's *Visualizing Mathematics with 3D Printing*.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Odds & Ends ▸ Symmetry Sphere*.
2. **Pick the Signature.** This is the symmetry type, named by its Conway orbifold signature — all **14 kinds of spherical symmetry**. Seven are infinite families with an adjustable axis order (`nn`, `*nn`, `n*`, `nx`, `22n`, `2*n`, `*22n`), and seven are the fixed "oddities" tied to the Platonic solids (`332`, `*332`, `3*2`, `432`, `*432`, `532`, `*532`). Signatures with a `*` or `x` include mirror or rotoreflection symmetry and so produce mirror-image commas.
3. **Set n** — the order of the main rotation axis. It appears only for the seven families; the seven oddities have their order fixed by their solid and ignore it.
4. **Choose raised or carved relief with Motif Relief.** A **positive** value raises the commas as bumps above the surface (boolean union); a **negative** value carves them into the shell (boolean difference), and a carve deeper than the wall is thick cuts comma-shaped holes clean through. **Motif Size** scales the comma.
5. **Set the shell.** **Sphere Radius**, **Sphere Resolution** and **Shell Thickness** define the ball (thickness 0 gives a solid ball). Turn on **Color Reflected Copies** to give the mirror-image commas a second material so the orientation-reversing copies stand out.
6. **Read the report.** The operator prints the group order $|G|$, how many of its copies are mirrored, and the vertex/face counts; if the group fails to close to its theoretical order (float drift) it warns with both counts.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Cut a little comma out of paper — the shape on a yin-yang symbol, or a tadpole with a curling tail. It is *chiral*: its mirror image is a backwards comma you can't rotate back onto the original, the same way a left glove never becomes a right glove by turning it. Now stick one comma onto a ball and ask: what are all the ways to decorate the whole ball so it looks the same after you spin it or reflect it? Spin the ball a third of a turn and stamp another comma; spin again and stamp again; add a mirror and stamp the backwards comma its reflection demands. Keep going until stamping produces nothing new. The set of stamps you end up with is a *symmetry group*, and there turn out to be exactly **14** genuinely different kinds for a ball — no more. Because the comma is chiral and is placed at a "generic" spot that sits on no axis and no mirror line, every symmetry rule prints a visibly distinct arrangement, and a glance at how the commas and their backwards twins are laid out tells you which of the 14 you are looking at. The maths below lists those 14, shows how the code builds each one as a concrete set of rotations and reflections, and how it stamps the comma with every one of them.

**Orbifold symmetry groups.** The finite subgroups of $O(3)$ -- the symmetry types of patterns on a sphere -- number exactly 14, named by Conway's orbifold notation. Seven are infinite families parametrized by a main-axis order $n$:

| Signature | Schoenflies | Description | Order |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $nn$ | $C_n$ | cyclic | $n$ |
| $\ast nn$ | $C_{nv}$ | pyramidal (vertical mirrors) | $2n$ |
| $n\ast$ | $C_{nh}$ | rotation + horizontal mirror | $2n$ |
| $n\times$ | $S_{2n}$ | rotoreflection | $2n$ |
| $22n$ | $D_n$ | dihedral | $2n$ |
| $2\ast n$ | $D_{nd}$ | antiprismatic | $4n$ |
| $\ast22n$ | $D_{nh}$ | prismatic | $4n$ |

and seven are the "oddities" tied to the Platonic solids: $332$ ($T$, 12), $\ast332$ ($T_d$, 24), $3\ast2$ ($T_h$, 24), $432$ ($O$, 24), $\ast432$ ($O_h$, 48), $532$ ($I$, 60), $\ast532$ ($I_h$, 120).

**Group construction by closure.** Every symmetry of a sphere fixing its centre is an orthogonal $3\times3$ matrix — a rotation ($\det = +1$) or a reflection/rotoreflection ($\det = -1$) — so each group is built explicitly as a set of such matrices. The construction mirrors the "keep stamping until nothing new appears" picture exactly: start from a short list of **generators**, then take products of what you have until the set stops growing. The generators are rotations $R_{\hat a}(\vartheta)$ about an axis $\hat a$ (Rodrigues' formula), together with the horizontal and vertical mirrors $\sigma_h,\sigma_v$, a coordinate swap, and the central inversion $-I$. For example the pure rotation groups are generated by the main-axis turn $C_z=R_{\hat z}(2\pi/n)$ and a horizontal 2-fold $R_{\hat x}(\pi)$, while the three exceptional rotation groups grow from the seeds $R_{(1,1,1)}(2\pi/3)$ (a body-diagonal 3-fold of the cube), $R_{\hat z}(\pi/2)$ (a face 4-fold), and $R_{(0,1,\varphi)}(2\pi/5)$ (a 5-fold through an icosahedral vertex, $\varphi$ the golden ratio) — the axes of the Platonic solids. Adjoining $-I$ or a diagonal mirror to a rotation group is what promotes it to its reflection extension, which is why the mirror families come in at twice or four times the order. The rotoreflection group needs a genuinely new kind of element, $S_{2n}=R_{\hat z}(\pi/n)\,\sigma_h$ — turn a half-step *and* reflect, a motion that is neither a pure rotation nor a pure mirror. Closing the product set can accumulate floating-point drift, so equal matrices are recognised by rounding before dedupe; as a safety check the realized element count is compared to the group's known order and any mismatch is reported.

**The comma motif.** `comma_outline` builds a closed 2D polygon of a comma / teardrop: a circular head of radius $r_0$ blended into a tapering tail swept along a circular-arc centreline of radius $\rho$ through a curl angle of $150^\circ$, with tapering half-width $w(t)=r_0(1-t)^{1.4}$. The tail curls clockwise, making the motif chiral so its mirror images are distinguishable. The polygon is triangulated, placed tangent to the sphere at the generic point (spherical coordinates $\theta=63^\circ$, $\phi=21^\circ$, chosen off every axis and mirror of all 14 groups), and projected radially onto the sphere.

**Relief and replication.** For raised relief ($h>0$) the motif is extruded between radii $R$ and $R+h$ into a closed bump and unioned with the sphere shell. For carved relief ($h<0$) the commas become cutter solids from below the carve depth up through the surface, boolean-differenced out of a hollow shell (relief deeper than the wall pierces right through). Each group element $M$ maps the base motif by $\mathbf p\mapsto M\mathbf p$ — one stamp of the comma per element. The determinant sorts the stamps into the two visible kinds: $\det M=+1$ is a pure rotation and lands a normal comma, whereas $\det M<0$ is orientation-reversing and lands the *backwards* comma (its winding is flipped so it stays a valid solid, and it can optionally be given a second material). This is exactly the chirality made visible: a group with no mirror or rotoreflection element ($532$, say) shows only one handedness, while a group containing them shows commas paired with their reflections. In the larger reflective groups adjacent commas overlap, so the cutter is first resolved into one clean solid by a self-union before the difference, and an EXACT/FLOAT solver cascade accepts the first watertight non-empty result.
