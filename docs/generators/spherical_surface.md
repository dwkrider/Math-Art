# Spherical Surface

![Spherical Surface](../images/spherical_surface.png)

## Overview

Add a surface of revolution of constant positive Gaussian
curvature: the sphere, or Minding's spindle and bulge types.

These are the surfaces of **constant positive Gaussian curvature** ($K = +1$). **Minding** classified the surfaces of revolution of constant curvature in 1838–39; among the $K > 0$ members the sphere is only the symmetric case. Change the meridian's amplitude and you get Minding's other two: the **spindle** (a pointed lemon) and the **bulge** (an open barrel). The operator also offers **Sievert's surface** (1886), a $K = +1$ surface with *no* axis of symmetry — the standing proof that Minding's classification is complete only among surfaces of revolution.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Spherical Surface*.
2. **Pick the Surface.** **Sphere** ($a=1$) is the round sphere; **Spherical Spindle** ($a<1$) closes to a conical point at each pole (a lemon); **Spherical Bulge** ($a>1$) is an open barrel ending at two circular rims; **Sievert's Surface** is the non-rotational $K=+1$ surface.
3. **Set the shape.** For the three rotational surfaces the one knob is **Profile a** — the meridian amplitude in $f(u)=a\cos u$; leaving it at 0 uses the preset's own value, below 1 gives a spindle, above 1 a bulge, exactly 1 the sphere. So the three presets are really one slider.
4. **Sievert only:** in place of Profile a the panel shows **End Trim** — how far short of its two ends the surface is cut. Its height runs off logarithmically there, so the surface is infinitely long and some trim is compulsory; this is the shape control, not a fudge.
5. **Resolution and finish.** **Segments** divides the surface around the axis, **Rings** along the meridian; **Scale** sizes it into the unit cube, and **Smooth Shading** toggles the shading.
6. **Optional rim.** Turn on **Rim Curve** to sweep a tube along the surface's open edge — the bulge's two rims or Sievert's cut ends (a closed sphere or spindle has no edge and gets none). That reveals **Rim Thickness**, **Rim Smoothing** (non-shrinking Taubin passes), **Rim Profile** (Circular, Square, Channel, Beam, Reeded, Curve Only), **Rim Twist** and **Reeds**. The operator reports the surface label, the value of $a$ used, the vertex and face counts, and any rim loops added.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** *Gaussian curvature* measures how a surface bends in two directions at once: a sphere curves the same way all over (positive curvature), a saddle curves opposite ways (negative), a rolled sheet of paper does not really curve at all (zero). Demanding the *same* positive curvature at every single point is a stiff requirement. If, on top of that, you insist the surface be made by spinning a curve around an axis — like shaping clay on a potter's wheel — there turn out to be only three answers: the sphere itself, a pointed "lemon" if you spin a shallower arc, and an open "barrel" if you spin a fuller one. Drop the spinning requirement and stranger shapes with the very same even curvature appear; Sievert's surface is one, with no axis of symmetry anywhere. The rest of this section turns "same curvature everywhere" into the equation the profile curve must satisfy, and then integrates it.

**The profile equation.** Write the meridian in arclength as $(f(u), z(u))$, so that $f'^2 + z'^2 = 1$. For a surface of revolution the Gaussian curvature depends only on the radius function:

$$ K = -\frac{f''}{f}. $$

Setting $K = +1$ turns this into the simple linear oscillator equation

$$ f'' + f = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f(u) = a\cos u, $$

against $f'' - f = 0$ and $f = a\cosh u$ for the $K = -1$ case. The single amplitude $a$ is what splits Minding's family into three, and each case has a clear geometric reading:

- $a = 1$: $f = \cos u$ is the meridian of the **unit sphere**.
- $a < 1$: the meridian meets the axis at a nonzero angle, so the surface closes to a **conical point** at each pole — the **spindle**, a lemon with two sharp tips.
- $a > 1$: the height's slope $z'$ vanishes *before* the meridian reaches the axis, so the surface stops at two circles of positive radius — the **bulge**, an open barrel rather than a closed shape.

**Recovering the height.** With $z' = \sqrt{1 - f'^2}$ and $f' = -a\sin u$, the height is the elliptic integral

$$ z(u) = \int \sqrt{1 - a^2\sin^2 u}\;du, $$

evaluated numerically by cumulative Simpson quadrature. It is computed two different ways, and the reason is geometric. For $a < 1$ the integrand never drops below $\sqrt{1-a^2} > 0$ and is smooth along the whole meridian, so it is integrated directly in $u$. For $a \ge 1$ it vanishes like a square root exactly where $|a\sin u| \to 1$ — which is precisely the rim that gives the bulge its shape — and quadrature in $u$ would then converge only like $h^{3/2}$. The substitution $a\sin u = \sin\phi$ removes the singularity:

$$ r = \sqrt{a^2 - \sin^2\phi}, \qquad dz = \frac{\cos^2\phi}{\sqrt{a^2 - \sin^2\phi}}\;d\phi, $$

which is smooth on the closed interval because $a \ge 1$. At $a = 1$ it degenerates gracefully to $r = \cos\phi$, $dz = \cos\phi\,d\phi$ — the unit sphere in closed form — so the sphere needs no special case of its own.

**Sievert's surface.** Minding's classification is complete only *among surfaces of revolution*, and the natural question is whether $K = +1$ forces that symmetry. It does not, and **Sievert's surface** (1886) is the answer — a surface of constant positive curvature with no axis of symmetry at all, and, what makes it worth having rather than merely existing, one written in ordinary functions rather than as the solution of a differential equation. In cylindrical form, with $D = 2 - \sin^2 v\,\cos^2 u$,

$$ \rho = \frac{2a\sqrt{2 + 2\sin^2 u}\,\sin v}{D}, \qquad \theta = -\frac{u}{\sqrt 2} + \arctan\!\big(\sqrt 2\,\tan u\big), \qquad z = a\left(\ln\tan\tfrac{v}{2} + \frac{4\cos v}{D}\right). $$

The $\ln\tan(v/2)$ term runs to $-\infty$ as $v \to 0$ and to $+\infty$ as $v \to \pi$: those are the surface's own two ends, so it is genuinely infinite in extent and **End Trim** is what cuts it back to a finite mesh. That it is *not* a surface of revolution can be made precise: a surface of revolution meets every horizontal plane in circles, so $\rho$ would be constant along a level set of $z$; on Sievert's surface $\rho$ instead varies by tens of percent along such a level set. The construction is verified by measuring $K$ from the first and second fundamental forms directly rather than trusting the transcription — a necessary check, since a plausible-looking variant of these formulas passes every mesh-level test yet measures $K$ between 2.5 and 7.4 instead of the constant 1.
