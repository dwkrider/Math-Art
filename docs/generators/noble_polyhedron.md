# Noble Polyhedron

![Noble Polyhedron](../images/noble_polyhedron.png)

## Overview

A polyhedron is **noble** when its symmetries can carry any face onto any other
face *and* any vertex onto any other vertex — maximally uniform in two ways at
once. The nine regular polyhedra are noble; so, it turns out, are exactly **146**
others (plus two infinite prismatic families). Mathematicians found them one at a
time for a century and a half — Hess in the 1870s, Brückner by 1907, then the
Stella community — but whether the list was complete stayed open until Connor
Hill's 2026 enumeration settled it. This operator is that complete catalogue.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Noble Polyhedron.
2. **Filter by Vertex Orbit** — the kind of symmetric point set the vertices form.
   `T`, `O`, `C`, `I`, `ID`, `D` are the fixed vertex sets of the regular and
   quasiregular solids; the lowercase `t`, `r`, `s`, `g` types have vertices that
   slide with one or two parameters.
3. **Pick the Polyhedron** by Hill's symbol — e.g. `gD-19.1` is the first faceting
   (largest inradius first) of the 19th critical orbit of the `gD` type.
4. **Dual** builds the polar dual (itself another noble polyhedron, except for
   four whose duals have coinciding vertices).
5. **Finish it** with Style — *Solid*, *Leonardo* panels, *Struts*,
   *Ball and Stick*, *Wireframe*, or *Face Segments* — to make the often
   self-intersecting shells legible.

## Options

| Option | Default | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vertex Orbit | All (146) | Show only the noble polyhedra whose vertices form one kind of orbit. The symbol is Hill's: T, O, C, I, ID, D are the fixed vertex sets of the regular and quasiregular solids; t, r, s and g types have vertices that slide with one or two parameters. All (146), T -- 4 vertices (1), O -- 6 vertices (1), C -- 8 vertices (1), I -- 12 vertices (4), ID -- 30 vertices (6), D -- 20 vertices (7), tO -- 24 vertices (1), and 9 more. |
| Polyhedron |  | Which noble polyhedron of the chosen orbit, by Hill's symbol: T-x.y is the y-th faceting (largest inradius first) of the x-th critical orbit of type T |
| Dual | Off | Build the polar dual instead -- another noble polyhedron, except for four whose duals have coinciding vertices |
| Scale | 1 | Overall size of the result Range 0.01-100. |
| Style | Solid | Finish for the shell: solid, Leonardo panels, struts, ball-and-stick, wireframe, or face segments. Solid, Leonardo (da Vinci), Struts, Ball and Stick, Wireframe, Face Segments. |
| Border | 0.06 | Leonardo face frame width, the same on every face whatever its size Range 0.005-1. |
| Thickness | 0.05 | Panel / strut thickness for the Leonardo and Struts styles Range 0.001-1. |
| Strut Radius | 0.02 | Ball-and-stick edge cylinder radius Range 0.001-0.5. |
| Node Radius | 0.035 | Ball-and-stick vertex sphere radius (0 = no nodes) Range 0-0.5. |
| Depth | 0.15 | How far each face is extruded inward (Face Segments style) Range 0.01-2. |
| Bevel Gap | 0 | Shift the mitre bevels inward to open a gap between neighbouring segments (0 = flush) Range 0-0.5. |
| Explode | 0 | Move each face segment outward along its own normal Range 0-5. |
| Separate Meshes | Off | Output each face segment as its own mesh instead of one combined object |

## How it works

Think of the most symmetric solids you know — a cube, an icosahedron. Part of why
they look so perfect is that every face is interchangeable with every other, and
so is every corner: the shape has symmetries that shuffle the faces among
themselves and, separately, shuffle the corners among themselves. A **noble**
polyhedron is any shape with *both* of those properties — all faces alike, all
vertices alike — and it turns out the regular solids are only the beginning.
Allow faces to be star polygons and to pass through one another, and a whole
menagerie of spiky, self-intersecting shapes qualifies too. The surprising, hard
fact — only proved in 2026 — is that away from the regular solids there are
exactly 146 of them, and not one more.

**What "noble" means precisely.** A polyhedron's symmetry group $G$ acts on its
faces and on its vertices. Noble means that action is *transitive on both*: for
any two faces there is a symmetry taking one to the other (**isohedral**), and
likewise for any two vertices (**isogonal**). Regular polyhedra are flag-transitive,
which is stronger; nobility drops the flag condition but keeps the two separate
transitivities, which is exactly what lets the non-regular stars in.

**Hill's construction.** Every noble polyhedron is built from a single symmetric
**vertex orbit** — the set of images of one point under a symmetry group — by
choosing one face and letting the group carry it around (a **faceting**). Some
orbits are rigid (the `T`, `O`, `C`, `I`, `ID`, `D` types: the vertices of the
regular and quasiregular solids), while others have one or two free parameters,
so the orbit is a point moving on a curve or surface of possible positions. Hill's
theorem is that a noble faceting can only occur at special **critical orbits** —
isolated parameter values — and that across all orbit types these produce exactly
146 polyhedra beyond the regular ones. Each catalogue entry here is *rebuilt* by
the engine from its orbit type, the critical location, and one seed face — never
read back from a stored mesh — and the engine's self-test checks all 146 against
measurements of Hill's own models, so the symbol you pick and the solid you get
are guaranteed to be the one he enumerated.
