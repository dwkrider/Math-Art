# Noble Orbit Explorer

![Noble Orbit Explorer](../images/noble_orbit.png)

## Overview

Where the **Noble Polyhedron** catalogue hands you the finished 146, this is the
*method* that found them, made interactive. Choose a type of symmetric vertex
orbit, slide its free parameters, and a faceting search runs live on that orbit.
Snap to a **critical orbit** and you see the noble polyhedra Connor Hill proved
sit there; move anywhere else and the search comes back empty — which is his
theorem, made visible.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Noble Orbit Explorer.
2. **Pick the Orbit Type** — the family of vertex sets to search; `t`, `r`, `s`,
   `g` types carry one or two free parameters.
3. **Snap to a Critical Orbit** — `0` frees the Position sliders; `1, 2, …` jump to
   the successive critical orbits of this type that carry noble polyhedra, in
   Hill's order.
4. **Or explore freely** with **Position A/B** (the generating point's distances
   from the mirrors), and watch the search return nothing between critical values.
5. **Choose the Faceting** found on the current orbit, and a **Style** to finish.

## How it works

Imagine one dot floating inside a kaleidoscope. The mirrors multiply it into a
symmetric constellation of dots — and as you move the single dot, the whole
constellation breathes in and out while staying symmetric. Those dots are the
would-be *vertices* of a polyhedron. The question Hill answered is: as you slide
that dot, at which exact positions can you connect the dots into a polyhedron that
is **noble** — every face alike and every vertex alike? This tool lets you be the
hand sliding the dot, and runs the connect-the-dots search at every position so you
can watch nobility blink on only at the special spots.

**The orbit.** A symmetry group $G$ and a starting point $p$ give the **orbit**
$G\cdot p = \lbrace g\,p : g \in G\rbrace$ — the vertex set. For the rigid types
the orbit is fixed; for the parametric `t`/`r`/`s`/`g` types, $p$ has one or two
degrees of freedom (its distances from the generating mirrors, the **Position**
sliders), so the orbit sweeps a one- or two-parameter family.

**The search, and the critical orbits.** On a given orbit the tool looks for a
**faceting** — a single face whose images under $G$ close up into a polyhedron
that is both isohedral and isogonal. Hill's result is that such facetings exist
only at isolated **critical orbits**, the parameter values where the orbit's
geometry lines up to admit a noble face; everywhere else the search genuinely
returns nothing. Snapping the **Critical Orbit** control to $1, 2, \ldots$ jumps
to those values in his order and reconstructs the polyhedra found there, so the
same engine that builds the fixed catalogue is here driven by hand — the
enumeration as a live experiment rather than a finished list.

- E. Hess, "Ueber die zugleich gleicheckigen und gleichflaechigen Polyeder" (1876), and his papers of 1875 and 1877, for the first non-regular noble polyhedra and the stephanoids.
- M. Bruckner, "Ueber die gleicheckig-gleichflaechigen diskontinuierlichen und nichtkonvexen Polyeder", Nova Acta Leopoldina (1906).
- B. Grunbaum, "Polyhedra with hollow faces", in Polytopes: Abstract, Convex and Computational (1994), 43-70, which named the noble polyhedra.
- U. Mikloweit, "Exploring Noble Polyhedra With the Program Stella4D", Bridges 2020 Conference Proceedings, 257-264.
