# Neighbourly Polyhedron

![Neighbourly Polyhedron](../images/neighbourly_polyhedron.png)

## Overview

Two famous toroidal polyhedra pose extremal questions. In the **Szilassi**
polyhedron every face shares an edge with every other face; in its dual, the
**Császár** polyhedron, every vertex joins every other, on the fewest vertices a
triangulated torus can have. This generator carries both questions to higher
genus: genus‑3 solids whose faces (almost) all meet, and genus‑2/3 polyhedra built
on the minimum possible number of vertices.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Neighbourly Polyhedron.
2. **Pick the Solid** — the two eight‑nonagon genus‑3 solids (*Rost–Vigh* and
   *Mizhaev*, plus *Mizhaev V1* for contrast), or the fewest‑vertex *Ten‑Vertex
   Genus‑2* and *Genus‑3* polyhedra.
3. **Choose the Style** — *Solid*, a *Papercraft Net* (unfolded with glue tabs), or
   *Finger‑Jointed Plates* for laser cutting (with material thickness, kerf and
   finger width in millimetres).
4. **Map Colours** gives each face its own colour where neighbours must differ, and
   **Mark Doubled Edges** draws the edges where a face pair meets *twice* as red
   rods — the feature that distinguishes these solids.

## How it works

On an ordinary solid like a cube, most pairs of faces don't touch — opposite faces
never share an edge. Now ask for the opposite extreme: a solid where *every* face
borders *every* other face, so each face needs its own colour on a map. On a sphere
that is impossible past four faces (that's why the tetrahedron is special), but on
a **doughnut** it becomes possible — the seven‑faced Szilassi polyhedron does
exactly this, a torus whose seven faces all mutually touch. The dual question asks
for the *stingiest* such shape: the fewest corners a doughnut‑surface polyhedron can
have. This generator pushes both records to doughnuts with more holes.

**Face‑neighbourly, and why the genus jumps.** If every pair of faces shares exactly
one edge and three faces meet at each vertex, Euler's formula pins the genus of an
$n$‑faced solid to

$$g = \frac{(n-3)(n-4)}{12}.$$

After the tetrahedron ($n = 4$, $g = 0$) and Szilassi ($n = 7$, $g = 1$) the next
value needs $n = 12$ faces at genus 6 — still open. Relaxing the rule to let a pair
of faces meet along **two** collinear edges opens up the $n = 8$ case: two genus‑3
solids are known, each with eight non‑convex nonagons, 24 vertices and 36 edges, 20
face‑pairs meeting once and 8 meeting twice. **Mizhaev's** has integer vertices and
a fourfold rotoreflection (its doubled pairs form one 8‑cycle); the **Rost–Vigh**
polyhedron has rational vertices and is chiral (its doubled pairs form two 4‑cycles
— which is what proves the two are combinatorially different). *Mizhaev V1*, the
same size and symmetry but with four face pairs that never meet, is included for
contrast. The **Mark Doubled Edges** option draws exactly those twice‑met edges.

**Fewest vertices.** The other record is Heawood's bound, which limits how few
vertices a triangulated surface of a given genus can have from below; both genus 2
and genus 3 require ten. The ten‑vertex genus‑2 and genus‑3 solids here are concrete
realisations that meet that bound — the Császár question's higher‑genus answers.
