# Quadric

![Quadric](../images/quadric.png)

## Overview

Add a quadric: one of the degree-2 surfaces, built from its
exact parametrisation rather than by contouring.

The **quadrics** are the surfaces whose equation has degree two — the three-dimensional relatives of the conic sections. They are the oldest classified family of surfaces there is: Euler's 1748 *Introductio* already sorts every one of them into the short list below, and nothing outside it is possible. This generator builds each member from its **exact chart** (its parametrisation) rather than by contouring a level set, so the mesh arrives as clean quads with exact normals and no marching-cubes staircase.

There are only a handful of genuinely different shapes, and the whole of degree two fits in one menu:

| Surface | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Sphere, Spheroid, Ellipsoid | The **closed** quadrics — a ball, a rugby ball or squashed globe, a fully triaxial egg |
| Elliptic Paraboloid | A **bowl** — the reflector dish |
| Hyperbolic Paraboloid | A **saddle** — doubly ruled, so built from two families of straight lines |
| Hyperboloid of One Sheet | A **waisted tube** — the other doubly ruled quadric, the cooling-tower shape |
| Hyperboloid of Two Sheets | Two facing **bowls** — genuinely disconnected, two components |
| Elliptic Cone | A **cone** with a sharp apex where it stops being smooth |
| Circular / Elliptic / Hyperbolic / Parabolic Cylinder | A conic **dragged along a line** — the hyperbolic one is again two components |
| Plane | The trivial degree-two surface, both flat and minimal |

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Quadric*.
2. **Pick the Surface.** The thirteen members are the whole of degree two: the closed trio (Sphere, Spheroid, Ellipsoid), the two paraboloids (bowl and saddle), the two hyperboloids (one-sheet tube, two-sheet pair of bowls), the elliptic cone, the four cylinders, and the plane.
3. **Set the shape with the semi-axes.** **Semi-axis a**, **Semi-axis b** and **Semi-axis c** stretch the surface along the three coordinate axes. The Sphere ignores all three (it is always the unit ball); the Spheroid ignores b because it is a surface of revolution, and reads c as its polar radius — c > 1 is prolate (rugby ball), c < 1 oblate (squashed globe); every other quadric uses all three.
4. **Bound the open ones with Extent.** The paraboloids, hyperboloids, cone, cylinders and plane run to infinity, so **Extent** sets how far they are drawn before being cut off. The closed quadrics (sphere, spheroid, ellipsoid, and the two-sheet hyperboloid's throats) ignore it.
5. **Choose the output.** **Style** is either *Solid* (the surface as a continuous sheet) or *Cell Lattice* (an openwork net of struts along the surface's dual, added as a live modifier stack); when Cell Lattice is chosen its own controls appear — **Cell Size**, **Strut Thickness**, **Smoothing**, **Keep Boundaries** and **Even Thickness**. **Segments U/V** set the mesh resolution and **Size** the final half-extent.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints its vertex, face and **component** counts. A component count of 2 is the tell for the two genuinely disconnected quadrics — the Hyperboloid of Two Sheets and the Hyperbolic Cylinder — so you can confirm the surface really is the two-piece one it should be rather than a single sheet drawn by mistake.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Slice a cone with a flat plane and the rim of the cut is a *conic section* — a circle, an ellipse, a parabola, or a hyperbola. Those are exactly the curves whose equation is **degree two**: the coordinates appear squared, or multiplied together, but never to any higher power. A **quadric** is the same idea one dimension up — the *surface* whose equation is degree two in the three coordinates. Write down the squared and product terms of x, y and z, set the sum equal to a constant, and the points that satisfy it trace a smooth shell: a rugby ball, a saddle, a cooling-tower waist, an endless trough. The remarkable fact, known since Euler, is that there are only a handful of essentially different such shells, and everything below is how you name which one you have and how the code draws it exactly.

**The general equation.** Every quadric is the zero set of a single second-degree polynomial,

$$Ax^2 + By^2 + Cz^2 + 2Dxy + 2Exz + 2Fyz + 2Gx + 2Hy + 2Iz + J = 0 .$$

That looks like ten free numbers, but most of them only describe how the surface is turned and where it sits. Collect the quadratic part into a symmetric matrix and the equation becomes $\mathbf{x}^\top M \mathbf{x} + 2\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{x} + J = 0$. Because $M$ is symmetric it has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, so **rotating** to its principal axes clears every cross term $xy, xz, yz$, and then **translating** to the centre clears the linear part wherever a centre exists. What is left is a **normal form** with the axes aligned to the coordinate axes — and the surface's identity is carried entirely by the **signs** of the three remaining quadratic coefficients (the eigenvalues) together with the constant.

**Classification by sign.** Reading off those signs is the whole classification, and it is why the list is finite:

- three like signs, opposite the constant — an **ellipsoid** $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1$ (with $a=b$ a spheroid, with $a=b=c$ the sphere): a closed, bounded shell;
- two positive, one negative, constant $= 1$ — a **hyperboloid of one sheet**, a single connected waisted tube;
- two positive, one negative, constant $= -1$ — a **hyperboloid of two sheets**, the same algebra but now the surface splits into two facing caps;
- two positive, one negative, constant $= 0$ — the **elliptic cone**, the limiting case dividing the two hyperboloids, with a singular apex at the centre;
- one squared term missing, replaced by a linear one — the **paraboloids** $z = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}\pm\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}$: same signs give the elliptic bowl, opposite signs the hyperbolic saddle;
- one coordinate entirely absent — a **cylinder**, the plane conic in the other two coordinates dragged straight along the missing axis.

**Why charts, not level sets.** Each of those normal forms is *exactly parametrisable*, so instead of hunting for the level set numerically the code lays a $(u,v)$ grid onto a known parametrisation. The ellipsoid is the sphere's angles scaled by the semi-axes,

$$(x,y,z) = (a\sin u\cos v,\; b\sin u\sin v,\; c\cos u),$$

with the two poles welded to single vertices so the mesh has no seam. The one-sheet hyperboloid rides hyperbolic functions along its axis and a circle around it,

$$(x,y,z) = (a\cosh u\cos v,\; b\cosh u\sin v,\; c\sinh u),$$

so that $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$ reproduces its defining equation automatically; the paraboloids use $z = u^2$ against a radial or planar sweep, and the cylinders sweep a fixed conic along a straight generator. Every vertex therefore lands *on* the surface to machine precision — the self-test confirms it by feeding each vertex back into its own implicit equation and demanding the residual stay below $10^{-9}$ — and the faces come out as clean quads with exact normals and usable texture coordinates, which a contoured level set could never guarantee. (The two doubly ruled quadrics, the hyperbolic paraboloid and the one-sheet hyperboloid, are also reachable as ruled surfaces elsewhere in the add-on; being buildable two ways is a fact about the surface, not a duplication.)

**The two shapes that are easy to mesh wrongly.** Two members need deliberate care, because a plausible-looking mesh of either is simply wrong. The **elliptic cone** has an **apex** where the surface is not smooth and the chart degenerates; the mesh welds all the apex copies to one vertex rather than leaving a pinched fan. The **hyperboloid of two sheets** really is *two* surfaces — the algebra $|z|\ge c$ forbids the neighbourhood of the equator — so the generator builds both caps and the self-test not only counts two components but checks that they straddle $z=0$ with a clear gap between them; a generator that returned a single sheet would look perfectly reasonable and be wrong. The reported **component** count surfaces exactly this distinction to the user: it is 2 for these two quadrics (and the hyperbolic cylinder, which is the cylinder over the two-piece hyperbola) and 1 for everything else.

## References

- L. Euler, "Introductio in analysin infinitorum", vol. II (1748), appendix on surfaces -- the classification of the quadrics.
- G. Salmon, "A Treatise on the Analytic Geometry of Three Dimensions" (1862), for the ruled quadrics and their generators.
- D. Hilbert and S. Cohn-Vossen, "Anschauliche Geometrie" (1932), chapter 1 -- the quadrics as the second-order surfaces, with the doubly ruled cases discussed geometrically.
- E. W. Weisstein, "Quadratic Surface", MathWorld, for the standard normal forms used here.
