# Turtle Curve

## Overview

One operator for the whole family of classical 2-D line-art fractals — Koch curves, dragon curves, space-filling curves and spirolaterals — each traced by a "turtle" walking and turning, then lifted from a flat drawing into printable geometry.

The six modes are kept separate because there are genuinely six different ways to produce a turtle path, and collapsing them into one would misrepresent all of them (node and edge rewriting, for instance, give *different* figures from the same rule). Whichever mode you pick, the result is one planar path; a shared set of **output lifts** then turns that path into a tube, a filled plate, a terraced relief, or a scaffold.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Fractals ▸ Turtle Curve*.
2. **Pick the Mode** — this chooses the family and changes every control below it:
   - **Koch Teragon** — edge rewriting: replace each segment of a polygon by a scaled generator, giving snowflakes and their kin.
   - **Paper Fold** — the dragon family, drawn by repeatedly folding a strip and unfolding the creases.
   - **FASS Curve** — space-**F**illing, self-**A**voiding, **S**imple, self-**S**imilar curves (Hilbert, Peano, Gosper…).
   - **Word Curve** — turns read off a combinatorial sequence (Fibonacci, Thue–Morse, primes…).
   - **Spirolateral** — walk steps of length $1,2,\dots,n$ at a fixed turn, repeated until it closes.
   - **Node Rewriting** — the classic L-system path, for comparison with edge rewriting.
3. **Set the mode's main shape knob.** For Koch/FASS/Node it is **Iterations** (recursion depth); Paper Fold adds **Fold** (which dragon) and a continuous **Unfold** slider; FASS picks the **Curve**; Word picks the **Sequence** and **Terms**; Spirolateral sets the **Order** $n$.
4. **Dial the mode-specific controls that only some modes show.** Koch Teragon exposes **Generator**, **Initiator Sides**, **Outward** (snowflake vs. antisnowflake) and an optional **Bump Angle** override (Cesàro's apex angle) that only appears when *Override Bump Angle* is on. Word Curve shows **Recommended Settings**: leave it on to draw the sequence's intended figure, or turn it off to expose the **Turn Rule** and **Angle** and explore freely. Spirolateral takes a **Reversals** list (Krawczyk's enumeration of which steps flip their turn) and reports how many blocks it needs to close.
5. **Choose the Output lift.** **Bevelled Curve** is a round tube (with **Tube Radius**, **Bevel Resolution** and **Round Corners** to keep the cross-section even at sharp turns); **Filled Island** and **Extruded Prism** fill the enclosed area flat or with **Height** — closed paths only; **Plain Mesh** is the exact polyline with no bevel; **Mitred Relief** is a ribbon that terraces upward (**Band Width**, **Height Step**, **Miter Limit**) as the curve winds; **Scaffold on Columns** raises the path on columns over a base plate to make an open figure printable. An **Assembly** option can then repeat the piece about an axis or onto the six faces of a cube, and **Scale** sizes the result.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the segment count, whether the path is **closed or open**, and its **turning number** (net turns, $+$ or $-$). If you ask to fill an open path, the operator says so and draws the bevelled curve instead rather than closing it with an arbitrary chord.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture a turtle holding a pen, crawling on paper. It only ever obeys three orders: *go forward*, *turn left by some angle*, *turn right by some angle*. That is the entire vocabulary — yet by feeding the turtle a long enough list of these orders you can draw a snowflake, a dragon, a curve that fills a whole square, or a spiralling rosette. Every mode of this generator is just a *different way of writing that list of orders*. One mode builds the list by repeatedly replacing each straight stretch with a wigglier copy of itself (that is how you get a Koch snowflake); another builds it by folding an imaginary paper strip and reading off which way each crease bends; another reads the turns straight out of a number sequence like the Fibonacci word. Once the turtle has finished drawing, the flat outline it left behind is thickened into a real 3-D object.

The six modes exist because those "different ways of writing the list" are genuinely different mathematics, not cosmetic variations:

**NODE** — node-rewriting [L-systems](lsystem.md), the classic path. The turtle's instruction string is rewritten symbol by symbol, and a module's placement depends on the *whole chain* of instructions from the base of the structure up to it, so context (orientation, branching state) accumulates along the branch. This is the natural setting for plant-like, branching figures where where-you-are depends on the entire route taken to get there.

**EDGE** — the Koch construction. Here a *segment* is replaced by a polyline whose position, orientation and scale come from **that segment alone**. This locality is exactly what distinguishes it from node rewriting: the replacement is a similarity transform of one fixed generator onto the segment, and it knows nothing about where the segment sits in the larger figure. Signed teragon generators are applied to an $n$-gon initiator, and the sign chooses which side of each segment the new detail bulges toward — flip it and the outward snowflake becomes the inward antisnowflake, because every bump is reflected across its own edge. Repeating the replacement drives the curve toward a true fractal whose dimension is $\log(\text{new segments})/\log(\text{scale factor})$.

**FOLD** — paper folding. Fold a strip of paper in half, then in half again the same way, and so on; unfold it and every crease is either a valley or a ridge. Reading that crease sequence as a list of left/right turns and drawing it with equal angles produces the dragon curve — at $90°$ the Heighway dragon. The insight the generator exploits is that the crease pattern of generation $k{+}1$ is the pattern of generation $k$ with a fixed alternating sequence interleaved, so the curve refines predictably. The **Unfold** parameter is genuinely **continuous**: rather than snapping between whole generations it opens each new crease from $0$ to its full angle, so the curve can be keyframed to unfold smoothly on screen.

**FASS** — space-**F**illing, self-**A**voiding, **S**imple and self-**S**imilar curves: Hilbert, Peano, Gosper and their kin. These are the turtle-drawn cousins of the [space-filling curves](space_filling_curve.md): each iteration subdivides the region more finely, and in the limit the curve passes arbitrarily close to every point of a 2-D region while never crossing itself — a genuinely one-dimensional path with a two-dimensional image.

**WORD** — the turn sequence is read off a combinatorial word rather than generated by geometric rewriting. Each term of a sequence — Fibonacci word, Thue–Morse, the ruler sequence, the primes — is mapped to a turn by the chosen **Turn Rule**, which reaches a class of figures defined by number theory rather than self-similarity. Because most rule/angle combinations degenerate into a zigzag along one axis, each sequence carries a recommended angle and rule under which it draws its intended figure.

**SPIRO** — spirolaterals: walk steps of length $1, 2, \dots, n$ in a repeating block, turning by a constant angle at each step, optionally reversing the turn at selected steps. The figure closes only after a whole number of blocks, and how many is given in closed form by $q/\gcd(n,q)$ where $q = 360/\text{angle}$ — the operator computes and reports it, and warns when the parameters produce an unbounded spiral instead ($q$ dividing $n$). The enumeration of which reversal sets give *distinct* figures is pruned using the **Whitney–Graustein invariant** — the turning number of a closed plane curve, which classifies such curves up to regular homotopy (continuous deformation keeping the velocity nonzero). Two reversal sets with the same turning number give the same figure up to deformation, so comparing invariants prunes the search instead of drawing and eyeballing every combination.

**Lifting into geometry.** Whatever the mode, the finished path is one $(N,2)$ array of points, and a shared stage turns it into an object. The lifts split into curve settings and mesh builds: the **bevelled tube**, the **filled island** and the **extruded prism** are all one Blender curve with, respectively, a round bevel, a flat 2-D fill, and an extruded fill; the **mitred relief** and the **scaffold** are built directly as meshes because they terrace or add columns. Which fills are offered depends on **closure** — an open arc has no interior to fill — and that decision is not guessed from whether the endpoints happen to land near each other. It comes from the Closed-Path Theorem (Abelson–diSessa) in `lsystem/closure.py`, which decides closure from the *turn sequence itself*: a turtle path closes exactly when its steps and net turning return it to its start pose. A round-corner fillet is applied to the tube only, because a sharp mitre would otherwise pinch the tube's cross-section to $\cos(\theta/2)$ of its width at each corner; the mesh lifts mitre their own joints instead.
