# Biscribed Solid

![Biscribed Solid](../images/biscribed_solid.png)

## Overview

Add a biscribed solid: vertices on a circumsphere AND faces
tangent to a concentric insphere (exact symmetric construction).

A **biscribed** solid has all its vertices on a circumsphere *and* all its faces tangent to a concentric insphere. Ordinary Archimedean solids manage only the first: their faces come in several orbits at different distances from the centre, so no single insphere touches them all.

Restoring that second property means deforming the solid until the face orbits agree — which is possible for some families and provably impossible for others. The interesting part of this generator is therefore as much what it *cannot* build as what it can.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Biscribed Solid*.
2. **Pick the Solid.** The list is the catalog of biscribable forms — the biscribed truncations (truncated octahedron, icosahedron, cuboctahedron, icosidodecahedron), the biscribed snubs and their Catalan-type duals (tetrakis hexahedron, pentakis dodecahedron, disdyakis and pentagonal-hexecontahedron families), and the chiral **propello / hexpropello** families. Every entry is already solved to be biscribed; there is no shape knob to dial because the biscribing condition fixes the geometry uniquely.
3. **Choose a Style** — Solid, Leonardo (open face-frames, with **Border**/**Thickness**), Struts, Ball and Stick (**Strut Radius**/**Node Radius**), Wireframe, or Face Segments (one inward-extruded plate per face, with **Depth**, **Bevel Gap**, **Explode** and **Separate Meshes**) — and a **Coloring** (by face size, or none).
4. **Notice what is absent.** The forms with no biscribed solution — the plain rectified cuboctahedron and icosidodecahedron, and any truncation whose defining function never balances — are simply not in the list, because the generator reports "no biscribed form" rather than approximating one.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture two soap bubbles, one just inside the other, sharing a centre. A "biscribed" solid is one that touches *both* at once: every one of its corners lies on the outer sphere, and every one of its flat faces just kisses the inner sphere. A cube already does this — all eight corners at one distance, all six faces at another. But most many-faced solids come with faces of *different* sizes sitting at *different* depths, so no single inner sphere can touch them all; a soccer-ball shape has its pentagons and hexagons at slightly different heights. To biscribe such a solid you have to squash and stretch it — deepen the shallow faces, raise the deep ones — until every face lands at the same depth. For some shapes there is a squash that works; for others you can prove there is none, and the honest answer is "impossible", which is what the generator reports instead of fudging a near-miss. Each family below is fixed by its own bit of algebra.

The unifying fact is that for a **vertex-transitive** family the outer (circum)sphere is automatic — a single orbit of vertices means a single distance from the centre — so biscribing reduces to one demand: **equalise the face-orbit plane distances** using whatever shape freedom the family has. The number of independent handles a family offers turns out to equal the number of face orbits it must reconcile, which is the deep reason some families biscribe and others cannot.

**Truncations** ($tT$, $tC$, $tO$, $tD$, $tI$) carry exactly one handle, the truncation depth $t$, which slices each edge at $(1-t)A+tB$ — with $t=\tfrac12$ the full rectification. As $t$ grows, the original base faces stay put (their plane distance is constant in $t$) while the fresh truncation faces at the cut corners march inward or outward, so the mismatch

$$g(t)=d_{\text{vertex face}}(t)-d_{\text{base face}}$$

changes sign exactly where the two orbits meet at a common depth. A **root of $g$ in $(0,\tfrac12)$** is therefore a biscribed form, and it is located by bisection. If $g$ never changes sign on the interval the two orbits can never be brought level, so **no biscribed form exists** — again reported, not approximated.

**Rectified solids** — the cuboctahedron and icosidodecahedron — are the limiting case with *zero* handles: rectification has already frozen the shape, yet its two face orbits sit at unequal depths. With nothing left to vary there is no way to level them, so no biscribed form exists.

**Omnitruncates** (the truncated cuboctahedron and truncated icosidodecahedron) are the clean, numeric-free case. Their faces lie in planes perpendicular to the 2-, 3- and $n$-fold symmetry axes, and the three plane offsets $(d_2 : d_3 : d_n)$ are free up to overall scale. Because every vertex is the intersection of exactly one plane of each type, pushing all three planes to the same distance,

$$d_2=d_3=d_n=1,$$

*is* the biscribed form outright — the tangency condition and the vertex-on-sphere condition are satisfied by construction, with no equation to solve.

**Snubs** (snub cube, snub dodecahedron) start from the orbit of a free point on the sphere under the *pure rotation* group, which gives two parameters (mod scale) against three face orbits — the $n$-gons, the axis triangles and the generic triangles. Two of those orbits must be levelled against the third, giving two equations $g(p)=0$ that are solved by a damped Newton iteration. The chiral **propello / hexpropello** families (and the propello/snub of the truncated forms, and the orthokis-propello) are handled by the same idea generalised to a larger symmetry orbit; because those roundest-root solves are slow, their coordinates are precomputed and reloaded so the operator responds instantly.

**Duals come for free.** Polar reciprocation about the sphere of radius $\rho=\sqrt{R\,r}$ — the geometric mean of the circum- and in-radii — carries a biscribed solid to its dual while *preserving* both spheres, so the dual is biscribed with the very same $(R,r)$. That single fact hands over the tetrakis hexahedron, pentakis dodecahedron, the disdyakis solids and the pentagonal icositetra-/hexecontahedron without any further solving.

The pattern worth carrying away: a family's freedom is exactly its number of independent face orbits, which is why the parameterless solids simply have no biscribed form at all.
