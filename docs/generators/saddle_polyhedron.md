# Saddle Polyhedron

![Saddle Polyhedron](../images/saddle_polyhedron.png)

## Overview

Add one of Pearce's saddle polyhedra.

A **saddle polyhedron** is a solid bounded not by flat faces but by **saddle polygons** — skew (non-planar) circuits, each one spanned by a curved minimal surface, the shape a soap film takes across a bent loop of wire. Peter Pearce catalogued fifty-three of them in *Structure in Nature is a Strategy for Design* (1978), every one built from a single scaffold he called the **Universal Node**: a branching net whose struts run in only 26 directions of the cubic lattice. Because so many of these cells tile space with no gaps, they form a designer's kit of parts for space-filling structure — and the operator can build a single cell, its repeat unit, or a whole packing.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Saddle Polyhedron*.
2. **Pick the Solid** from the thumbnail gallery. Each entry is one verified row of Pearce's Table 8.1, numbered as in the book; the names — decatrihedron, tetragonal saddle hexahedron, and the rest — are told apart by shape, which is why the selector is a picture gallery rather than a list.
3. **Choose the Faces.** *Minimal surface* relaxes each saddle face to the soap film Pearce defines it as; *Ruled* keeps the straight-line ruled patch a flat panel approximates; *Spidron nest* fills each face with a spiralling nest of triangles; *Branch network* draws the Universal Node net alone, as rods.
4. **Set the mesh density** with **Edge divisions** (segments per branch), and, for minimal faces, the number of **Relax steps** of area minimisation. These two appear only for the Minimal and Ruled styles.
5. **Dial the spidron controls** (Spidron nest only): **Nest rings** (annuli per face), **Nest step** (shrink between rings), **Nest twist** (rotation between rings), **Fill centres** (close the tiny hole each spiral leaves), and **Limit twist** (clamp the twist to the measured intersection-free range).
6. **Choose the Layout.** *One solid* is the bare cell; *Repeat unit* (two cells sharing a face) and *Space filling* (a block of **Cells across / deep / high**) appear only for solids that actually pack. For a packing you can also set **Colour** (by cell, winding form, face, or uniform), **Shrink** each cell so the packing reads as separate solids, **Mirror** to the enantiomorph, and **Separate objects** to emit each cell as its own object.
7. **Read the report.** Each build prints the Table 8.1 number, the face count, and — for spidron faces — how many faces took a *true* nest of congruent triangles versus a *generalised* one (mere decoration on an irregular face). A packing reports how many cells it placed and what fraction of the block they fill, and says plainly when a solid *does not fill space alone*, rather than passing a partial packing off as a tiling.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Bend a loop of wire into a wavy, non-flat ring and dip it in soapy water: the film that spans it is not flat — it dips on one side and rises on the other into a *saddle*. Now imagine welding several such loops edge to edge into a closed cage. You get a solid whose "faces" are all gentle saddles instead of flat polygons — a saddle polyhedron. Peter Pearce's discovery is that a whole family of these, fifty-three of them, can be built from one simple connector whose arms only ever point in a fixed set of directions, like the struts of a climbing frame. And because the arms are so regular, many of the cages stack together to fill space completely, the way cubes do but far more interestingly. Everything below makes that picture exact: which directions the arms take, how the code *finds* Pearce's solids rather than copying coordinates he never printed, and how each saddle face is computed.

**The Universal Node net.** All fifty-three solids live inside one graph. Its edges — Pearce's "branches" — run in just 26 directions of a cubic lattice: the 6 face directions $\langle 100\rangle$, the 12 face-diagonals $\langle 110\rangle$, and the 8 body-diagonals $\langle 111\rangle$ of a cube. A **saddle polygon** is a closed circuit in this net, and a **saddle polyhedron** is a closed complex of such circuits enclosing a volume. The whole catalogue is therefore not fifty-three unrelated shapes but fifty-three ways of closing up circuits in one fixed scaffold — which is exactly what makes them fit together.

**The table as a checksum, not a blueprint.** Pearce prints no coordinates, only a *row of invariants* for each solid: node valences, branch counts split by direction class, face count, face types, included angles, face-plane directions, symmetry axes. So the code does not transcribe the solids — it **finds** them. It searches the net for closed complexes and keeps only those matching a row on every column it can check; the table is the acceptance test, not the source. Coverage is honest as a result: rows for which no geometry has been verified are simply not offered, because a plausible guess with the right face count would be worse than a shorter, correct list.

**The angle theorem the search rests on.** Over all $\binom{26}{2}=325$ pairs of the 26 branch directions there are exactly **12 distinct angles**, and every included angle Pearce prints is one of the twelve. The angle between two integer lattice directions $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ is

$$\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\Vert\mathbf a\Vert\,\Vert\mathbf b\Vert},$$

and enumerating it over the 325 pairs collapses to a tiny finite set. That finiteness is what lets a found circuit's corners be checked against Pearce's row exactly, rather than to within a tolerance — the difference between verifying a solid and hoping it is right. (The check even catches a misprint: the table's lone "69°" is not a branch-pair angle at all, and two-fold symmetry forces that corner to be 60°.)

**Minimal faces.** With a saddle polygon's skew boundary fixed, *Minimal surface* spans it with a discrete minimal surface — the soap film — by cotangent-Laplacian area minimisation after Pinkall and Polthier. Each interior mesh vertex $\mathbf x_i$ is relaxed toward the position where the cotangent-weighted Laplacian vanishes,

$$\sum_{j\in N(i)}\bigl(\cot\alpha_{ij}+\cot\beta_{ij}\bigr)\,(\mathbf x_j-\mathbf x_i)=\mathbf 0,$$

where $\alpha_{ij}$ and $\beta_{ij}$ are the two angles opposite edge $ij$ in the triangles sharing it. This is the discrete statement that the surface has zero mean curvature everywhere — mechanical equilibrium of a soap film, equal tension pulling in every direction — so the relaxed face is the genuine minimal spanning surface, not merely a smoothed one. *Ruled* skips the relaxation and keeps the straight-line patch, the ruled saddle a flat physical panel actually approximates.

**Spidron nests, and an honesty gate.** A *true* spidron nest — the foldable kind, all its triangles congruent — exists only on an **equilateral, equiangular** skew polygon: one edge length and one included angle throughout. That is an exact test, not a judgement call, and the operator applies it face by face. Faces that pass get a true nest; faces that fail get the *generalised* similarity spidronisation, which is drawable but whose annulus triangles are not congruent — decoration rather than a spidron in the strict sense. The build reports the split so the distinction is never silent.

**Space filling, and the two winding forms.** A packing is *verified*, not assumed: the tiling engine measures whether the cells' volume actually accounts for the block, and the operator passes that fraction on rather than presenting a partial packing as a space filling. When faces are spidron-decorated a further rule bites, from van Ballegooijen, Gailiunas and Erdely: any two coincident faces must read **clockwise from one cell and counter-clockwise from the other**, so their nests match across the shared face. Cell adjacency is bipartite, so a breadth-first two-colouring assigns exactly two winding "forms" in the required alternation. Chirality then makes this subtle: a rotation of $+t$ about a face's outward normal is a rotation of $-t$ about the inward one, so the two cells meeting at a face build the *same* surface — but the intersection-free range of twists is not symmetric in sign for a single chiral cell, and a packing, containing both forms, is clamped to the symmetric minimum of the two. Every finished solid is then fitted to the standard 2 m cube.

## References

- Peter Pearce, "Structure in Nature is a Strategy for Design", The MIT Press, 1978 (paperback 1990), ch. 8 -- the Universal Node system, saddle polygons spanned by minimal surfaces, and Table 8.1's inventory of 53 saddle polyhedra.
- Walt van Ballegooijen, Paul Gailiunas & Daniel Erdely, "Spidronised Space-fillers", Bridges 2009 Conference Proceedings, pp. 271-278 -- spidron nests on saddle-polyhedron faces and the rule that clockwise must meet counter-clockwise across a shared face.
- Daniel Erdely, "Some Surprising New Properties of the Spidrons", Bridges 2005 Conference Proceedings, pp. 179-186 -- the spidron nest the face decoration generalises.
- Ulrich Pinkall & Konrad Polthier, "Computing Discrete Minimal Surfaces and Their Conjugates", Experimental Mathematics 2(1), 1993, pp. 15-36 -- the cotangent-Laplacian area minimisation used for the saddle faces.
