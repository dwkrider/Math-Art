# Polytwister (Experimental)

![Polytwister (Experimental)](../images/polytwister.png)

## Overview

Add the 3D cross-section of a polytwister -- a Boolean tree over
cycloplanes (Hopf-fibration 4D->3D, after Bowers / Ho).

A **polytwister** is a curved four-dimensional shape built from a polyhedron through the Hopf fibration. Jonathan Bowers discovered them around 2007 and enumerated 222 uniform ones plus three infinite families.

The idea is a substitution. A polyhedron is an intersection of half-spaces, one per face. A polytwister replaces each half-space by a **cycloplane** — the Hopf preimage of a spherical cap — and intersects those instead. Because cycloplanes live in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and are invariant under the Hopf circle action, the result is a 4-D solid that can only be seen by slicing: what you get here is a 3-space cross-section, and sweeping the slice coordinate animates the shape passing through our space.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Polytwister (Experimental)*.
2. **Pick the Polytwister.** The five regular seeds — **Tetratwister**, **Cubetwister** (the default), **Octatwister**, **Dodecatwister**, **Icosatwister** — are the plain intersection twisters, one per Platonic solid. Each has a **Quasi-** and a **Bloated-** variant that rebuild the same seed as a *union* of ring cells (open, flower-like, rather than one solid lump), and the star polyhedra add the **Great**, **Small Stellated** and **Great Icosa** twisters. **Dyadic Twister (n)** is the infinite family — just $n$ cycloplanes spaced around the equator — and it reveals the **Dyadic n** slider.
3. **Sweep the Slice w.** This is the fourth-dimensional cross-section coordinate. $w=0$ is the central slice; key it across a range to animate the polytwister drifting through our 3-space, its cells appearing, twisting and shrinking away. Push $|w|$ past the shape's extent and the slice is empty — the operator then reports *"Empty cross-section at this slice w"* and cancels, so dial $|w|$ back down.
4. **Set Cylinder Segments** for resolution — the number of facets per cycloplane cylinder. Because the quasi, bloated and great twisters union many solids through **Exact** Boolean modifiers, high segment counts get slow; raise it only when you need the silhouette clean.
5. **Choose the output framing.** *Recenter* re-centres each slice on its own bounding box (leave it **off** to keep a fixed centre across a $w$-sweep, so the animation doesn't jitter); *Shade Smooth* and *Scale* finish the look.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the vertex count and the slice value it was cut at (`… verts, slice w=…`), so you can confirm which cross-section you are looking at.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A flat creature living in a plane, watching a sphere pass through its world, would see a dot that swells into a disc and shrinks back to a dot — a moving *slice* of a shape one dimension higher than it can picture. A polytwister does the same to us: it is a shape in **four** dimensions, and all we can ever see is its three-dimensional slice, a solid that grows, twists and dissolves as we slide the slice across. To build one, start from the recipe for a polyhedron — a cube, say, is "the set of points on the inside of all six face-walls at once." A polytwister keeps that recipe but swaps every flat wall for a special **curved** wall borrowed from the geometry of the 3-sphere (a *cycloplane*), and keeps the points inside all of them. The everyday miracle that makes it drawable is that although those walls live in 4-space, each one's slice is just an ordinary cylinder — so the whole thing can be assembled with the same Boolean union/intersect/subtract tools you would use on any solid model. Everything below makes that precise.

**Cycloplanes.** The Hopf fibration maps $S^3\subset\mathbb{R}^4$ to $S^2$, sending each point of the little sphere back to a whole *circle* on the big one — a way of combing $S^3$ into interlocking rings. A **cycloplane** is the preimage of a spherical *cap*: gather all the Hopf circles over a cap, and you get a solid, circle-invariant region of $S^3$. So where a polyhedron's face contributes a flat half-space, a polytwister's face contributes this curved region, and the size of the cap plays the role the face's distance-from-centre plays for the polyhedron. A "hard" polytwister is then a Boolean tree over cycloplanes — intersections, unions, differences and rotated copies — mirroring exactly how the parent polyhedron is assembled from half-spaces, which is why the regular seeds are plain intersections and the quasi/bloated variants are unions of pieces.

**The load-bearing closed form.** Everything rests on one fact that makes the shape computable at all: the 3-space cross-section of a *single* cycloplane is a **transformed cylinder**. For a cycloplane with zenith $z$ and azimuth $a$, writing $\theta=z/2$, the section at slice $w$ is obtained from a unit $Z$-axis cylinder by

1. rotate $+90°$ about $X$ — lay the cylinder on its side, so its axis is the direction the Hopf circles sweep;
2. scale $X$ by $1/\cos\theta$ — the cap's tilt foreshortens the section into an ellipse of exactly this eccentricity;
3. translate along $X$ by $w\tan\theta$ — this is where the slice coordinate enters, sliding the cylinder sideways in step with $w$;
4. rotate by $-\theta$ about $X$ — swing it to the cap's colatitude;
5. rotate by the azimuth $a$ — turn it to the cap's longitude, placing it around the equator like the corresponding face of the polyhedron.

That is why the construction is tractable: slicing a curved 4-D region would normally demand sampling a scalar field, but here each primitive sections to an *exact* cylinder mesh, so the entire Boolean tree evaluates with ordinary solid modelling in 3-space (the code uses Blender's **Exact** Boolean solver, baking each node before the next). The $1/\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ terms both diverge as $\theta\to\pi/2$: a cycloplane whose cap approaches a full hemisphere sections to an *unbounded* region, so the code handles that south-pole limit as a special flat slab of half-height $\sqrt{1-w^2}$ — which is why it empties out entirely once $|w|\ge 1$ and a slice can come back empty.

**Sweeping $w$.** The slice coordinate is the fourth dimension made into a dial. Animating it shows the polytwister entering, passing through and leaving our 3-space — the exact 4-D analogue of the flat creature's sphere-as-a-circle, except that the cross-sections here *twist* as they go (the Hopf circles carry a rotation along with them), which is the "twister" the name records. Each named twister is a fixed tree of these cylinders; only $w$ (and the mesh resolution) changes between frames, so an animation is a genuine tour through the 4-D cells rather than a morph faked in software.

This module is a faithful port of Nathan Ho's reference toolchain, whose realizer walks a JSON-able tree of `{cycloplane, intersection, union, difference, rotated_copies}` nodes; the zenith constants and per-shape trees are transcribed from it verbatim, so the shapes match Bowers' enumeration exactly.
