# Supershape

![Supershape](../images/supershape.png)

## Overview

Gielis superformula: 3D supershapes, supertoroids, seashells
and superellipsoids from a compact set of parameters.

Johan Gielis's **superformula** is a single polar equation that, by varying a handful of parameters, produces circles, polygons, stars, flowers, starfish, seed pods and gears — an unreasonable range of outlines from one expression. Gielis proposed it in 2003 as a unifying description of natural form.

The claim is worth taking with some salt: a formula flexible enough to fit almost any shape explains none of them. But as a *generator* it is genuinely powerful, and this module builds the 2-D outline up into solids four different ways.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Supershape*.
2. **Start from a Preset.** The 22 curated shapes are grouped by construction: the 3-D blobs (Sphere, Starfish, Flower, Gear Blob, Spiky, Rounded Cube, Seed Pod, Twisted, Bulb), the rings (Torus, Gear Ring, Star Tube, Faceted Ring), the shells (Conch, Snail, Tusk) and the superquadrics (Ellipsoid, Cube, Cylinder, Octahedron, Star, Pillow). Each preset carries its own mode and every parameter that mode needs, so picking one fully determines the shape.
3. **Or choose Custom** and set the **Mode** yourself: **3D Supershape** (the spherical-product blob), **Supertoroid** (a lobed, faceted ring), **Shell** (a cross-section swept along a growth spiral) or **Superellipsoid** (Barr's cube-to-sphere-to-star superquadric).
4. **Dial the superformula.** Two parameter sets — each $(m, n_1, n_2, n_3, a, b)$ — drive the shape. **m** sets the lobe count (the symmetry); the three **n** exponents trade roundness against sharpness; **a**, **b** scale the two in-plane axes. For 3D Supershape and Supertoroid, *set 1* shapes the longitude/ring and *set 2* the latitude/tube; for Shell only *set 1*'s $m, n_1, n_2, n_3$ shape the swept cross-section.
5. **Set the mode-specific controls** that appear only for the relevant mode: **Torus Radius** for the Supertoroid (keep it above the tube size to stay embedded); **Exponent (vertical)** and **Exponent (horizontal)** for the Superellipsoid (below 1 is boxy, 1 an ellipsoid, above 2 a pinched star); **Whorls**, **Growth / Whorl**, **Tube Size** and **Spire Rise** for the Shell.
6. **Raise the resolution** where the outline turns sharply. **Segments** samples the longitude/ring and **Rings** the latitude/tube — the lobe corners are genuine kinks in the function, so they facet unless finely sampled.
7. **Colour and scale.** **Coloring** is None, Latitude Bands (a pole-to-pole gradient) or By Distance; the mesh is centred and fit to a 2 m cube, then multiplied by **Scale**. The status line reports the mode and the vertex/face counts, and degenerate parameters that collapse the mesh to nothing are caught and reported as an error rather than producing an empty object.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Stand at the centre of a flat shape and, for every direction you can face, ask "how far away is the edge?" A circle always answers with the same distance; a star answers with a long reach out to each point and a short one into each notch. The **superformula** is a single rule that produces that answer for any direction, and a few knobs reshape what it says. One knob, called $m$, decides how many times the outline repeats as you turn all the way around — how many petals, arms or gear teeth. The others decide whether each lobe bulges out fat and round or pinches in thin and spiky. Because the whole outline is nothing more than "distance as a function of direction," it is easy to spin or stack that outline into a solid, and this module does exactly that four different ways.

**The superformula.** In polar coordinates the radius at angle $\varphi$ is

$$r(\varphi)=\left(\left|\frac{\cos(m\varphi/4)}{a}\right|^{n_2}+\left|\frac{\sin(m\varphi/4)}{b}\right|^{n_3}\right)^{-1/n_1}.$$

Read it from the inside out. The argument $m\varphi/4$ steps the cosine and sine through $m$ quarter-cycles over one full turn, so $m$ is exactly the **symmetry number**: a whole revolution meets $m$ lobes, which is why $m=5$ draws a starfish and $m=16$ a gear. The two terms are the outline's grip on its two axes, and the exponents $n_2,n_3$ set how sharply each term wins or yields as $\varphi$ sweeps — the geometry of "rounded" versus "cornered" lives entirely in them. The outer power $-1/n_1$ then inverts the sum, so a *large* bracket pulls the radius *inward*: that inversion is what turns a gentle bump in the bracket into a sharp notch in the shape. Setting $n_1=n_2=n_3=2$ with $m=4$ makes the bracket $\cos^2+\sin^2=1$, a constant radius — a plain circle; raise the exponents and the outline squares off, drop them below 1 and the sides cave inward into star points. It is the superellipse (the $m=4$ case) generalised to any number of lobes.

**Building solids.** Each of the four routes assembles superformula curves in a different way, so the same 2-D idea becomes a whole family of solids.

**Supershape 3D** — the *spherical product* of two superformulas, one governing longitude ($\theta$) and one latitude ($\varphi$):

$$\mathbf{x}(\theta,\varphi)=\big(r_1(\theta)\cos\theta\, r_2(\varphi)\cos\varphi,\ \ r_1(\theta)\sin\theta\, r_2(\varphi)\cos\varphi,\ \ r_2(\varphi)\sin\varphi\big).$$

This is the classic 3-D supershape. Think of it as a globe whose lines of longitude are one superformula outline and whose lines of latitude are another: the first modulates the equatorial profile, the second the pole-to-pole profile, and their product is a genus-0 blob welded shut at two poles. Setting either formula to $m=0$ recovers a plain sphere in that direction, which is how the Sphere preset falls out of the same machine.

**Supertoroid** — the same two formulas mapped onto torus topology instead. Now neither direction closes to a pole: one superformula bends the *big ring* into a lobed or gear-toothed path, the other shapes the *tube* cross-section into a faceted star, and because both directions wrap the result is a watertight genus-1 ring. The Torus Radius sets how far the tube sits from the axis, and dropping it below the tube size is what makes the ring pass through itself.

**Shell** — a superformula cross-section swept along a **logarithmic spiral**, the growth curve molluscs actually follow. A snail cannot rebuild its shell as it grows, so it can only *add* to the open rim; the only way to grow while keeping the same shape is to scale up by a fixed factor each turn, which is exactly an exponential spiral. Here the cross-section keeps its outline but multiplies in size by *Growth / Whorl* every revolution and climbs by *Spire Rise*, so a fixed superformula section on that spiral produces conch and snail forms with genuine self-similar whorls.

**Superellipsoid** — Barr's superquadric. Instead of the full superformula it uses signed powers of cosine and sine, $\mathrm{sign}(\cos\theta)\,|\cos\theta|^{e}$, with two exponents that run the solid continuously from cube (exponents near 0) through sphere (exponents 1) to octahedron and on to pinched stars (exponents above 2). The signed power is the trick that keeps the correct octant while allowing a fractional exponent — a plain fractional power of a negative number is undefined. This is the one member with a life outside art: superquadrics are a standard primitive in solid modelling and shape fitting.

**A numerical note.** The absolute values in the formula make $r$ continuous but not smooth: at each lobe boundary its derivative jumps, so a mesh sampled at uniform $\varphi$ facets exactly where the outline turns most sharply. Denser **Segments**/**Rings** is the only remedy, because the corner is genuinely a kink in the function and not an artefact of the mesh. One more subtlety: a lobed supershape is not star-shaped about its centre, so the builder checks the mesh's actual signed volume and flips every face if it came out inside-out, rather than trusting a fixed winding to put the normals outward.
