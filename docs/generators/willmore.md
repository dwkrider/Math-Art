# Willmore Surface

![Willmore Surface](../images/willmore.png)

## Overview

Minimize the bending energy of a closed surface: Willmore
tori converging to the Clifford shape, red-blood-cell vesicles
at reduced volume, and prescribed-curvature rings.

The **Willmore energy** measures how much a surface bends:

$$E=\int (H-h_0)^2\,dA .$$

Minimising it asks the purely geometric question "what is the least-bent surface of this topology?" — and among all tori the answer was conjectured by Willmore in 1965 to be the stereographically projected **Clifford torus**, of aspect ratio $\sqrt2$ and energy $2\pi^2$. It stood for nearly fifty years until Marques and Neves proved it in 2014.

The same energy, constrained instead by fixed area and volume, is the **Helfrich membrane** model — and its equilibria are the shapes of red blood cells. One functional, a pure geometry problem at one end and cell biology at the other.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Willmore Surface*.
2. **Pick the Mode.** **Willmore Torus (Clifford)** flows a doughnut to the least-bent torus; **Vesicle (red blood cell)** minimises bending at fixed area and volume (the Helfrich problem); **Inflated Ring** relaxes a torus at fixed volume toward a prescribed tube curvature.
3. **Torus mode:** set the **Start Ratio** (fat or thin) — the flow forgets it, which is the point — and the **Resolution** around the major circle, then watch it converge to the Clifford shape.
4. **Vesicle mode:** the main knob is **Reduced Volume**, the enclosed volume relative to the sphere of the same area ($1$ is the sphere, $\approx 0.65$ the classic biconcave disc, lower deepens the dimples). **Seed** picks which side of the energy barrier the descent starts on — Oblate (discocyte) or Prolate (capsule) — **Sphere Subdivision** sets the mesh, and **Spontaneous Curvature** biases the branch.
5. **Inflated Ring mode:** **Spontaneous Curvature** $h_0$ is the target the tube thins toward, and **Start Ratio** seeds the loop.
6. **Set Iterations and read the report.** The descent is a budget that stops early once it reaches a stationary point. The torus prints its final energy against $2\pi^2\approx19.739$ and its fitted aspect ratio against $\sqrt2\approx1.414$; the vesicle prints its energy against the sphere's $4\pi$, the reduced volume, and the constraint drift; the ring prints its energy and final aspect. Those numbers are the check that the flow found the true minimiser rather than stalling.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Bend a thin sheet — a page, a plastic lid — and it pushes back; the harder you curve it, the more it resists, and holding it curved stores energy. The **Willmore energy** adds up that stored bending over an entire closed surface, giving one number for "how bent, in total, is this shape." Ask which shape of a given kind bends the *least* and you get a clean geometry puzzle. Among all doughnuts the answer is a particular one — the **Clifford torus**, whose fat-to-thin ratio is $\sqrt2$ — conjectured in 1965 and only proved in 2014. Now add two rules of the game: hold the surface's **area** fixed and hold the **volume it encloses** fixed. The least-bent shape under those rules is the shape of a **red blood cell** — a sphere deflated until it caves into a biconcave disc. None of these is drawn from a formula. The program starts from a rough guess and lets it roll downhill in energy, like a marble settling to the bottom of a bowl, until it stops moving. All three modes are that same downhill roll on the same energy, just with different rules held fixed.

**The energy.** Everything is one functional,

$$E=\int (H-h_0)^2\,dA,$$

where $H$ is the **mean curvature** — the average of the surface's two principal curvatures, the sharpest and gentlest bends at a point. A flat spot has $H=0$; a sphere of radius $R$ has $H=1/R$ everywhere; a saddle, curving up one way and down the other, has the two contributions partly cancel. Squaring $H-h_0$ and integrating penalises any departure from the preferred curvature $h_0$, summed over the whole surface. With $h_0=0$ this is the pure Willmore energy $\int H^2\,dA$; it is **scale-invariant** — shrinking or growing the shape does not change $E$ — which is why the torus mode needs nothing pinned down to keep the flow from simply collapsing or blowing up.

**Descending on a mesh.** The surface is carried as a triangle mesh, so the energy becomes a sum over vertices (each vertex's mean curvature read off from the areas and angles around it). The key to a stable flow is that the solver knows the **exact gradient of that discrete sum** — the true first variation of the energy the mesh actually has, derived in closed form rather than estimated by finite differences. Having the exact slope means every step points genuinely downhill, and a line search can guarantee the energy decreases at each accepted move.

**Why motion is restricted to the surface normal.** The continuous Willmore flow only ever pushes points *perpendicular* to the surface — sliding a point along the surface does not change the shape, so it should not change anything. On a mesh, though, that freedom is a trap: a solver allowed to move vertices *tangentially* can lower the discrete energy by quietly **bunching vertices together**, a numerical artifact that improves the number without improving the shape and eventually degenerates the mesh. The minimiser therefore moves every vertex **only along its normal** and hands the job of keeping vertices evenly spaced to periodic **groom** passes. This is what separates a torus flow that terminates cleanly at the Clifford shape from one that sails past it and falls apart — the exact difference the solver was tuned against.

**Holding the constraints.** The vesicle and ring fix area and/or volume. Each descent step is projected back onto the constraint surface — a Lagrange-multiplier correction followed by a damped Newton restore — and, crucially, the line search measures the energy at the *restored* trial position, so an accepted step is honestly downhill *on the constraint manifold*, not merely before the correction. The reported **constraint drift** is how far area and volume slipped despite this; a tiny drift certifies the shape really is the deflated cell it claims to be.

**The three families.** **Willmore torus** flows from *any* torus of revolution with $h_0=0$ and no constraints, converging to the Clifford torus; the reported energy against $2\pi^2\approx19.739$ and aspect ratio against $\sqrt2$ are the check that it found the true minimiser and make the Marques–Neves theorem something you can watch happen. **Vesicle** fixes area and volume — their ratio is the *reduced volume* $v<1$, the degree of deflation — and minimises bending, the Helfrich problem, with an optional spontaneous curvature $h_0$ biasing which branch is found. **Inflated ring** relaxes a torus at fixed volume toward a prescribed tube curvature $h_0$, thinning the tube and widening the loop.

> **Note on the branch.** The module describes an oblate start descending to the biconcave discocyte and a prolate start to a capsule. In practice this generator returns the **capsule** in both cases: measured dimensions are $(2.0, 0.55, 0.55)$ for `seed_shape='OBLATE'` and `'PROLATE'` alike, at reduced volume 0.65 and 0.60. The figure above therefore shows the capsule, which is what the operator produces; the discocyte branch appears not to be reachable at present.
