# Antoine's Necklace

![Antoine's Necklace](../images/antoine.png)

## Overview

Add Antoine's necklace: nested chains of interlocked tori.

**Antoine's necklace** (Louis Antoine, 1921) is a Cantor set that is *knotted into space*. Take a solid torus; replace it by a chain of smaller solid tori linked through its hole; replace each of those by a chain of smaller ones still; continue forever. What remains in the limit is a Cantor set — totally disconnected, uncountable, of measure zero.

The point is what surrounds it. An ordinary Cantor set sitting on a line has simply connected complement: any loop in the surrounding space can be shrunk to a point. Antoine's cannot — a loop threaded through the original torus's hole is trapped by the chains at every level. So two sets can be homeomorphic to each other and yet sit in space in genuinely different ways, which is the discovery that opened up **wild** topology.

### Using it

There is no mode to choose — the generator always builds the same nested-chain construction — so the picture is driven entirely by how deep the recursion goes and how the chains are shaped.

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Knots & Curves ▸ Antoine's Necklace*.
2. **Set the Depth** — the one knob that changes *what you are looking at*. Depth 1 is a single closed chain of tori; depth 2 replaces every ring in that chain by its own smaller chain; depth 3 does it again. The ring count grows as $\text{links}^{\text{depth}}$, so depth is capped at 4 to keep the mesh manageable.
3. **Choose the chain shape.** **Chain Links** is how many tori make up each chain (rounded up to an even number so the alternating links close the loop). **Link Overlap** is how far each child ring reaches across the gap to its neighbour, as a fraction of the centre spacing — it is automatically raised for you when a chain has many links, so adjacent rings always stay genuinely interlocked rather than merely close.
4. **Set the tube thickness** with **Tube Ratio**, the tube radius as a fraction of each ring's radius — thin rings read as a delicate chain, fat ones fuse into a chunkier braid.
5. **Pick the output resolution** — **Ring Segments** and **Tube Segments** are the two mesh resolutions of each torus, plus **Scale** and **Smooth Shading**. The whole result is centred at the origin and fit to a 2 m cube.
6. **Read the report.** On completion the operator prints the total ring count and the vertex/face totals (`N rings, V=…, F=…`), so you can see the geometric explosion — a 6-link chain at depth 3 is already $6^3 = 216$ rings.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture the chain of a necklace: a loop of rings, each one hooked through its two neighbours. Now imagine that instead of being solid metal, every ring is *itself* a tiny necklace — a smaller loop of smaller linked rings — and every one of those is a still tinier necklace, and so on without end. Antoine's necklace is that idea followed all the way to its limit. What the generator draws is a snapshot after a few rounds of this replacement; the true necklace is what you would see if you never stopped. The remarkable fact is not the object itself but the *space around it*: because a chain always threads through the hole it hangs from, a loop of string passing through the original ring can never be lifted free — it is caught by the chain, and the chain is caught by its chain, forever. That "can't-be-freed" quality, present at every scale, is what makes the necklace **wild**.

**One stage of the construction.** Around each parent torus's core circle, `count` child tori are placed at equal angular spacing $\varphi_i = 2\pi i/\text{count}$. Their axes **alternate** between the parent's axis $\mathbf e_3$ and the radial direction $\mathbf{rad}_i = \cos\varphi_i\,\mathbf e_1 + \sin\varphi_i\,\mathbf e_2$: an even-indexed child lies in the plane the parent lies in, an odd-indexed child is tipped a quarter-turn out of it. That alternation is the whole trick. Two rings whose axes are perpendicular and whose centres are about a ring-radius apart pass through one another exactly like the links of a chain, so consecutive children — one flat, one tipped — automatically interlock, while children two apart share an orientation and merely sit side by side.

For this to close up into a genuine chain the child radius cannot be too small. The centre-to-centre spacing of neighbours around a circle of radius $R$ is $2R\sin(\pi/\text{count})$, and each child ring is given radius $\rho = \text{gap}\cdot 2R\sin(\pi/\text{count})$. If $\text{gap}$ is too small the rings fall short of each other and the "chain" is really a loop of *unlinked* tori — no longer Antoine's construction at all. The code therefore floors the overlap at $\text{gap} \ge 0.52 + 0.8/\text{count}$, an empirical threshold (verified in the self-test by computing the Gauss linking number of neighbouring core circles, which must equal $\pm 1$ for a Hopf link and $0$ for non-neighbours). This is why the **Link Overlap** slider silently rises as you add links: more links sit closer together angularly, so each must reach proportionally further to stay hooked.

The chain closes on itself around the parent's core, and — this is the essential part — the closed chain **threads the parent's hole**. That is what a loop through the hole cannot escape: to shrink, it would have to pass through the chain, and the chain is itself a closed loop around the same hole.

**Recursion.** Each child becomes a parent at the next stage, with its own chain of still smaller, still interlocked tori. Stage $n$ holds $c^{\,n}$ rings for a chain of $c$, so the count grows geometrically and the radii shrink geometrically; the generator renders the rings of the deepest stage as tube tori.

**The limit.** The nested intersection

$$A=\bigcap_{n\ge 0} T_n,\qquad T_{n+1}\subset T_n$$

of all stages is the necklace itself. It is a Cantor set: each stage separates the previous one into disjoint pieces, the diameters go to zero, and nothing connected survives. It is *wild* because the complement's fundamental group is non-trivial at every level — the linking that makes each chain thread its parent is never undone by going deeper.

An alternating axis pattern requires an even `count`; with an odd one the chain fails to close consistently, and consecutive rings at the seam share an axis instead of interlocking.
