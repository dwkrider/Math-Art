# Spidron Rosette

![Spidron Rosette](../images/spidron_rosette.png)

## Overview

Add a spidron figure: a spiral of triangles filling a
regular polygon, as a single arm, a rosette or a tiling.

A **spidron** is a plane figure made of an ever-shrinking, alternating chain of triangles that spirals down to a point. Dániel Erdély devised it in 1979 as an exercise for Ernő Rubik's design class: connect alternate corners of a regular polygon, a smaller turned copy appears inside, and the ring between the two subdivides into triangles; repeat and the polygon fills with a spiral of them. Six such spiralling "arms" fill a regular hexagon — the **spidron nest** — and because the hexagon tiles the plane, so does the whole spidron system. This operator draws the flat side of the family: the bare subdivision, a single arm, a **rosette** of arms wound round a polygon (following Gangopadhyay), and the plane tilings built from either.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Spidron Rosette*.
2. **Pick the Layout.** *Full Figure* fills one polygon with all its arms; *Single Arm* draws one spiralling limb on its own; *Rosette* wreathes one arm onto each edge of a polygon round an open centre; *Tiling* repeats a motif across the plane; *Named Nest* builds one of the 34 catalogued spidronised polygons (most are skew and so not truly planar — those are better seen in the [Spidron Nest](spidron_nest.md) and [Spidroball](spidron_ball.md) generators).
3. **Set the main shape knobs.** **Arm Parts** is the number of sides of the polygon each arm subdivides (its arm angle is $360°$ divided by this); five is the smallest that spirals inward and six is the classical hexagonal spidron. **Rings** is how many times the spiral step repeats — each ring is smaller than the last, so past about sixteen the additions are invisible.
4. **Dial the layout-specific controls.** For *Rosette* (and the *Wreath* tiling) set **Corners**, the number of arms carried round the central polygon — independent of Arm Parts — and **Winding**: *Clockwise* and *Anticlockwise* are the papers' two mirror designs and *Both* superimposes them. For *Tiling* pick the **Motif** (*Wreath* repeats the open rosette; *Figure* repeats the filled polygon, and at six arm parts the hexagons sit on the honeycomb lattice and tile with no gaps — the classic spidron tiling) and the **Columns**/**Rows** count. *Named Nest* instead exposes **Nest**, **Nest step**, **Nest twist** and **Cap centre**. Every arm can also be a *Spidron*, *Creeper* or *Ladder* (the **Arm** control), which changes how the triangles group into arms.
5. **Choose the output.** **Color** assigns materials by arm, triangle shape, ring or uniformly; **Colors** caps the palette (two gives the two-tone pinwheel). **Margin** insets each triangle to leave a grout gap, **Height** extrudes it into relief, and **Separate Tiles** emits one object per triangle instead of a single mesh — all through the shared Pattern Engine.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the layout, the arm count $m$, the number of arms and the triangle count; a *Figure* tiling with an arm-part count other than six warns that only six tiles the plane gaplessly and that a spaced grid was built instead.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Draw a regular polygon and join every *other* corner. A smaller polygon, turned a little, appears inside — and the star-shaped ring left between the big polygon and the small one splits neatly into triangles. Now do the same thing to the small polygon, and to the one inside that, and so on: you get a spiral staircase of triangles winding tighter and tighter toward the centre, each ring a shrunk, rotated copy of the one outside it. That spiral is the spidron figure. A single "arm" is one column of that staircase followed inward; the whole figure is several arms interlocked, and a rosette is just a ring of arms stood up around a polygon. The rest of this section turns "join alternate corners" into the exact shrink factor and turn angle, because those two numbers are all the code needs to draw every ring.

**The nested-polygon step.** Take a regular m-gon of circumradius $R$ with vertices $P_k$, and join every second vertex. The inner polygon that appears is another regular m-gon $Q_k$, and a little trigonometry (a similarity of two isosceles triangles) fixes its size and orientation exactly:

$$\frac{R'}{R} = \frac{\cos(a)}{\cos(a/2)}, \qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{a}{2}, \qquad a = \frac{2\pi}{m}.$$

Here $a$ is the polygon's central angle, $R'$ the inner circumradius and $\Delta\phi$ the twist between the two rings. Geometrically the ratio is the cosine of the whole central angle over the cosine of half of it — the projection that carries an outer edge onto its inner chord — and it is **positive only when $a < 90°$, that is $m \ge 5$**. Below that the "inner" polygon would be larger than the outer one and the figure never converges. At $m = 6$ the numbers are the famous ones: $\cos 60° / \cos 30° = \tfrac{1}{2} \big/ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 1/\sqrt3$, with a $30°$ turn.

**Filling the ring.** The annulus between the outer m-gon $P$ and the inner m-gon $Q$ triangulates into $2m$ triangles, two per sector $k$:

$$(P_k,\; P_{k+1},\; Q_k) \quad\text{and}\quad (Q_k,\; P_{k+1},\; Q_{k+1}).$$

The first is a wide triangle sitting on an outer edge, the second a narrow one closing onto the inner ring. At $m = 6$ these are precisely the two triangles of Erdély's original definition — a $120°$ isosceles triangle and an equilateral one — with edge lengths in the ratio $1 : 1/\sqrt3$. Iterating the similarity ring after ring stacks these pairs into the full subdivision, and because every ring is the same shape scaled by $R'/R$, the triangles never overlap and exactly tile the polygon minus a vanishing central core of radius $R\,(R'/R)^{\text{rings}}$.

**Arms, and the three flavours.** Following Gangopadhyay, the figure is assembled not ring by ring but **arm by arm**: an arm is the chain of triangles at a fixed sector index as it spirals inward, and the full figure is exactly $m$ of them interleaved, so every triangle remembers which arm it belongs to (which is what colour-by-arm shows). The three arm kinds differ only in how the sector index advances from ring $i$ to the next:

- **Spidron** — the index is fixed, $k = \text{index}$, so the arm spirals straight in; its scale per step is $\cos(a)/\cos(a/2)$.
- **Creeper** — the index advances one per ring, $k = \text{index} + i$, so the limb winds around the figure as it shrinks.
- **Ladder** — the index advances one every *second* ring, $k = \text{index} + \lfloor i/2 \rfloor$, so a single step spans two rings and four triangles and the scale per step is *squared*, $\big(\cos(a)/\cos(a/2)\big)^2$.

**The rosette.** A rosette carries $n$ arms round a regular n-gon, one per edge, spiralling on the outside round an open centre. The trick that keeps **Corners** and **Arm Parts** independent is matching lengths: an m-part arm of circumradius $R$ has base edge $L = 2R\sin(\pi/m)$, so the carrier n-gon is built with exactly that side length, circumradius

$$R_n = \frac{L}{2\sin(\pi/n)} = \frac{R\sin(\pi/m)}{\sin(\pi/n)},$$

and each arm drops onto its edge at natural size whatever $n$ is. The *Anticlockwise* winding is each *Clockwise* arm reflected across its edge's perpendicular bisector, and *Both* lays the two mirror families together (lifted a hair apart when flat, so the coincident faces do not z-fight).

**Tiling the plane.** For most motifs neighbouring copies are packed on a lattice whose pitch is the motif's own bounding box, so they touch without overlapping and open background shows between them. The exception is the *Figure* motif at six arm parts: the filled hexagon tiles the plane on the **honeycomb lattice**, neighbouring centres one double-apothem apart,

$$\text{pitch} = 2\cos\!\frac{\pi}{6} = \sqrt3\,,$$

along the edge-normal directions. On this lattice adjacent hexagons share whole edges exactly, the spiral arms of neighbouring tiles meet without a seam, and the plane is covered with no gaps at all — Erdély's classic spidron tiling. No other arm count tiles the plane, so those fall back to an honest spaced grid.
