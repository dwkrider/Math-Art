# Celtic Knot 2D

![Celtic Knot 2D](../images/celtic_knot_2d.png)

## Overview

Add a 2D Celtic knot (grid-and-barriers plait).

Classic grid-and-barriers **Celtic knotwork**: an interlaced plait laid on a rectangular grid, woven strictly over-and-under, and carved into bordered panels and knots by internal **barriers**. This is the construction the Insular and Pictish stone-carvers actually used, as formalised by the modern mathematical accounts.

The elegant reading is Jablan's: a barrier is a **mirror**, and the cord is a light ray bouncing around a box of mirrors. Where a wall carries no barrier the two strands crossing there pass through one another; where it does, the strand reflects. That single reinterpretation turns a craft tradition into a computable object — and yields facts like the loop count of a plain $W\times H$ bordered plait being exactly $\gcd(W,H)$.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Celtic Knot 2D*.
2. **Pick the Substrate.** **Square Grid** is the classic mirror-curve plait; every other choice is a tiling's medial graph (Mercat's "knot on any graph"). **Grid Width** and **Grid Height** set the panel size in cells.
3. **Choose the frame.** On the square grid, **Border** rings the panel with barriers (a bordered knot) or, off, leaves an open tileable plait; on a tiling substrate you get **Trim Boundary** instead, which keeps only the faces inside a clean central rectangle.
4. **Pick where the internal barriers come from** with **Barriers**: **Preset** draws a named classic panel (the **Panel** menu — Plain Plait, Cross Break, Framed Panel, Triquetra, Josephine, Kells Border, Carpet Page — square grid only); **Procedural** rules regular walls (**Wall Spacing**, **Vertical/Horizontal Walls**, and a **Symmetry** point group); **Random** scatters them from a **Seed** at a **Barrier Density**, with **Single Cord** nudging the layout toward one continuous loop and its own **Symmetry** group.
5. **Shape the cord.** **Cord Width** sets the ribbon width; **Style** is **Angular** (sharp mitred corners) or **Smooth** (Catmull-Rom, which unlocks **Smoothness** and a **Corner Style** — **Rounded** cap or the authentic **Pointed (Lenticular)** leaf turn with its **Corner Tightness**).
6. **Choose the Output.** **Ribbon Mesh** builds solid cords and unlocks the **Profile** (Flat Ribbon / Round Tube / Twisted Rope, with **Tube Sides**, **Rope Strands**, **Rope Twist**), **Interlace (Weave)** (plus **Interlace Mode** and **Weave Height**), **Color By**, **Relief Height**, **Backing Slab** and **Separate Cords**; **Centerline Curves** emits the bare cord paths instead.
7. **Read the report.** The operator prints the substrate and the number of separate closed **cords** (with vertex/face counts) — the count you can predict from $\gcd$ on a plain plait — and warns if a requested single-cord search or symmetry could not be fully satisfied.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture one ribbon laid diagonally across a grid of square cells, always travelling at 45°. Each time it reaches a little wall between two cells it does one of two things. If that wall is open, it simply crosses the ribbon coming the other way — one passes over, one under. If that wall is a **barrier**, it can't get through and **bounces**, turning a sharp 90° like a ball off a cushion or a light ray off a mirror. Ring the whole panel with barriers and the ribbon is trapped inside, looping around forever: that loop *is* the knot. Where you place the interior barriers decides the outline of the design and how many separate loops it breaks into.

**The grid.** Work on an integer lattice $(i,j)$ with $0\le i\le 2W$, $0\le j\le 2H$, over a $W\times H$ array of cells each two units wide. The parity of the coordinates classifies every point:

| Point | Parity | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | $i,j$ both even | the cell corners |
| **Secondary** | $i,j$ both odd | the cell centres, about which the cord crosses |
| **Edge-midpoint** | $i+j$ odd | the midpoints of the cell walls |

The cord threads the grid through the edge-midpoints, running as straight $45°$ diagonal strands between them. Every **barrier** occupies one edge-midpoint.

**The mirror rule.** A strand's state is a position together with one of the four diagonal directions $(\pm1,\pm1)$, and it has a single deterministic successor. Advancing one step lands on the next edge-midpoint; if that wall carries no barrier the two strands there pass straight through one another (a crossing), and if it carries a barrier the strand **reflects** — the direction component perpendicular to the wall flips, turning it through $90°$:

$$\text{vertical wall: } (d_i,d_j)\mapsto(-d_i,\,d_j),\qquad \text{horizontal wall: } (d_i,d_j)\mapsto(d_i,\,-d_j).$$

This is exactly a light ray in a box of mirrors — Jablan's "mirror-curve" reading of knotwork. The outer border is a full ring of barriers, which is what turns the plait into a bordered panel; interior barriers carve the design into separate knots and motifs.

**Why it always closes.** The successor map is a **bijection** on the finite set of directed strand-segments — every state has one predecessor as well as one successor — so iterating it **partitions** all segments into disjoint closed orbits, the separate cords. No strand can dangle or dead-end; a plait is always a union of closed loops.

**The loop count.** For a plain $W\times H$ bordered plait with no internal barriers, the number of separate closed cords is

$$\gcd(W,H).$$

So a $6\times4$ panel is two interlocked cords and a $5\times3$ panel is a single unbroken one — which is why traditional designs favour coprime dimensions when a single continuous cord is wanted, a rule the carvers knew empirically. Adding internal barriers changes the count, and that is the design lever: barriers both shape the outline and decide how many separate cords the finished piece contains. The **Single Cord** option leans on this directly, toggling one wall at a time — each toggle merges or splits two cords — until a single loop remains.

**Weaving.** Celtic interlace is an *alternating* diagram: following any cord the sense runs over, under, over, under…, and each crossing has one strand over and one under. Because a reflection swaps a strand between the two diagonal families, the alternation reduces to a parity (XOR) constraint between consecutive crossings along a cord,

$$x_{c_1}\oplus x_{c_2} = 1 \oplus (\text{number of reflections between them}),$$

solved globally with a parity union-find. The result is rendered either as a $z$-offset (the woven 3D surface) or by breaking the under-cord at each crossing (flat 2D interlace), so the plait reads as woven ribbon rather than as crossing lines.

**Beyond the square grid.** The same cord lives on **any** planar tiling by Mercat's "knot on any graph" construction: a crossing sits at every tiling **edge midpoint** and the cord routes around the faces, weaving over and under. Reusing the generator's uniform-tiling substrates, the cord's control points are the shared edge midpoints, the cross/reflect successor is again a bijection so the orbits are again closed cords, and the tiling's boundary edges act as the border. The over/under becomes a checkerboard 2-colouring of the faces, with a graceful frustration seam wherever the faces are not bipartite.

- Aidan Meehan, "Celtic Design: Knotwork" (1991) -- the grid-cell drawing tradition.
- Slavik V. Jablan, "Mirror curves" and "Symmetry, Ornament and Modularity" (2002) -- knotwork as billiard / mirror curves, and the component count of the plain plait.
- Christian Mercat, "Knots and links from a graph" / "Les entrelacs des enluminures celtes" -- the construction of an interlaced knot on the medial graph of ANY planar graph, generalizing the grid plait to arbitrary tilings (used here for the non-square substrates).
