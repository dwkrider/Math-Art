# Canal Surface

![Canal Surface](../images/canal_surface.png)

## Overview

A **canal surface** is the smooth skin wrapping a ball of changing size as it
slides along a curve — the envelope of a one-parameter family of spheres. A
garden hose (a constant-radius ball on a bent wire) is the everyday case; let the
radius pulse or taper and you get bulging tubes, beaded strands and, in one
special case, the Dupin cyclide. This generator builds the *true* envelope, with
each sphere's contact circle correctly tilted as the radius changes — not the
naive swept tube, which is subtly wrong wherever the radius varies.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Canal Surface.
2. **Pick the Spine** — the curve the spheres travel along: a straight line,
   circle, ellipse, helix, torus knot, or a curve you have selected. Each spine
   has its own shape knobs: **Ellipse Aspect**, **Turns**/**Pitch** for the
   helix, **Knot P**/**Knot Q** for the torus knot.
3. **Choose the Radius Law** — how the sphere's radius changes along the spine:
   *Constant*, *Taper* (from **Radius** to **End Radius**), *Waves* (**Wave
   Count**, **Wave Depth**), or *Cyclide*.
4. **For the exact cyclide:** the *Cyclide* law on an *Ellipse* spine, with
   **Slope** read as $c/a$, produces a genuine Dupin cyclide — the surface that
   is a canal surface in two different ways.
5. **Set resolution and caps** — **Spine Samples** (how many contact circles
   along the spine), **Sides** (vertices per circle), and **Cap Ends** to close
   an open spine watertight with the true spherical caps.

## How it works

Picture sliding a ball along a bent wire, letting the ball grow and shrink as it
goes, and shrink-wrapping cling film around every position the ball passes
through. That film is the canal surface. The one subtlety — and the whole reason
this is not just a bevelled tube — is *where the film touches each ball*. If the
ball's size is changing, the film does not touch it around the "equator"
perpendicular to the wire; it touches along a circle that is tilted and slid a
little forward or back, because the growing ball is being overtaken by the film
on one side. Get that tilt wrong and the tube quietly pokes through its own
spheres; get it right and the surface is exactly the envelope.

**The characteristic circle.** Let the spine be $c(s)$ parametrised by arclength
$s$, carrying a sphere of radius $r(s)$, and write $r' = \mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}s$
and $T(s)$ for the unit tangent. Each sphere touches the envelope along a circle
— the *characteristic circle* — whose geometry is the entire construction:

$$\text{centre } = c(s) - r\,r'\,T(s), \qquad \text{radius } = r\sqrt{1 - r'^2},$$

and whose plane is tilted away from the normal plane (the plane perpendicular to
$T$) by the angle $\arcsin(r')$. Read those three facts off one at a time. The
centre is pulled *back* along the tangent by $r\,r'$: when the sphere is growing
($r' > 0$) the contact slips backward, toward where the sphere was smaller. The
radius $r\sqrt{1 - r'^2}$ is *smaller* than the sphere's own $r$, shrinking to
zero as $r'\to 1$ — when the radius grows as fast as arclength, the envelope
pinches to a point and there is no room for a circle at all. And the tilt
$\arcsin(r')$ is what a naively swept tube omits: that tube places a circle of
radius $r$ in the normal plane, which cuts into the spheres wherever $r'\ne 0$.
The tilt term is exactly the correction this generator adds over the repo's
constant-radius tube machinery, which remains correct for $r' = 0$.

**The Dupin cyclide.** A surface that is a canal surface in *two* different ways
— enveloping spheres along two distinct families of spines — is a **Dupin
cyclide**, the shape whose lines of curvature are all circles. It appears here as
the *Cyclide* radius law on an *Ellipse* spine: with the slope read as $c/a$, the
radius varies exactly as Dupin's construction requires, and the tilted contact
circles close up into the cyclide's own circular curvature lines.

- C. Dupin, "Applications de geometrie et de mechanique" (1822) -- the cyclides as the surfaces that are canal surfaces in two ways.
- V. Chandru, D. Dutta and C. M. Hoffmann, "On the geometry of Dupin cyclides", The Visual Computer 5 (1989), 277-290 -- the sphere-family construction and the (a, b, d) quartic normal form used by the self-test.
