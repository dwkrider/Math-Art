# Zonohedrification

## Overview

Add a zonohedrification, a zonish polyhedron or a rhombohedral
dissection.

A **zonohedron** is a convex polyhedron built from a set of line segments — its *zones* — every face of which is centrally symmetric. **George Hart's zonohedrification** (1999) makes one from any seed solid: take a direction out of the seed (usually one per vertex) for each zone, and sum the segments. The tetrahedron and the cube both become the rhombic dodecahedron; the icosahedron becomes the rhombic triacontahedron; a truncated cuboctahedron becomes a 24-zone solid of 552 parallelograms.

This operator offers that construction and two relatives that share the same star of directions: the **zonish** polyhedron (the seed *plus* those zones, which — unlike a true zonohedron — keeps the seed's odd-sided faces), and the **dissection** of the zonohedron into its parallelepiped blocks (for the rhombic triacontahedron, Kowalewski's twenty golden rhombohedra — ten acute, ten flat).

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Zonohedrification*.
2. **Pick the Mode.** **Zonohedron** builds the pure zonotope (Hart's zonohedrification); **Zonish** builds the seed expanded by its zones, keeping its odd-sided faces; **Dissection** cuts the zonohedron into its rhombohedral blocks.
3. **Choose the star of directions** with **Seed** (Icosahedron by default, from 22 Platonic, Archimedean and Catalan solids) and **Zones From** (**Vertices** — Hart's choice — or **Face Normals** or **Edges**). These two together fix the set of zones everything is built from.
4. **Set the mode-specific control that appears.** Zonish adds **Zone Length** — small values barely bevel the seed, large ones swamp it toward the zonohedron. Dissection adds **Explode**, which pushes each block outward along its own centre to open the solid up, and **Separate Blocks**. Zonohedron and Zonish (but not the dissection, which is already an assembly of solid blocks) show the finish **Style** — Solid, Leonardo, Struts, Ball and Stick, Wireframe or Face Segments — with that style's own sub-controls (Border, Thickness, Strut/Node Radius, Depth…).
5. **Colour and output.** **Colour By** paints faces by **Zone Pair** (the two zones spanning each face), **Face Size** (edge count, telling on a zonish solid), or **Block** (dissection only, one colour per zone triple); **None** leaves one material. **Separate Blocks / Meshes** splits the result into one object per piece.
6. **Read the report.** A zonohedron or zonish solid reports its vertex and face counts (`V=… F=…`); a dissection reports `N blocks (a acute, b flat)`, so you can check, for instance, that the icosahedron's dissection gives the expected twenty (ten acute, ten flat).

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Take a handful of drinking straws pointing in various directions. Lay copies of them out tip-to-tail in every possible combination and mark every point you can reach; the solid boundary of all those reachable points is a **zonohedron**. Every flat face on it is a little parallelogram spanned by two of the straws, which is why a zonohedron always looks like it is tiled with slanted diamonds. The seed polyhedron does just one job here — it supplies the *directions* of the straws (its vertices pointing outward, say). Choose a different seed, or take the straws along the seed's edges or face-normals instead of its vertices, and you get a different zonohedron. Everything below turns that straw picture into the sum-of-segments the code actually computes, and then reads off how many faces and corners it must have.

**The zonotope.** Collect the seed's chosen directions into a *star* of vectors $\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ (parallel and antiparallel duplicates collapsed, so the icosahedron's twelve vertices give six zones). The zonohedron is their **Minkowski sum of segments** — the *zonotope*

$$ Z = [0,v_1] \oplus [0,v_2] \oplus \cdots \oplus [0,v_n] = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} t_i\,v_i : t_i \in [0,1] \right\}. $$

Geometrically each generator $v_i$ contributes a family of parallel edges — a *zone* — running right around the solid. Every edge of $Z$ is a translate of some $v_i$; every 2-face lies in the plane of a pair $v_i, v_j$ and is centrally symmetric (a plain parallelogram when only that pair is coplanar). That every face has a centre of symmetry is exactly the defining property of a zonohedron, and it is forced by the construction rather than imposed.

**Counting the faces.** For a star in general position — no three generators coplanar — each unordered pair $\{v_i,v_j\}$ contributes one pair of opposite faces, so

$$ F = n(n-1), \qquad V = n^2 - n + 2, \qquad E = 2n(n-1), $$

the last two following from the first by Euler's formula $V-E+F=2$. The 24-zone truncated cuboctahedron lands on $F = 24\cdot 23 = 552$ and $V = 24^2 - 24 + 2 = 554$; the icosahedron's six zones give $F = 6\cdot 5 = 30$ and $V = 32$ — the rhombic triacontahedron, exactly. The code enumerates these faces directly from the star rather than hulling the $2^n$ subset sums, which is why the 24- and 30-zone models are ordinary to build rather than intractable.

**The dissection.** A three-dimensional zonotope of $n$ generators tiles into $\binom{n}{3}$ parallelepipeds, one for each *triple* $\{v_i,v_j,v_k\}$ — the block spanned by those three vectors (Ziegler). For the rhombic triacontahedron ($n=6$) that is $\binom{6}{3}=20$ blocks: **Kowalewski's twenty golden rhombohedra**, which split ten *acute* and ten *flat* according to whether the triple spans a sharp or a squashed cell. The rhombic enneacontahedron from the dodecahedron's ten zones gives $\binom{10}{3}=120$. **Explode** slides each block outward along its own centroid so the packing comes apart on screen.

**The zonish variant.** A zonish polyhedron is the Minkowski sum of the *whole seed* (not just its directions) with the scaled star, taken as a convex hull. Because the seed's own faces survive, the result keeps odd-sided faces — triangles, pentagons — that a true zonohedron can never have; the zones merely bevel the seed's edges into centrally symmetric strips. **Zone Length** scales the segments, running the solid from the bare seed (short zones) out toward a zonohedron-dominated form (long zones). Colouring by **Zone Pair** then separates the added zone faces (each matched to its pair of generators by its normal) from the seed's own faces, which belong to no pair — precisely the distinction worth seeing on a zonish solid.
