# Spidron Nest

![Spidron Nest](../images/spidron_nest.png)

## Overview

Add a folded spidron nest: a hexagon of triangles crumpled
into relief without distorting any of them.

A **spidron nest** is a regular hexagon filled by six spidron arms — an alternating spiral of ever-shrinking triangles, Dániel Erdély's 1979 construction. Its remarkable property, and the one this generator draws, is that the flat hexagon can be **folded into three dimensions without distorting a single triangle**: the whole family of folded states is a one-parameter rigid motion, hinging only along the creases. Lajos Szilassi gave the first rigorous analysis of that fold; this operator builds one nest at any fold angle, a honeycomb landscape of them, or Imada–Hull–Ku–Tachi's square-ring generalisation.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Spidron Nest*.
2. **Pick the Boundary.** *Hexagon* is the classical nest — six arms folding as a single one-parameter rigid motion. *Square* is Imada, Hull, Ku and Tachi's generalisation on square rings, with its own scale, twist and three folding modes.
3. **Set the Fold.** This is the master knob: the tilt of the outer edges out of the base plane, from $0°$ (flat) up to $60°$, where the surface is as folded as it can be before it starts passing through itself. **Rings** sets how many rings of triangles spiral inward; because the fold decays very slowly, five to nine is the useful range.
4. **Dial the boundary-specific controls.** For *Hexagon*, **Allow Crossing** opts into fold angles past $60°$ (up to about $70.53°$), where the closed forms stay real but the surface self-intersects. For *Square*, **Ring Scale** is how much each ring shrinks, **Twist** how far each ring turns relative to the one outside it (clamped automatically so the creases never cross), and **Mode** chooses *Pro-rotation* (every ring turns the same way), *Anti-rotation* (each against the ring outside it), or *Pleats* (alternating).
5. **Build a landscape and give it body.** For the hexagon, **Columns** and **Rows** repeat the nest by pure translation on the honeycomb lattice — neighbours share their boundary edges exactly at every fold angle. **Cap Centre** closes the small central hole to make the shell watertight, and **Thickness** adds material depth with a Solidify modifier.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the vertex and face counts, and notes when a hexagon fold was clamped to $60°$, when a square twist was clamped to keep the creases apart, or when a square nest has converged to a self-similar state.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Cut a spidron hexagon out of paper — a flat rosette of triangles spiralling to the middle — and score every edge as a crease. Now push the middle and let it buckle. Because every triangle is rigid, it can only tip and turn about its creases, like the folds of a paper fan or a crumpled origami tessellation, and the whole sheet lifts into a gently corrugated dish. There is only *one* way it can move: a single "fold" angle, the tilt of the outer rim, drives every crease at once. Turn that dial and the dish deepens; the triangles themselves never bend or change size. Everything below is the recipe for where each corner goes as you turn the dial, ring by ring toward the centre.

**A ring is a skew hexagon.** Each ring of the fold is described by three numbers — a state triple $(d, \alpha, \beta)$: the edge length $d$, the azimuth $\alpha$ (how far the ring has rotated about the nest's axis), and $\beta$, the inclination of the ring's outer edges to the flat base plane. From them the six outer vertices are placed as

$$V_j = \Big(d\cos\beta\,\cos\theta_j,\;\; d\cos\beta\,\sin\theta_j,\;\; (-1)^j\,\tfrac{d}{2}\sin\beta\Big), \qquad \theta_j = \alpha + \tfrac{\pi}{6} + \tfrac{j\pi}{3}.$$

Read that geometrically. The vertices alternate up and down about the base plane, $+\tfrac{d}{2}\sin\beta$ and $-\tfrac{d}{2}\sin\beta$, so the hexagon is *skew* — bent like a saddle rather than lying flat. Its edge midpoints, though, all stay exactly in the base plane, and seen from above it still projects to a perfectly regular hexagon of side $d\cos\beta$. Crucially every edge keeps length exactly $d$ no matter what $\beta$ is — that length-invariance is precisely what "fold without distortion" means, and it is what makes the motion rigid.

**Marching inward — Szilassi's recursion.** The nest is built one ring at a time by a rule that shrinks the edge, advances the azimuth, and steepens or slackens the fold:

$$d \to \frac{d}{\sqrt3}, \qquad \alpha \to \alpha + a(\beta), \qquad \beta \to b(\beta).$$

The factor $1/\sqrt3$ is the same hexagonal scale ratio as the flat figure. The azimuth advance $a(\beta)$ runs from $30°$ when the nest is flat to $60°$ at the physical fold limit. The heart of it is $b(\beta)$, the **Spidron Formula**, which gives the inner ring's inclination from the outer one's. Writing $c = \cos\beta$, three independently-derived forms all agree (the module carries all three and cross-checks them); the numerically safest is

$$b(\beta) = \arctan\!\frac{\sqrt{\,-1 + 4c - 3c^2\,}}{\sqrt{\,2 - 4c + 3c^2\,}}.$$

**Why the fold has a ceiling.** The number under the top root, $-1 + 4c - 3c^2$, factors as $-(3c-1)(c-1)$, so it stays non-negative — and the formula stays real — exactly when $c = \cos\beta$ lies in $[\tfrac13, 1]$, i.e. for fold angles up to $\arccos(\tfrac13) \approx 70.53°$. The **physical** bound is tighter: at $\beta = 60°$ both triangles of a base figure stand vertical, and past it the surface would pass through itself, so the operator clamps there unless **Allow Crossing** is set.

**Why so many rings barely change the fold.** Near flat the Spidron Formula behaves like

$$b(\beta) = \beta - \tfrac14\beta^3 + O(\beta^5),$$

so its slope at zero is $1$: each ring reduces the fold only by a tiny cubic amount. Iterating $\beta_{k+1} = \beta_k - \tfrac14\beta_k^3$ gives the slow decay $\beta_k \to \sqrt{2/k}$, not a geometric one. In Szilassi's own vivid estimate a nest whose rim measured $10^{23}$ m would still be tilted about six degrees by the time its rings had shrunk to $10^{-16}$ m — which is why five to nine rings is all that is ever worth drawing.

**Tiling and the square generalisation.** Folded nests tile by **pure translation**: on the honeycomb lattice with pitch $\sqrt3\,\cos\beta$ neighbouring nests share their boundary edges exactly, at every fold angle, with no mirroring — giving a periodic folded landscape. The *Square* boundary is Imada, Hull, Ku and Tachi's two-parameter cousin: annular square rings under a similarity of scale $s$ and twist $\theta$. A single square ring turns out to have two folded solutions — the inner boundary can turn *with* the outer one or *against* it — which are the *Pro-rotation* and *Anti-rotation* modes; *Pleats* alternates them ring to ring. At zero twist the pleated case degenerates to the triangulated hypar, which has no exact flat-facet rigid fold (Demaine and others), so what is drawn there is the usual bent-facet approximation.
