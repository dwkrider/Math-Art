# Polyhedron Compound

## Overview

Add a compound of regular polyhedra (each component coloured
separately).

A **polyhedron compound** is several copies of a solid interpenetrating in a symmetric arrangement — the copies share a centre and often their vertices, and the union has a symmetry the individual solid does not. Kepler drew the first, the **stella octangula** (two tetrahedra forming an eight-pointed star), in *Harmonices Mundi* in 1619.

The icosahedral ones are the striking members: **five tetrahedra**, whose union is chiral and whose twenty vertices are exactly a dodecahedron's; **five cubes**; **five octahedra**. Hess and Brückner catalogued them in the nineteenth century, and Skilling completed the enumeration of the uniform compounds in 1976.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Polyhedron Compound*.
2. **Pick the Compound.** The choices are the stella octangula (2 tetrahedra), the compounds of 5 and 10 tetrahedra, 5 cubes and 5 octahedra, and the two dual pairs — cube + octahedron and dodecahedron + icosahedron. Each is a fixed symmetric arrangement, so there is nothing to tune; the entry *is* the shape.
3. **Turn on Separate Objects** if you want each interpenetrating component as its own mesh object rather than one merged, multi-coloured mesh — useful for exporting or shading the copies independently. Left off, the whole compound is a single object whose components still keep distinct colours (each copy needs its own colour, or the union just reads as one tangled polyhedron).
4. **Set Scale** to size the result; the compound is normalised to fit the standard 2 m cube first, then multiplied by this factor.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Take one solid and make several identical copies of it, all sharing the same centre point but each spun to a different angle, then let them pass through one another. The overlapping copies lock into a single starry shape that is more symmetric than any one copy alone — the classic example is two tetrahedra rotated into an eight-pointed star, Kepler's *stella octangula*. The clever part is choosing the *angles*: pick them at random and you get a mess, but pick them as the rotations of a highly symmetric solid and the copies mesh perfectly, often with their corners landing on the corners of some larger solid. Everything here comes from that one idea — a seed shape plus a set of rotations to spin it by.

**A compound is an orbit.** Formally, take a seed solid $S$ and a rotation group $G$, and form

$$C=\bigcup_{g\in G/H} g(S),$$

where $H$ is the subgroup of rotations that already map $S$ to itself. Only the *distinct* rotations matter — spinning the seed by one of its own symmetries produces the same copy — so the copies are counted by the **quotient** $G/H$, not by $G$ itself. That quotient is what makes the numbers come out clean: the five-tetrahedra compound takes the icosahedral rotation group of order 60 against a tetrahedron whose own rotation subgroup has order 12, giving $60/12 = 5$ copies exactly. Nothing is placed by hand — fixing the seed and the group determines every copy. The **ten-tetrahedra** compound is the natural next step: it is the five-tetrahedra orbit together with its mirror image, so both left- and right-handed fives coexist and the result regains the mirror symmetry each chiral five lacks.

**Why the frame matters.** The rotation groups are generated in the **standard cube / dodecahedron frame**, so an axis-aligned seed lands where it should. This is the quiet step that decides whether a compound is beautiful or merely overlapping: the five cubes interlock because each cube's four body diagonals line up with five of the dodecahedron's axis directions, and that alignment only happens if the seed *and* the group are written in the same coordinate frame. Rotate the seed by some arbitrary angle first and the copies still form a symmetric spray, but their vertices no longer coincide, and the special "corners-on-corners" magic is lost.

**Dual pairs.** Cube + octahedron and dodecahedron + icosahedron are a different construction — not an orbit of one seed but a solid placed together with its **dual**, scaled so the dual's vertices sit directly over the original's face centres. Their intersection is then the corresponding *rectified* solid — the cuboctahedron and the icosidodecahedron — and their convex hull is the dual's dual, so the pair reads as a single crisply stellated form rather than as two solids awkwardly sharing space. This is why a cube-octahedron compound looks like one faceted gem instead of a cube with an octahedron poking through it.

Each component keeps its **own colour**, because once the copies interpenetrate there is otherwise no visual way to tell which face belongs to which solid — with a shared colour the compound collapses into one complicated-looking polyhedron instead of reading as the several simple ones it really is.

## References

- Stella octangula: Johannes Kepler, "Harmonices Mundi" (1619).
- The regular compounds (5 tetrahedra, 5 cubes, ...): Edmund Hess (1876); Max Bruckner (1900); catalogued in H. S. M. Coxeter, "Regular Polytopes" (1948).
- Full enumeration of uniform compounds: John Skilling, "Uniform compounds of uniform polyhedra", Math. Proc. Camb. Phil. Soc. 79 (1976).
