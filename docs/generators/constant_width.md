# Constant Width Solid

## Overview

Add a surface of constant width -- a Reuleaux solid of revolution, the Reuleaux tetrahedron, or a Meissner tetrahedron.

A body of **constant width** measures the same across in every direction, exactly as a sphere does — so it rolls smoothly under a flat board, holding it at a constant height, without being round. The Reuleaux triangle is the plane version; these are its solid counterparts.

The **Meissner tetrahedron** is the celebrated one. Take a Reuleaux tetrahedron — the intersection of four balls centred at a regular tetrahedron's vertices — and it is *not* quite of constant width; three of its curved edges must be replaced by spindle-shaped patches to make it so. Meissner and Schilling built the models in 1912, and the resulting bodies are conjectured to have the **smallest volume** of any solid of constant width, a conjecture still open after more than a century.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Rollers ▸ Constant Width Solid*.
2. **Pick the Shape.** **Meissner Tetrahedron** is the true constant-width body — a Reuleaux tetrahedron with the three edges meeting at one vertex rounded into spindle patches. **Reuleaux Solid of Revolution** spins a Reuleaux polygon about an axis of symmetry and is genuinely of constant width. **Reuleaux Tetrahedron** is the four-ball intersection on its own: it is the natural first guess and the cautionary tale, because it is *not* quite of constant width.
3. **Set the Width** — the constant across-measure, i.e. the height at which the body holds a flat board as it rolls. All three shapes carry the value you set.
4. **Dial the mode-specific controls.** These appear only for the relevant shape: **Reuleaux Solid of Revolution** exposes **Polygon Sides** (the number of sides of the profile polygon, forced odd) and **Segments** (revolution resolution); the two tetrahedral shapes instead expose **Smoothness**, the icosphere subdivision of the four Boolean balls — the real accuracy-versus-cost knob, since the sharp fold curves are only as clean as that subdivision.
5. **Output.** You get one watertight mesh, centred at the origin. Its sharp fold edges are marked as creases by default, so the arcs meet in crisp ridges rather than being rounded off by smooth shading.
6. **Read the result.** The revolution and Meissner bodies carry the requested width *exactly*; the plain Reuleaux tetrahedron is deliberately about 2.5% too wide, and seeing that excess is the reason it is offered. The operator's report prints the vertex and face totals (`V=… F=…`).

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A shape has *constant width* if, no matter which way you turn it, the distance from one side to the other is always the same — so you could roll it under a flat board and the board would never rise or fall, even though the shape is not a ball. The flat poster child is the **Reuleaux triangle**: take an equilateral triangle and bulge each side into an arc drawn from the opposite corner, and you get a plump, guitar-pick shape that fits between two parallel rulers the same way in every orientation. You might hope that four spheres arranged on a triangular pyramid would do the same trick in 3-D. They *almost* do — and the small amount by which they fail, together with Meissner's clever fix for it, is the whole story below.

**What constant width means, exactly.** The right tool is the **support function** $h(u)$ of a convex body: the distance from the origin to the supporting plane whose outward normal is $u$. The width across the body in direction $u$ — the gap between the two supporting planes perpendicular to $u$ — is then $h(u) + h(-u)$, and constant width $w$ is the condition

$$h(u) + h(-u) = w \quad\text{for every direction } u.$$

The insight hiding in that formula is that it constrains **opposite pairs** of directions, not each direction on its own. Roundness ($h \equiv w/2$) is only one way to satisfy it; the freedom to let $h(u)$ and $h(-u)$ trade off against each other, so long as they sum to $w$, is exactly what leaves room for non-spherical solutions.

**Reuleaux polygons and their revolution.** In the plane, a **Reuleaux polygon** is built on a regular $n$-gon with **odd** $n$: each side is replaced by a circular arc of radius $w$ centred on the *opposite* vertex. Odd $n$ is what makes this possible — it guarantees that every vertex faces an edge across the polygon, so each direction pairs a sharp corner on one side with a smooth arc on the other and the two contributions to $h(u) + h(-u)$ add to a constant. Spinning such a polygon about one of its axes of symmetry gives the **Reuleaux solid of revolution**; because the profile has constant width and the axis lies in its plane, the property survives the sweep, and the $n = 3$ case has the least volume of any known constant-width surface of revolution.

**Why the Reuleaux tetrahedron falls short.** The obvious spatial analogue is to intersect four balls of radius $w$ centred at the vertices of a regular tetrahedron of edge $w$. Along each of the six curved edges the surface is a sharp arc, and measured between the midpoints of two *opposite* such edges the body is too wide — its width there reaches

$$w\left(\sqrt{3} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) \approx 1.025\,w,$$

about 2.5% over $w$. Geometrically, the two opposite edges bulge toward each other more than a pair of flat-and-arc directions would, so the neat plane cancellation no longer closes up. The naive lift of a 2-D construction into 3-D simply does not preserve constant width — which is precisely what makes the tetrahedral case worth including.

**Meissner's repair.** The excess lives on the edges, so the fix is to reshape edges. Replace **three** of the six curved edges — either the three meeting at one vertex, or three that form a triangle — with spindle-shaped patches, each the surface of revolution of a radius-$w$ arc about the line joining that edge's two end vertices. Sweeping those patches shaves off exactly the surplus, restoring $h(u) + h(-u) = w$ everywhere. Only three edges are altered, and which three distinguishes the two Meissner bodies; the choice changes the shape but not the width. This generator builds the **vertex** variant — the three edges meeting at a single vertex.

**Construction here.** The tetrahedral shapes are built by exact Boolean geometry rather than by direct sampling, because a single lat–long mesh tears along the patch-boundary seams. Four icospheres are intersected (the **Smoothness** control is their subdivision level — a genuine cost, since the accuracy of the sharp fold curves rides on it); for the Meissner body three wedge cuts pare the chosen edges back and three revolved spindles fill them in. The revolution shape is meshed directly by sweeping its Reuleaux polygon. Finally the fold edges are marked as creases, because a Meissner body genuinely *has* sharp ridges where the arcs meet, and shading smoothly across them would round off the one feature that defines the shape.
