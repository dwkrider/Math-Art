# Star Prism / Antiprism

## Overview

Add a uniform {p/q} star prism, antiprism, dipyramid or
trapezohedron (Step = 1 gives the ordinary convex form).

The infinite families of the uniform polyhedra, built on a **star polygon** base instead of a convex one. Where an ordinary prism caps a pentagon at each end, a star prism caps a pentagram — and the same substitution gives star antiprisms, star dipyramids and star trapezohedra.

These matter because the 75 uniform polyhedra are only the *finite* part of the classification. The prismatic families run on forever, one for each admissible $\{p/q\}$, and they are uniform by exactly the same standard: regular faces, one vertex orbit.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Star Prism / Antiprism*.
2. **Pick the Form** — the family: **Prism** (two star bases joined by squares), **Antiprism** (bases joined by a band of triangles), **Dipyramid** (the prism's dual, a bipyramid over a star), or **Trapezohedron** (the antiprism's dual, a zig-zag of kites, including concave star forms).
3. **Set the star $\{p/q\}$** with the two integer knobs. **Sides (p)** (3–32) is how many points sit on the circle; **Step (q)** (1–15) is the *density* — how many points you skip between corners. $q=2$ on $p=5$ is the pentagram $\{5/2\}$; **$q=1$ recovers the ordinary convex polygon**, so a plain pentagonal prism is just the $\{5/1\}$ case, not a separate tool.
4. **Colouring and Scale.** **Coloring** *By Face Size* tints each face class (the star caps versus the lateral band) from a shared palette; *None* leaves it plain. **Scale** sizes the result, which is otherwise centred and fit to a 2 m cube.
5. **Watch for the coerced-step notice.** $q$ must be coprime to $p$ and less than $p/2$ (otherwise $\{p/q\}$ is not a single star — $\{6/2\}$ is two triangles). If you dial an invalid $q$, the operator silently nudges it to the nearest valid value and prints a status message like `{6/2} is not a star polygon; built {6/1}` so you know what was actually made.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Draw a five-pointed star in one stroke without lifting your pen: you put five dots evenly on a circle and, instead of joining each dot to its neighbour, you jump *two* dots ahead every time. That jump is the whole trick — a "star polygon" is nothing but "$p$ dots on a circle, skip $q$ each step." Now take that star, make a second copy directly above it, and connect the two: joining corresponding corners with vertical rectangles gives a *star prism*; giving the top a half-turn and connecting with triangles gives a *star antiprism*. The pointy duals — where the star becomes a set of spikes meeting at two poles — are the *dipyramid* and *trapezohedron*. Everything below is that picture made exact: where to put the corners, how big the star is, and how the corners join into faces.

**The star polygon $\{p/q\}$** visits $p$ points on a circle, stepping $q$ each time (the corner after vertex $k$ is vertex $(k+q)\bmod p$). It closes into a *single* circuit exactly when $\gcd(p,q)=1$; otherwise it degenerates into $\gcd(p,q)$ separate polygons — the hexagram $\{6/2\}$ is two overlaid triangles, not one figure you can trace in a stroke. The step is therefore coerced to a valid value (folded into $1\le q<p/2$ and nudged to the nearest coprime, falling back to $q=1$), and since $q=1$ recovers the ordinary convex polygon, a plain prism is just the $q=1$ case rather than a separate code path.

**Sizing.** Place the $p$ corners at angles $2\pi k/p$. For unit edge length — where "edge" means the *star* edge, the chord that actually gets drawn — the circumradius is

$$R=\frac{1}{2\sin(q\pi/p)},$$

the $q$ inside the sine being exactly what distinguishes a pentagram from a pentagon: the drawn chord subtends $q$ steps of the circle, an angle $2q\pi/p$, and half of that sits in the right triangle giving $R$. As $q$ approaches $p/2$ the sine approaches $1$, $R$ approaches its minimum, and the star is as sharp — as deeply notched — as it can be. The two bases are then stacked at $z=\pm\tfrac12$ (prism) so that lateral edge and base edge match.

**The four families.** Two bases at $z=\pm\tfrac12$ joined by a band:

| Family | Side faces | $V$ | $E$ | $F$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Star prism | $p$ squares | $2p$ | $3p$ | $p+2$ |
| Star antiprism | $2p$ triangles | $2p$ | $4p$ | $2p+2$ |
| Star dipyramid | — (two apexes) | $p+2$ | $3p$ | $2p$ |
| Star trapezohedron | $2p$ kites | $2p+2$ | $4p$ | $2p$ |

The prism's lateral faces are the $p$ squares hung on the star edges $k\to(k+q)$; the two star caps are the faces $[(qk)\bmod p]$ that trace the polygon itself. For the **antiprism** the top is given a half-step twist and the band becomes $2p$ triangles, with the height chosen so each lateral edge equals a base edge (the genuine *uniform* condition) whenever that height is real — and a valid crossed form substituted when it is not. The **dipyramid** and **trapezohedron** are then built as the **duals** of the prism and antiprism: the dipyramid puts the star on the equator and an apex at each pole ($2p$ triangles), and the trapezohedron is the exact *polar reciprocal* of the star antiprism — every antiprism face plane becomes a vertex — giving a zig-zag equator of $2p$ kites, concave stars included. Duality swaps vertices and faces, which is precisely why the dipyramid's and trapezohedron's $V$ and $F$ are the prism's and antiprism's read in reverse. All four counts satisfy $V-E+F=2$, and the self-test checks them against these formulas for several $\{p/q\}$ rather than trusting the construction.

**Density.** Each face is stored with its winding **density** alongside its vertex indices, so a $\{7/3\}$ base renders as the three-times-winding star it is rather than as a filled heptagon — the same requirement as in the [uniform polyhedra](uniform_polyhedron.md).
