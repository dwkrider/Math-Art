# Hopf Fibration

![Hopf Fibration](../images/hopf_fibration.png)

## Overview

Add fibres of the Hopf fibration of S^3, stereographically
projected to R^3 as circles and coloured by base point.

The **Hopf fibration** is the map $h: S^3 \to S^2$ whose fibres are great circles of the 3-sphere, and it is one of the most consequential objects in topology — Heinz Hopf's 1931 discovery that a sphere can be fibred by circles in a way that cannot be untangled, which founded homotopy theory's interest in higher spheres.

Its geometry is startlingly concrete: **any two distinct fibres are linked exactly once**, forming a Hopf link. Project the whole thing stereographically into $\mathbb{R}^3$ and every fibre becomes an ordinary circle — a Villarceau circle — except the one through the projection pole, which straightens into the central axis. Fibres over a circle of latitude fill a torus, and sweeping the latitude fills space with nested, pairwise linked tori.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Knots & Curves ▸ Hopf Fibration*.
2. **Choose the Base Points** preset — this decides *which* points of $S^2$ get a fibre drawn, and so the whole composition. **Latitudes** is the classic nested-tori picture (circles of latitude); **Flower** takes one small circle, whose fibres interleave into a linked bundle; **Greatcircle** is a tilted band whose two extreme fibres bound a Hopf band; **Fibonacci** scatters points quasi-uniformly over the sphere; and **Tetra / Octa / Cube / Icosa / Dodeca** put a fibre through each vertex of that Platonic solid.
3. **Set the counts.** For *Latitudes*, **Latitudes / Rings** is the number of latitude circles and **Fibres** is how many fibres ride each one; for *Flower / Greatcircle / Fibonacci*, **Fibres** is the total fibre count (and for *Fibonacci* the point budget). **Samples** is the number of points drawn around each fibre.
4. **Mode-specific controls.** **Colat Min / Colat Max** bound the colatitude band the base points span (shown for *Latitudes / Flower / Greatcircle*). **Wind P / Wind Q** default to $(1,1)$, the genuine Hopf circles; set them otherwise to lay a $(P,Q)$ torus knot or link on each fibre's Clifford torus instead of a plain circle.
5. **Pick the output.** *Bezier / Poly / NURBS* curve, or **Mesh Tube** (the default); **Tube Radius**, **Tube Sides** and **Bevel Resolution** set the thickness and roundness. **Colour by Base Point** gives every fibre its own material — hue from the base point's longitude, brightness from its latitude — so neighbouring fibres share a colour.
6. **Read the report.** It states how many fibres were built and the samples each, and — when the winding is not $(1,1)$ — the $(P,Q)$ that was laid on them. Fibres whose base point strays too near the projection pole are dropped automatically (they would project to near-infinite lines), so the reported count can be a touch below what you asked for.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture the ordinary sphere — the surface of a globe — and imagine that floating above *each single point* of it is a whole circle, a loop all its own. The Hopf fibration is nothing more than the exact rule that says which loop belongs to which point. What makes it famous is how the loops sit together: take any two of them and they are **linked**, hooked through each other like two rings of a keychain you cannot separate without cutting one open. Stack up all the loops at once and they fill space seamlessly — no gaps, no crossings — so that space is *combed* entirely by interlocked circles. The "3-sphere" $S^3$ that appears below is just the four-dimensional analogue of a ball's skin; it is the natural home where all these circles live, roomy enough that they never collide, before we flatten the whole arrangement down into ordinary 3-D space to draw it. Everything that follows turns that mental picture into the formulas the code actually evaluates.

**The map.** A point of the 3-sphere is most cleanly written using complex numbers: take a pair $(z_1,z_2)\in\mathbb{C}^2$ with $|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1$ — four real coordinates summing in squares to one, which is exactly $S^3$. The Hopf map sends that point down to the ordinary sphere by

$$h(z_1,z_2)=\big(2z_1\bar z_2,\ |z_1|^2-|z_2|^2\big)\in S^2,$$

a formula whose right-hand side is a point of $S^2$ (one can check its three components' squares sum to $(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)^2=1$). Now ask which points get sent to the *same* place. Multiply both coordinates by a common unit complex number, $(e^{i\theta}z_1,\,e^{i\theta}z_2)$: the phase $e^{i\theta}$ cancels in $z_1\bar z_2$ and in each $|z_k|^2$, so $h$ is unchanged. As $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $2\pi$ that common phase traces a circle, and that circle is the **fibre** — the whole loop lying over one base point. This is the everyday picture made exact: the fibres are precisely the orbits of the circle acting by simultaneous rotation of both complex coordinates. Because the action is free (no point is fixed) and fills $S^3$, the fibres are honest great circles and they **partition** $S^3$ with none left over and none overlapping.

**Why any two fibres link once.** Two distinct base points give two disjoint great circles in $S^3$, and the question is how they are threaded through one another. Their **linking number** — how many times one loop passes through the disc spanned by the other — is the degree of an associated map, and it comes out to exactly $1$ for *every* pair. That single integer is the **Hopf invariant** of the fibration. Its being non-zero is the whole point of Hopf's 1931 discovery: it certifies that the map $S^3\to S^2$ **cannot be deformed to a constant map**, so $S^3$ is genuinely *twisted* over $S^2$ and is not the trivial product $S^2\times S^1$. Geometrically, non-trivial linking is the obstruction to untangling the loops; homotopically, it was the first non-trivial higher homotopy group ($\pi_3(S^2)=\mathbb{Z}$) ever found, and it founded the subject's interest in the higher spheres.

**Projecting to $\mathbb{R}^3$.** The fibres live in four dimensions, so to draw them we need to bring them down to three. Stereographic projection

$$\sigma(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=\frac{(x_1,x_2,x_3)}{1-x_4}$$

does this, and it has the property we need: it is **conformal** and it carries circles to circles. So each fibre — a circle in $S^3$ — lands as a genuine round circle in space, and these are exactly the **Villarceau circles** that a torus of revolution secretly carries through each of its points. The one exception is the fibre that happens to pass through the projection pole $N=(0,0,0,1)$: since $\sigma$ sends $N$ off to infinity, that lone fibre straightens into an infinite line — the central axis of the picture. (This is why the generator applies a small fixed tilt to every base point, keeping fibres off the pole so nothing actually blows up.)

**Nested tori.** Fix one circle of latitude on $S^2$ and collect all the fibres over it: together they sweep out a single **torus of revolution** in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The equator gives the central, "middle" torus; latitudes marching toward one pole give tori that nest *inside* it, and toward the other pole tori that swell *outside* it, while the two poles themselves degenerate to the central axis and the unit circle threading it. So sweeping the latitude from pole to pole fills **all** of $\mathbb{R}^3$ with disjoint tori, every pair of them linked — this is the canonical, luminous picture of the fibration, and choosing *Latitudes* in the operator draws exactly a finite slice of it.

**Hopf tori.** See [Hopf Torus](hopf_torus.md) for the surface swept by the fibres over an arbitrary *closed curve* on $S^2$ rather than over a circle of latitude — the same construction with the base loop set free.
