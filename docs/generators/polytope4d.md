# 4D Polytopes

![4D Polytopes](../images/polytope4d.png)

## Overview

The six **regular convex 4-polytopes** — the 5-cell, tesseract (8-cell), 16-cell, 24-cell, 600-cell and 120-cell — are the four-dimensional cousins of the Platonic solids. This generator renders them in 3D as strut-and-sphere edge frameworks projected down from 4-space, with edges either **straight** (a perspective projection that approaches a Schlegel diagram at close range) or **curved** (vertices on the unit 3-sphere, edges drawn as great-circle arcs and mapped by exact stereographic projection, so every edge becomes a circular arc). Sliders spin the polytope in 4D, and extras add a Leonardo open-panel style, equatorial half-cutaways, primal+dual compounds, and the 120-cell's twelve Hopf-fibration rings of dodecahedral cells.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ 4D Polytope*.
2. **Pick the Polytope.** The **Tesseract (8-cell)** is the default; the **120-cell** (600 vertices, 1200 edges) and **600-cell** (120/720) are the two largest.
3. **Pick the Edges.** **Curved (stereographic)** is the classic round-arc look; **Straight (perspective)** draws line segments and exposes **Projection Distance** — near 1 it pulls the near cell forward into a Schlegel diagram (for the 5/16/600-cell a vertex sits at the pole $w=1$, so nudge a rotation slider or back the distance off).
4. **Turn it in 4D.** **Rotate XW / YW / ZW / XY** spin the polytope in the four coordinate planes before projection — the XW/YW/ZW rotations bring different cells to the front, while XY is an ordinary in-view spin. This is the main way to explore the structure.
5. **Choose the Style.** **Struts** (square-section beams) and **Ball and Stick** (round struts plus vertex spheres) both follow the curved arcs; **Wireframe** is a bare polyline; **Leonardo** builds a flat open panel per 2D face. **Taper With Projection** fattens near-pole features, and **Arc Segments** sets how finely curved edges are sampled.
6. **Add cutaways, compounds and rings.** **Half (Cutaway)** keeps only one side of the equator (Segerman's legible half-models); **Dual Compound** overlays the dual polytope (5↔5, 8↔16, 24↔24, 120↔600) in a second material; and for the **120-cell only**, **Hopf Rings** renders $N$ of the 12 rings of ten dodecahedral cells that partition it along the Hopf fibration (with **Ring Cell Scale** shrinking each cell and **Rings Only** dropping the edge framework).
7. **Read the report.** The build prints the framework's vertex and edge counts, noting "(half)" for a cutaway and appending the dual's counts and the ring/cell totals when those are on.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A four-dimensional polytope has one more direction than we can see, so to picture it we do what a map does to the globe: cast a shadow. Imagine the tesseract's corners glowing on the surface of a 4D "sphere," put a lamp just outside it, and catch the shadow in our ordinary 3D space. Corners near the lamp throw big shadows and corners far from it throw small ones, which is why these pictures have one roomy cell in front and nested cells shrinking toward the middle — it is the *same* cell, near and far, not different-sized ones. If the lamp sits right *on* the sphere, the straight edges bend into perfect circular arcs, giving the familiar "round" tesseract. Turning the object in 4D before casting the shadow (the four rotation sliders) brings different cells to the front. Everything below is the exact bookkeeping behind that shadow.

**Vertices.** Each polytope is built from its standard coordinate set — the tesseract as the sixteen sign choices of $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$; the 16-cell as all sign changes of $(1,0,0,0)$; the 24-cell from $(\pm1,\pm1,0,0)$; the 600-cell and 120-cell from sign/permutation orbits involving the golden ratio $\varphi$ (the 600-cell, for instance, includes the even permutations of $(\varphi/2, \tfrac12, 1/(2\varphi), 0)$). All vertices are then normalized to unit length, which places them on the **unit 3-sphere** — the 4D analogue of the ball's surface — where the curved-mode geometry lives. **Edges** are read off automatically as the vertex pairs at the *minimal* nonzero distance: in a regular polytope every edge is the same length, so the shortest gap between any two vertices is an edge, and every pair realizing it is one. **2D faces** (needed only for the Leonardo style) are found by walking edge cycles that stay within a common 2-flat of $\mathbb R^4$ — an ordinary flat plane sitting inside four-space. The vertex and edge counts are checked against the known values (120-cell: 600 vertices, 1200 edges; 600-cell: 120/720), a built-in sanity test that the coordinate sets came out right.

**4D rotation.** Before projecting, every point set is turned by one common 4D rotation. In four dimensions a rotation does not fix an *axis* the way it does in 3D — it fixes a whole *plane* and turns the two directions perpendicular to it — so the rotation is assembled from independent turns in the XW, YW, ZW and XY planes. A rotation in the plane $(a,b)$ acts as

$$v_a \mapsto v_a\cos t - v_b\sin t,\qquad v_b \mapsto v_a\sin t + v_b\cos t,$$

leaving the other two coordinates alone. The three planes touching the fourth axis $w$ (XW, YW, ZW) are the interesting ones: they tip the polytope through the fourth direction and so swap which cell faces the "lamp," while XY is a plain spin within our view.

**Projection.** A point $\mathbf v = (x,y,z,w)$ is dropped into $\mathbb R^3$ by central projection from the eye at $(0,0,0,d)$ on the $w$-axis:

$$\pi(\mathbf v) = \frac{1}{d - w}\,(x, y, z),\qquad \text{local scale } s = \frac{1}{d-w}.$$

Dividing by $d-w$ is exactly the perspective foreshortening of the shadow picture: a vertex with large $w$ (close to the eye) divides by a small number and looms large, one with small $w$ shrinks. The scale $s$ is recorded per point because the strut thickness and panel offset later reuse it.

- **Straight** edges use eye distance $d = \max(\text{proj\_dist}, 1.001)$ and draw each edge as a plain line segment; small $d$ puts the eye just outside the polytope and pulls the near cell forward into a **Schlegel diagram** — the whole polytope flattened into one of its own cells.
- **Curved** edges set $d = 1$, which — for $\mathbf v$ on the unit 3-sphere — is precisely the **stereographic projection** from the pole $(0,0,0,1)$. Each edge is sampled along the great-circle arc between its endpoints by 4D spherical interpolation (slerp), so the tube traces the shortest path *on the sphere* rather than a straight chord, and every projected sample is threaded into one tube. Because stereographic projection is conformal and carries circles to circles, each great-circle edge lands as a genuine **circular arc** in $\mathbb R^3$, and each 2D face's circumscribing circle maps to a circle — so the projected face stays flat, which is exactly what lets the Leonardo panels be planar in both edge modes. One catch: a vertex sitting at the pole ($w \approx 1$) would project to infinity. To avoid that, a small deterministic extra 4D rotation is applied first to nudge every vertex off the pole, and the *same* rotation is reused on every auxiliary point set so compounds and rings stay registered with the main framework.

**Struts and spheres.** Each projected edge is turned into a closed tube swept along its polyline with a **parallel-transport frame** — the cross-section is carried from one sample to the next without spinning, so a curved arc gets a clean untwisted tube instead of a rippled one. If *taper* is on, each sample's radius is multiplied by its local projection factor $s$, so a strut swells where it comes near the pole and thins where it recedes, echoing the foreshortening of the vertices themselves. In Ball and Stick, vertices are additionally capped with small spheres of radius `radius × sphere_factor × s`.

**Leonardo panels.** In the Leonardo style each projected 2D face becomes a flat open frame: the face is inset by `Border`, a hole is cut through it, and a solid shell is extruded to give the panel thickness. The delicate part is the joints. Every polytope vertex is offset inward just once, along a single mitre direction $\mathbf m$ solving $\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf n_f = \text{thickness}$ in the least-squares sense over all the panels $f$ meeting at that vertex — the same condition a mitred picture-frame corner satisfies. Because the offset is shared, adjacent panels reuse the *same* inner-boundary vertices, so the frames meet exactly along edges and at corners with no cracks or overlaps.

**Cutaway, compound and rings.**

- **Half** keeps only the vertices with $w \le 0$ (the equator included) and the edges/faces they fully span, applied in the polytope's own coordinates *before* any rotation — so the cut is always the clean equatorial slice of Segerman's legible half-models, wherever you later spin the result.
- **Dual compound** adds the dual polytope, whose vertices sit at the centres of the primal's cells (5↔5, 8↔16, 24↔24, 120↔600). In curved mode both frameworks already lie on the 3-sphere together; in straight mode the dual is scaled down to the primal's cell inradius so each dual vertex lands on the corresponding cell centre.
- **Hopf rings** (120-cell only) exploit a beautiful coincidence: the 120 cell centres of the 120-cell *are* the 120 vertices of the 600-cell — the unit **icosians**, which form the binary icosahedral group $2I$ under quaternion multiplication. The left cosets of a cyclic order-10 subgroup partition those 120 elements into 12 rings of 10 cells, and each ring traces a fiber of the **Hopf fibration** $S^3 \to S^2$ — the way the 3-sphere threads itself with linked circles. Each ring's dodecahedral cells (the 20 polytope vertices nearest the centre, shrunk toward their 4D centroid so gaps make the rings legible) are projected and hulled into solid cells, one material per ring. Two rings give the classic pair of interlocked orthogonal rings; *Rings Only* drops the 1200-strut framework for the printable presentation.
