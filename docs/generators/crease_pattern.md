# Crease Pattern

![Crease Pattern](../images/crease_pattern.png)

## Overview

Crease Pattern builds one of nine classical origami tessellations — Miura-ori,
the accordion pleat, the waterbomb (magic-ball) tessellation, the Yoshimura
diamond buckle, the pleated hypar and monkey saddle, both Kresling stackings,
and the Ron Resch pattern — as a flat sheet of mountain and valley folds. Unless
you ask otherwise it then folds that sheet into three dimensions and caches the
whole motion as an animation you can scrub. These are folk mathematics: no one
author to credit, nothing to download, and without them the add-on could import
crease patterns from anywhere yet produce none of its own.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Origami ▸ Crease Pattern. The nine patterns
   are shown, each with a folded thumbnail, in the gallery below.
2. **Pick the Pattern.** This is the main dial: the whole family is one operator
   with one selector rather than nine menu entries, the same way the CMC
   surfaces and the Hopf/Willmore tori are each a single entry.
3. **Set the grid.** Rows and Columns choose how many panels span the sheet each
   way, and Sheet Size scales the flat sheet. For Miura-ori, Panel Angle sets the
   acute angle of the parallelogram — the one knob that distinguishes a tight
   zig-zag from a shallow one.
4. **Fold, or stay flat.** Leave Fold on to fold the pattern the moment it is
   built; turn it off to keep the flat crease pattern, its mountains and valleys
   marked, for export or inspection. (The separate Fold Pattern operator folds a
   flat pattern you kept.)
5. **Choose the Solver.** *Rigid Panels* folds with flat, undeformed panels —
   correct for Miura-ori and the other rigid-foldable patterns. *Bending Paper*
   lets the sheet curve between its creases, which the pleated hypar and several
   others *require*; Fold Amount then sets how hard the folds are driven.
6. **Animation.** With Animate on, the fold path is solved once and stored as
   shape keys strung along a single Fold slider — Steps sets how many states, and
   so the animation's resolution. Scrub or keyframe Fold and the model folds;
   nothing re-solves at render time.
7. **Read the checks.** With Report Checks on, the operator tests every interior
   vertex against the two flat-foldability conditions (Maekawa and Kawasaki) and
   lists the vertices that fail. It reports and never repairs — the mountain and
   valley assignment is your design, so the counts below a folded hypar are
   honest facts about the pattern, not errors.

### Importing crease patterns

The companion **Import Crease Pattern** entry reads patterns designed elsewhere
— FOLD (`.fold`), SVG (`.svg`) and ORIPA (`.cp`) files — and folds them with the
same two solvers, so an imported design behaves exactly like a built-in one.

## How it works

A crease pattern is a flat sheet of paper with lines drawn on it, each line
marked as a **mountain** (it folds away from you) or a **valley** (it folds
toward you). The interesting question is whether all those creases can snap shut
at the same time and lie flat, or whether the paper fights itself. Around any
interior point where several creases meet, two little counting rules settle it.
First, count the mountains and the valleys through that point: they must differ
by exactly two. Second, measure the wedge angles of paper between consecutive
creases and add up every other one — going around, the odd wedges and the even
wedges must each come to a straight angle. Satisfy both rules and that
neighbourhood can fold flat; break either and it never can, no matter how you
push. The generator draws one of nine classic patterns that repeat such vertices
across the sheet, checks each vertex against these rules, and then actually folds
the paper for you.

**The three local conditions.** At an interior vertex with sector angles
$\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{2n}$ going around, three statements matter. The first
is *developability*,

$$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \cdots + \alpha_{2n} = 2\pi,$$

which is not a folding condition at all — it just says the drawing really is
flat paper (it fails only for a file edited in 3-D). The second is *Maekawa's
theorem*, $|M - V| = 2$, relating the numbers of mountains and valleys; an
immediate consequence is that the degree must be even, so an odd-degree vertex
can never fold flat. The third is the *Kawasaki–Justin condition*, that the
alternating sum of sector angles vanishes:

$$\alpha_1 - \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 - \cdots - \alpha_{2n} = 0.$$

Geometrically this is the statement that the alternate wedges balance — each
group folds up to the same half-turn — so the paper returns to its own plane
after one trip around the vertex. For a *single* vertex, Maekawa and Kawasaki
together are both necessary and sufficient. For a pattern with *many* vertices
they are only necessary: every vertex can pass and the sheet still fail globally
because its layers cannot be stacked without passing through each other, and
deciding that is NP-hard. So the operator reports the local conditions, reads a
layer order when a file provides one, and is honest that global flat-foldability
is left open.

**Folding with rigid panels.** For a rigid-foldable pattern like Miura-ori the
panels never bend — they turn about the creases like plates on hinges. The clean
way to model that is to make the unknowns the **fold angles** $\rho$, one per
interior crease, rather than the vertex positions. Angles keep the panels rigid
automatically (a panel that never appears in the unknowns can never stretch) and
shrink the problem from $3V$ position unknowns to $E$ angle unknowns. A valid
rigid folding is then exactly an assignment of fold angles for which the paper
*closes up* around every interior vertex: walk the faces in a loop around the
vertex, rotating by $\rho$ as you step across each crease, and the product of
those rotations must be the identity,

$$R_1(\rho_1)\, R_2(\rho_2) \cdots R_m(\rho_m) = I.$$

Because every crease at the vertex passes through it, the translations cancel and
the loop product is a pure rotation, so its failure to close is captured by three
numbers — the axis-angle of its skew part. Driving that residual to zero with
Newton's method, from the flat state outward by continuation, folds the sheet;
the Jacobian is available in closed form, which is where the speed comes from.
(One subtlety the code is careful about: the residual walk and the placement walk
must step across a crease with the *same* primitive, or a configuration can
satisfy the constraint to machine precision and still tear the sheet when its
vertices are actually placed.)

**Folding paper that bends.** Rigid panels are the wrong model for most of the
visually striking patterns, and provably so: the pleated hypar has *no* folding
with flat facets at all (Demaine et al., 2011) — it exists only because the paper
curves between its creases. For those, the Bending Paper solver uses the
Ghassaei–Demaine–Gershenfeld model behind Origami Simulator. The sheet is
triangulated into a pin-jointed truss carrying three kinds of linear-elastic
constraint: an **axial** spring on every edge resisting length change
($k_{\mathrm{axial}} = EA / l_0$), a **crease** spring on every interior edge
pulling its two faces toward a target fold angle ($k = l_0\, k_{\mathrm{fold}}$),
and a **face** spring at every triangle corner resisting shear. The truss is
marched forward with a small, semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler step — advancing
the position with the *new* velocity, which stays stable where the naive
explicit step would pump energy and diverge.

**Turning a fold into an animation.** A fold is not a cheap function of a slider:
each intermediate state costs its own solve. So the path is solved *once* by
continuation from flat to fully folded, every state along the way is stored as a
Blender shape key, and a single custom property drives them through hat-function
drivers. Keyframe that one property and the model folds — nothing re-solves at
render time.
