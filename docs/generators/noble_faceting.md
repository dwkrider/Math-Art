# Noble Faceting

## Overview

Add a noble faceting: a new polyhedron through the vertices of
an existing solid, isohedral and isogonal at once.

**Faceting** is the operation dual to **stellation**: instead of extending a solid's face planes outward until they carve out new cells, you keep the solid's *vertices* and thread brand-new flat faces through them. A **noble** faceting is one whose result is as symmetric as a solid can be — **isohedral and isogonal at once** (every face equivalent under the symmetry group, and every vertex too). That double regularity is exactly the property singling out the nine regular polyhedra, and a small handful of other solids share it. Bruckner first pictured several of these noble solids in 1900, and Anthony Smith made the stellation–faceting duality explicit in 1965.

Because a noble solid's vertices form a single orbit of its symmetry group and its faces do too, the whole solid is pinned down by one face plus the group — which makes the search for them finite and fast, and lets this generator recover polyhedra that are otherwise hard to reach: the four regular polyhedra on the icosahedron's twelve vertices, and the elusive **duals of the two noble stellations** of the icosahedron and of the rhombic triacontahedron.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Noble Faceting*.
2. **Pick the Vertices** — the seed solid whose vertex set new faces are run through. **Icosahedron (12)** carries the four regular polyhedra (the icosahedron, great dodecahedron, small stellated dodecahedron, great icosahedron); **Dodecahedron (20)** carries, among others, the duals of the icosahedron's two noble stellations (Du Val's D and H); **Cube (8)** and **Octahedron (6)** share the octahedral vertex set; **Icosidodecahedron (30)** carries the duals of the rhombic triacontahedron's two noble stellations; **Truncated Tetrahedron (12)** is Smith's seed.
3. **Step through the Faceting** index. Each seed's vertex set carries a different number of noble facetings, found in plane order; the index wraps around that count, so scrub it to walk every noble solid on that vertex set.
4. **Raise Face Orbits to 2** for the isogonal-but-not-isohedral solids — those whose faces come in *two* shapes rather than one. This is where Smith's Fig. 2 solid lives, its faces equilateral triangles together with alternate-sided hexagons; at the default of 1 every result stays strictly noble.
5. **Choose the output** with **Style** (Solid, Leonardo, Struts, Ball and Stick, Wireframe, Face Segments) and **Scale**; the face-frame, strut and segment styles bring their own thickness/gap/explode knobs.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the solid's name and `N faces of M sides`, so you can confirm the face count and polygon type of the faceting you landed on.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Start with a solid — say an icosahedron — and rub away its faces, keeping only the twelve corner points floating in space. Now play connect-the-dots: draw new flat faces that pass through those same corners in a different pattern. That is **faceting**, and it is the mirror image of **stellation**, where instead you slide the existing faces outward until they slice out new spikes. Some connect-the-dots patterns are messy, but a few are perfectly balanced: every face is a copy of every other face, and every corner sits in an identical arrangement. Those maximally symmetric ones are called **noble**, and they are surprisingly rare — the regular polyhedra are noble, and only a few other solids join them. This generator hunts down all of them for a given set of corners.

**Faceting and stellation as a dual pair.** A stellation takes a solid's face planes and extends them; wherever the extended planes re-enclose a region, a new cell appears. Faceting keeps the vertices fixed and chooses new faces among them. The two are *reciprocal under polarity*: reflecting a solid in a sphere swaps its vertices with its face planes, so the polar dual of a stellation of a solid $X$ is always a **faceting of $X$'s dual**. Smith stated it concretely — "the dual of each stellation of the triakis tetrahedron is a solid formed by faceting the truncated tetrahedron" — which is exactly why the Truncated Tetrahedron appears as a seed here.

**What "noble" buys, and why the search is finite.** A polyhedron is *isohedral* if its symmetry group $G$ acts transitively on its faces, and *isogonal* if it acts transitively on its vertices; **noble** means both at once. When the seed's vertex set is a single orbit of $G$ — as the icosahedron's twelve vertices are under the icosahedral group $I_h$ — a noble faceting is completely determined by **one face together with $G$**: apply every element of the group to that face and the whole face set drops out. So the search never has to consider arbitrary combinations of faces. It enumerates every plane through three or more of the vertices, sweeps each candidate face around under $G$, and keeps those orbits that close into a genuine polyhedron — every edge shared by exactly two faces, every vertex used. That is an $O(n^3)$ pass over the planes rather than an exponential search over face subsets, which is what makes the enumeration quick enough to re-run live on the redo panel.

**The classical check.** The icosahedron's twelve vertices carry exactly **four** noble polyhedra, and all four are regular: the icosahedron itself, the great dodecahedron, the small stellated dodecahedron, and the great icosahedron. Recovering precisely these four — no more, no fewer — is the standing test that the search is neither missing solids nor inventing them.

**The two elusive icosahedral duals.** Beyond the regular ones, the icosahedron has exactly two *noble* stellations — Du Val's **D** and **H** (H being the final stellation). Their duals were long recorded as unbuildable, and the reason is instructive: a stellation engine emits only the *visible surface*, whose apparent "vertices" are every point where the surface crosses itself, not the polyhedron's true corners. Reciprocating that boundary therefore reproduces the wrong solid. Faceting sidesteps the difficulty by **never reciprocating anything** — it builds the dual directly as a faceting of the dodecahedron. Both duals turn out to be 60-triangle facetings of the dodecahedron,

$$V = 20,\quad E = 90,\quad F = 60,\quad \chi = V - E + F = -10,$$

and the dodecahedral vertex set carries *exactly two* such facetings, so the correspondence is **forced by counting** rather than chosen. With the dodecahedron scaled to circumradius $1$ and edge $a = 2/(\sqrt3\,\varphi) \approx 0.7136$, the two are told apart only by the shape of that triangle — they share their long pair of sides $2\sqrt2/\sqrt3$ and differ in the base ($a$ for the dual of D, $\varphi^2 a$ for the dual of H). The build re-derives this correspondence on every run rather than trusting the enumeration order.

**Two face orbits.** Allowing a faceting to use **two** face orbits relaxes isohedral to merely isogonal, which admits solids whose faces come in two shapes. On the truncated tetrahedron this is where Smith's Fig. 2 solid appears: its faces are equilateral triangles together with **alternate-sided hexagons**, three of whose sides are diameters of the hexagon's *own* circumcircle. Such a hexagon closes only by winding twice about its centre — which is why the face is semi-regular and the solid is not among the uniform polyhedra. Smith claimed these vertices "can be joined in only one non-trivial way" into a solid of regular and semi-regular faces, and the generator confirms exactly one candidate survives that description.
