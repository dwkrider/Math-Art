# Bryant Surface

![Bryant Surface](../images/bryant_surface.png)

## Overview

Add a Bryant surface: constant mean curvature ONE in
hyperbolic 3-space, drawn in the Poincare or Klein ball.

Every other generator in this add-on lives in Euclidean space. These do not: **Bryant surfaces** have constant mean curvature $H=1$ in *hyperbolic* 3-space, drawn here through a model — the Poincaré ball by default — that packs the whole of $H^3$ into a finite picture.

$H=1$ is the distinguished value in hyperbolic space the way $H=0$ is in Euclidean space. It is the threshold at which a CMC surface stops behaving like a soap bubble and starts behaving like a minimal surface, and Robert Bryant proved in 1987 that these surfaces have a **Weierstrass-type representation** exactly as minimal surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^3$ do — the same holomorphic-data recipe, transplanted into curved space.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Bryant Surface*.
2. **Pick the Surface.** Four families of CMC-1 surface: **Catenoid Cousin** (a surface of revolution, the hyperbolic relative of the ordinary catenoid), **Enneper Cousin** (the relative of Enneper's surface), **Polynomial Data** (Bryant's general polynomial recipe, containing the Enneper cousin and generalising it), and **Trinoid** (a genus-zero surface with three flaring ends).
3. **Pick the Model** — the window onto hyperbolic space. **Poincaré Ball** (default) is conformal and packs the whole infinite surface into a finite ball, with the ends converging to the boundary sphere; **Klein Ball** also fits it in a ball but draws geodesics straight; **Hyperboloid** is undistorted but unbounded, so a complete surface runs off the picture.
4. **Turn the mode's main knob.** Catenoid Cousin has **mu** — its profile is embedded for $-\tfrac12<\mu<0$ and has exactly one self-intersection for $\mu>0$, a change of shape you can watch as $\mu$ crosses zero. Enneper Cousin has **lambda**. Polynomial Data has **Degree n** and the constant **r2 = c** (with **ODE Steps** controlling the integration accuracy). Trinoid has the three end moduli **d0**, **d1**, **d_inf** — set them equal for a symmetric trinoid, embedded below $\approx 0.2332$ and self-intersecting above.
5. **Push out with Domain Extent** to reach further toward the sphere at infinity, and set **U/V Resolution** for mesh density.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints vertex/face counts and `max |det f − 1|` — the surface's distance from the hyperboloid $\det f = 1$, which should be tiny (the construction lands there exactly). The catenoid cousin also prints its total curvature $-4\pi(2\mu+1)$. An **inadmissible trinoid** is refused with an error rather than drawn wrong: the three moduli must pass the unitarizable-monodromy condition, and out-of-range triples describe no trinoid at all.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** *Hyperbolic space* is a space that has more room in it than ours: step away from any point and the area around you grows faster than it would on a flat plane, so straight lines that start out parallel drift apart. Because it is so roomy, an infinite hyperbolic world does not fit in a finite drawing — unless you cheat with a fisheye lens. The **Poincaré ball** is exactly that lens: it squeezes all of hyperbolic 3-space into an ordinary ball, shrinking distances more and more toward the rim, so the edge of the ball is "infinitely far away." Now recall soap films. In our flat world a soap film is a **minimal surface** — the tautest, least-curved shape spanning its frame. Hyperbolic space is curved to begin with, and this shifts the special value of curvature: there the tautest, most soap-film-like surfaces are the ones bending at a constant rate of exactly **one**, not zero. Bryant's discovery is that these can be built by the very same trick that builds soap films in our world — feed a couple of complex-number functions through a fixed formula and the surface comes out. Everything below is that formula made exact.

**Hyperbolic space as matrices.** The trick works because $H^3$ can be repackaged as linear algebra. Model it as the positive-definite Hermitian $2\times2$ matrices of determinant 1:

$$f=\begin{pmatrix} x_0+x_3 & x_1+ix_2\\ x_1-ix_2 & x_0-x_3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det f = x_0^2-x_1^2-x_2^2-x_3^2 = 1.$$

A matrix and a point are the same data here: the four real numbers $(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)$ that fill the Hermitian matrix are exactly the four coordinates of a point, and the determinant being $1$ is exactly the equation $x_0^2-x_1^2-x_2^2-x_3^2=1$ — the upper sheet of a hyperboloid in Minkowski space, which *is* a copy of $H^3$. The payoff of this repackaging is that the rigid motions of hyperbolic space (its isometries — the moves that slide the space along itself without stretching it) become nothing more exotic than matrix conjugation $f\mapsto A f \overline{A}^{\mathsf T}$. Curved-space geometry has turned into $2\times2$ matrix algebra, which is what a computer can integrate.

**Bryant's representation (1987, Theorem A).** With the space so packaged, here is the recipe. Take *any* holomorphic map $F$ into $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ that is **null**, meaning

$$\det\big(F^{-1}\,dF\big)=0,$$

and form the product

$$f = F\,\overline{F}^{\,\mathsf T}.$$

The result is automatically a conformal immersion into $H^3$ of mean curvature exactly 1 — and, remarkably, *every* Bryant surface is obtained this way, so nothing is lost by only ever building them like this. The single condition $\det(F^{-1}dF)=0$ is doing all the work: it is the "no stretching in the wrong direction" constraint that in $\mathbb{R}^3$ makes the classical Weierstrass data of a minimal surface *null*. Because it is the same condition, these hyperbolic surfaces are exactly as computable as ordinary soap films — the whole problem collapses to *pick holomorphic functions, then integrate an ODE*, with no nonlinear surface equation left to solve.

**Four families.**

**Catenoid cousin** — Bryant's Example 2, a surface of revolution (spin a profile curve about an axis) with a real parameter $\mu>-\tfrac12$, $\mu\ne0$. Here $\mu$ is the one real shape knob, and it does something you can see: the profile is embedded — a clean, non-crossing curve — for $-\tfrac12<\mu<0$, but for $\mu>0$ it folds over itself exactly **once**, so the surface passes through itself in a single circle. The sign of $\mu$ is thus a visible, checkable switch in the shape. For this family the matrix product collapses to a closed form in $z=re^{i\theta}$ that lands on the hyperboloid $\det f=1$ *identically* — an algebraic identity, not a numerical near-miss — so any drift you might measure is a bug rather than a tolerance. Its total curvature (the integral of Gauss curvature, a single number summarising how much the whole surface bends) is

$$-4\pi(2\mu+1),$$

which — unlike the Euclidean case — is **not quantised**. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ a complete minimal surface of finite total curvature is forced to have it an integer multiple of $4\pi$, snapping to discrete rungs; here it slides continuously with $\mu$, one of the genuinely hyperbolic features of the family.

**Enneper cousin** — Bryant's Example 1. Its induced metric is the one Enneper's surface induces on $\mathbb{C}$, hence the name, with total curvature $-4\pi$.

**Polynomial family** — Bryant's Theorem B. For polynomials $r_1, r_2$ with no common zero, integrating

$$F'=F\begin{pmatrix} r_1r_2 & -r_2^{\,2}\\ r_1^{\,2} & -r_1r_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad F(0)=I$$

gives a complete CMC-1 immersion of $\mathbb{C}$ with total curvature $-4\pi k$. Notice the matrix has determinant $r_1^2r_2^2-r_1^2r_2^2=0$ identically — so the nullity condition holds automatically, for *any* polynomials, and the recipe can never accidentally leave the CMC-1 family. To evaluate $F$ at a sample point the code integrates that ODE outward from $F(0)=I$ along the straight ray to the point, by RK4. It can take the straight ray because the equation is holomorphic and the plane is simply connected: the answer does not depend on the path, so the cheapest path — the radial one — is as good as any. Exposed here as $r_1=z^n$, $r_2=c$, which contains the Enneper cousin at $n=1$ and generalises upward as $n$ raises the order of the end.

**Trinoid** — Bobenko–Pavlyukevich–Springborn's explicit CMC-1 surfaces of genus zero with **three catenoidal ends**: think of a sphere with three points removed, each puncture opening out into a flaring funnel, the three funnels joined at a central body. The parameter domain is exactly that thrice-punctured sphere $\mathbb{CP}^1\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}$, the three removed points $0,1,\infty$ becoming the three ends. Their spinor immersion $F=\Psi\overline{\Psi}^{\,\mathsf T}$ solves
$$\Psi_z=\begin{pmatrix} PQ & P^2\\ -Q^2 & -PQ\end{pmatrix}\Psi,\qquad P=\tfrac{p_0}{z}+\tfrac{p_1}{z-1}+p_\infty,\quad Q=\tfrac{q_0}{z}+\tfrac{q_1}{z-1}+q_\infty,$$
which a gauge reduces to a Fuchsian system $\Phi_z=\big(A_0/z+A_1/(z-1)\big)\Phi$ whose canonical solutions at the three punctures are $2\times2$ matrices of Gauss hypergeometric functions ${}_2F_1$, glued by connection matrices. The ends are described by three moduli
$$d_0,\;d_1,\;d_\infty,\qquad \alpha=\tfrac12-\sqrt{d_0},\;\;\tau=\sqrt{d_1},\;\;\rho=\sqrt{d_\infty},$$
which set the hypergeometric parameters $a=\alpha+\tau+\rho$, $b=\alpha+\tau-\rho$, $c=2\alpha$. The immersion closes up on the punctured sphere only when the monodromy is **unitarizable** (their Theorem 6 / Proposition 2); inadmissible $(d_0,d_1,d_\infty)$ describe no trinoid and are refused rather than approximated. The symmetric family $d_0=d_1=d_\infty$ is embedded below the threshold $D_0\approx0.2332$ and self-intersecting above it. (The numeric core lives in `math_art/trinoid.py`.)
