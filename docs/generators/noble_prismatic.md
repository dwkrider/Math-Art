# Crown Polyhedron

![Crown Polyhedron](../images/noble_prismatic.png)

## Overview

Beyond the 146 individual noble polyhedra lie two *infinite* families, and this
operator builds them: Hess's **crown polyhedra** (stephanoids) — prismatic and
antiprismatic rings of self-intersecting faces, one for every choice of side count
and star step — and the **disphenoids**, the tetrahedra whose four faces are all
congruent. Like the catalogue solids they are noble (every face alike, every
vertex alike), but here you dial the family parameters rather than pick from a
list.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Crown Polyhedron.
2. **Pick the Family** — *Prismatic Crown*, *Antiprismatic Crown*, or *Disphenoid*.
3. **For the crowns**, set the star: **Sides** $n$ (base polygon), with **Step P**
   and **Step Q** choosing how far the face's diagonals reach around it (an invalid
   $(p, q)$ falls back to the first valid one), and **Height** between the two
   vertex layers — any height stays noble.
4. **For the disphenoid**, set the box **Width**, **Depth** and **Box Height** —
   two equal sides make it tetragonal, three the regular tetrahedron.
5. **Finish** with **Style** as for the other noble generators.

## How it works

Most of the noble polyhedra are one-off oddities, but a few patterns repeat
forever. Picture two identical star polygons held parallel, one above the other,
and joined by a zig-zag band of triangles that laces up and down between them like
the points of a crown. Keep the lacing rule the same and you can use a 7-pointed
star, an 11-pointed star, any star you like — an endless family, all built the
same way, all noble. The crown polyhedra are exactly that; the disphenoids are the
other repeating case, the "squashed tetrahedra" whose four faces stay identical.

**The crowns.** A crown is specified by three integers $(n, p, q)$: the base is an
$n$-gon, and $p, q$ fix how far around it the long diagonal and the short side of
each face reach, with $1 \le q < p < n$. The *prismatic* crown (PC) stacks the two
rings directly; the *antiprismatic* crown (AC) offsets them by a half-step. Each
face is a crossed polygon, and the band of them closes into a ring that is
isohedral and isogonal for **any** separation of the two layers — which is why the
**Height** slider never breaks nobility: the whole one-parameter tower of a given
$(n, p, q)$ is noble at once.

**The disphenoids.** A disphenoid is a tetrahedron whose four triangular faces are
congruent — realised here as the four "long diagonals" of a rectangular box. All
four faces are alike and all four vertices are alike, so it is noble; with two of
the box dimensions equal it is the tetragonal disphenoid, and with all three equal
it collapses to the regular tetrahedron, the simplest noble polyhedron of all.
