# Zonohedral Cluster

![Zonohedral Cluster](../images/zonohedra_clusters.png)

## Overview

Add a cluster of polar zonohedra packed around a point, or the
solid that facets it.

A **polar zonohedron** is a spinning-top-shaped solid whose faces are all rhombi, built from equal-length edges fanned evenly about an axis. Phil Webster's **cluster** stands one such unit on every face of a symmetric seed solid, all sharing the seed's centre, so that neighbouring units meet face-to-face and pack the space around that point with no gaps and no overlaps. Twenty units on an icosahedron reassemble into the rhombic hexecontahedron; twelve on a dodecahedron give Webster's original twelve-fold cluster. The generator also builds the **faceted** solid that wraps the cluster — the hull of the units' outer tips — which, for the augmented (Kleetope) seeds of Marotta and Redmond, runs through the whole family of truncations of the icosahedron, the Archimedean "soccer ball" among them.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Zonohedral Cluster*.
2. **Choose the Mode.** *Cluster* builds the packing of zonohedra itself; *Faceted* builds only the solid hull that wraps them; *Cluster and Faceted* nests the spiky cluster inside its faceted shell so both are visible at once.
3. **Pick the seed.** **Family** selects where the seed solid comes from — *Platonic*, *Archimedean*, the nineteen circumscribable *Johnson* solids, or the *Prism* / *Antiprism* families — and the **Solid** enum then names the individual seed (for prisms and antiprisms, **Sides** sets its order instead). One zonohedral unit is built per face of whatever you pick.
4. **Reach the generalized family with Augment Faces.** Turned off, you get Webster's exact clusters. Turned on, a pyramid is raised on every face first (a *Kleetope*) and **Pyramid Height** appears: positive heights spike the seed outward, negative ones excavate it, and either way the units become general zonohedra and the cluster reads as a spiky star. Below about $-1.114$ the faceting collapses toward the icosidodecahedron. (Height zero is *not* the plain seed — the apex lands in the face plane and splits it into coplanar triangles, a different cluster again.)
5. **Set the output.** For the cluster, **Explode** pushes each unit outward along its own axis to open the assembly up, and **Separate Units** emits one object per zonohedron; **Colour By** paints the units by *Order*, *Unit*, or *Face Size* (or *None*). For the faceted shell, the **Style** control (Solid, Leonardo panels, Struts, Ball and Stick, Wireframe, or Face Segments) and its parameters appear. **Scale** sizes the whole result.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints the unit count and their orders (e.g. `20 units, orders 3`); the faceted modes add the shell's face count and its edge ratio, and any generalized (non-circumscribable or augmented) cluster is flagged `(generalized)`. If a seed cannot carry a cluster — no circumsphere, or its centre lies on a face — the operator reports why and builds nothing.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A **zonohedron** is a solid you grow from a handful of sticks. Take several line segments, and form every point you can reach by walking some fraction of the way along each one and adding the moves together; the solid blob you sweep out is a polyhedron whose every face is a parallelogram. A **polar zonohedron** is the tidy special case where all the sticks are the same length and fanned out evenly around a central axis, like the ribs of a half-opened umbrella — it comes out looking like a faceted spinning top. Now the cluster: stand at the centre of a symmetric solid like a dodecahedron, and for one of its faces draw the sticks that run from the centre out to that face's corners. Because the solid is symmetric those sticks are equal and evenly spread, so their zonohedron is polar, and it nestles snugly onto the face. Do it for every face at once and the units lock together — each shares a whole face with its neighbour — filling the space around the centre completely. The paragraphs below make "form every sum of the sticks" precise and pin down exactly which seeds this works for.

**The zonotope.** Given generator vectors $g_0,\dots,g_{m-1}$, their **Minkowski sum** — the zonotope — is

$$Z = \Big\lbrace\; \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} t_i\,g_i \;:\; t_i \in [0,1] \;\Big\rbrace .$$

Geometrically you slide each generator freely between "not used" and "fully used"; the extreme points where each $t_i$ is 0 or 1 are the vertices, and choosing any two generators to vary while the rest stay pinned sweeps out a parallelogram, which is why **every face of a zonohedron is a parallelogram**. A zonohedron is **polar** when its generators are equal in length and equally spaced in angle about a common axis — then the parallelograms are rhombi and the solid has the rotational symmetry of an umbrella of $m$ ribs.

**Webster's cluster.** Let the seed have centre $O$ and a face with vertices $V_0,\dots,V_{m-1}$. The construction takes the **segments from the centre to that face's vertices** as the generators,

$$g_i = V_i - O ,$$

and forms their zonotope. Everything hinges on where $O$ is: it must be the **circumcentre**, the centre of the sphere through all the vertices. Then every $\Vert g_i \Vert$ equals the circumradius, so the generators are equal in length; and for a regular face they are equally spaced about the axis $O \to C$ through the face centroid — precisely the polar condition. The zonotope's empty sum sits at $O$ (its **near pole**), and the full sum

$$P = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} g_i$$

is its **far pole**, the outward tip. Building one unit per face packs them around $O$ without gaps, because each edge of the seed is shared by two faces, so each generator $g_i$ is shared by two adjacent units and they meet along a whole common face.

**Why the circumcentre and not the centroid.** The distinction is not pedantic: a diminished or otherwise irregular solid can still have all its vertices on one sphere while its *centroid* has drifted off that sphere's centre. Measuring the segments from the centroid would then give **unequal** lengths and quietly turn a genuine polar-zonohedral (Webster) cluster into a lopsided generalized one. So the centre is found by a least-squares sphere fit: linearising $\Vert v - c\Vert^2 = r^2$ into $2\,v\cdot c + (r^2 - \Vert c\Vert^2) = \Vert v\Vert^2$ makes it linear in $(c,\,r^2 - \Vert c\Vert^2)$, one small normal-equation solve.

**Which seeds qualify.** Two conditions must both hold. First the seed must be **circumscribable** — all vertex radii equal, so the units are truly polar; this is tested as a relative spread $(\max r - \min r)/\max r$ below tolerance. Second, the centre must lie **strictly inside every face plane**, so that each face's umbrella of segments opens *away* from the centre rather than folding back through it; this is the signed face-plane margin, which must be positive. Testing both over the 92 Johnson solids leaves exactly **19** — the 25 with a circumsphere, minus the 6 pyramid-, cupola- and rotunda-shaped ones (J1–J6) whose circumcentre sits on or outside the base — which is the count Webster reports, arrived at independently here. The Platonic and Archimedean solids and the prism/antiprism families pass by construction.

**Icosahedron into the rhombic hexecontahedron.** Because every face of the icosahedron is a triangle, every unit has three generators and so is a **rhombohedron** (a parallelepiped) with six rhombic faces. Of those six, three touch the centre, leaving $20 \times 3 = 60$ outer golden rhombi — whose acute angle is $\arccos(1/\sqrt5) \approx 63.43°$ — that assemble into the rhombic hexecontahedron. The octahedron is prettier still: the three segments to a triangular face's corners are mutually **orthogonal**, so each unit is a **cube** and the eight of them tile a rhombic dodecahedron's worth of space around the centre.

### The generalized clusters — dropping the circumsphere

Webster's conditions are strict, and relaxing them is what opens the second half of the family (Marotta and Redmond). **Augmenting** each face of the seed with a pyramid of signed height $h$ — a **Kleetope** for $h>0$, an excavation for $h<0$ — moves the vertices off the circumsphere. The segments $g_i$ are then no longer equal and the axis is no longer perpendicular to the face, so each unit is a **general** zonohedron rather than a polar one (still every face a parallelogram), and the cluster reads as a spiky star. Height is measured in the seed's own edge lengths, which is the unit every published formula assumes. Note that $h = 0$ is a genuine member, not a no-op: the apex lands *in* the face plane and splits it into coplanar triangles, so a dodecahedron's twelve 5-fold units become **sixty** parallelepipeds.

**Faceting the spikes.** Because adjacent units still share a face, their far poles $P$ are the natural vertices of a solid **wrapping** the cluster, and the faceted mode takes their convex hull (merging the coplanar hull triangles back into real polygons). For the Kleetope of the dodecahedron this hull is combinatorially a **truncated icosahedron** — twelve pentagons and twenty hexagons — for *every* height; the height only slides the two edge lengths $A$ and $B$. Marotta and Redmond track their ratio in closed form,

$$\frac{B}{A} = \frac{2}{5}\sqrt{5\,(5 - 2\sqrt5)}\; h + \frac{5 + \sqrt5}{10},$$

so that $B/A = 1$ marks the **Archimedean** truncated icosahedron (the genuine soccer ball, all edges equal), while at the lower height floor

$$h = -\frac{\varphi^2}{2\sqrt{3 - \varphi}} \approx -1.114$$

the faceting reaches maximum truncation and collapses toward the **icosidodecahedron**. This is the closed form the operator prints as its reported edge ratio, and the reason its Pyramid Height slider bottoms out where it does.
