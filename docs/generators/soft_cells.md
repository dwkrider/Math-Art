# Soft Cells

![Soft Cells](../images/soft_cells.png)

## Overview

A **soft cell** fills space without gaps or overlaps while carrying the fewest
possible sharp corners — in the plane the minimum is two, but in three
dimensions it is *zero*: a single cell can tile all of space with no corners at
all. These shapes, described by Domokos and colleagues in 2024, turn up
throughout nature — in seashell chambers, muscle tissue and river estuaries —
yet were almost absent from the geometry written down before then.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Soft Cells.
2. **Pick the Cell.** This is the main selector, shown in the gallery below. The
   *analytic* cells (the saddle prism, soft triangular and hexagonal prisms)
   have closed formulas and need no solver; the *morphospace* cells (labelled
   f2, g2, h2, i2, …) are softenings of the truncated octahedron, and include
   the Schwarz P and Schwarz D cells and Kelvin's foam cell.
3. **Choose the Wall Scheme.** *Space-filling* keeps the two corners a tiling
   requires; *Soft* removes them at the cost of no longer tiling.
4. **Steer the morphospace.** For the softened cells, **Colatitude** and
   **Azimuth** are the two angles that pick the generating direction — together
   they range over the whole family — and **Symmetry** switches between the
   tetrahedral and octahedral arrangements.
5. **Tile it.** **Cells X/Y/Z** repeat the cell through the lattice, and **Gap
   Factor** shrinks each cell about its own centroid (1.0 shares walls exactly).
6. **Set the surface quality.** **Face Style** builds each face as a *Minimal
   Surface* or a *Ruled* patch; **Edge Samples**, **Face Rings** and **Relax
   Iterations** control how finely it is meshed.
7. **Output.** Weld the block into one mesh or emit **Separate Objects**, and use
   **Crease Angle** to keep the edges sharp while smoothing across the nodes — a
   soft cell is smooth *across* its corners but still creased *along* its edges.

## How it works

Think about how you would tile a floor with no gaps. Squares work, so do
hexagons — but every tiling you can draw has sharp corners where tiles meet.
Domokos and colleagues asked how *few* corners a space-filling shape can have,
and the surprising answer is that in three dimensions you can get all the way to
none: a cell whose entire boundary is smooth, meeting its neighbours along
curves rather than at points, and still packing to fill space. The generator
offers two ways to build such a cell — one with an exact formula, one obtained
by gently bending an ordinary honeycomb until its edges go soft.

**Analytic cells.** The simplest corner-free space-filler is the *saddle prism*:
a square prism capped above and below by the surface

$$z = \sqrt{1 - x^2} - \sqrt{1 - y^2} \pm 1.$$

The point of this cap is what happens at the wall. Its slope becomes infinite as
it approaches the side of the prism, so the cap meets the wall *tangentially*
instead of crossing it at an angle — and where there would have been an edge,
there is simply no corner at all. These cells have closed forms and need no
solver.

**Morphospace cells.** The richer family starts from the truncated octahedron,
the cell of the body-centred-cubic lattice and the classic space-filler. Its 24
vertices are held fixed; what is allowed to change is the *direction* each edge
sets off in as it leaves a vertex. Remarkably, the entire family is fixed by a
single unit vector — that is, by just two angles, the **Colatitude** and
**Azimuth** — so the whole design space is one sphere, and the named cells of the
literature are marked points on it. Two of those points are second-order
equivalent to the unit cells of the Schwarz P and Schwarz D minimal surfaces,
and another is Kelvin's foam cell, so sweeping the two angles carries a minimal
surface *continuously* into a foam. The edges themselves are not arbitrary
curves: they follow Dubins' minimum-curvature paths between the fixed vertices,
the gentlest bend that meets the prescribed end directions, and each face is then
filled as a minimal surface (or, optionally, a ruled patch) spanning those bent
edges.
