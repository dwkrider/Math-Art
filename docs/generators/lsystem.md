# L-system

![L-system](../images/lsystem.png)

## Overview

Add an L-system: fractal curves, plants and published tree
models, as bevelled curves or filled blades.

An **L-system** rewrites a short starting string by applying substitution rules to every character *simultaneously*, for a number of generations, then reads the result as turtle commands — draw forward, turn, pitch, roll, push and pop for branching. Aristid Lindenmayer introduced them in 1968 to model cellular development, and the parallel rewriting is not a detail: cells divide all at once, and that is what the formalism captures.

Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer's *The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants* (1990) turned them into the standard language for describing plant form, and remains the source of most of the production rules here.

This operator lives under *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Fractals* and, because the same engine draws both space-filling curves and plants, it is listed a second time under *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Plants & Growth*.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Fractals ▸ L-system* (the identical entry also sits under *Plants & Growth*).
2. **Choose the Source.** *Preset* draws from the shipped grammars, picked in **System** (50-plus, grouped Curves / Plants / Trees); *Text Block* runs a grammar you have written into a Blender text block, named in **Grammar** — the power-user escape hatch. Turn on **Show Grammar** to see the selected preset's axiom, productions and directives (and to copy them as a starting point).
3. **Tune Grammar Parameters.** If the chosen grammar declares `#param` knobs, they appear as labelled sliders (**P0–P7**) and menus (**C0–C3**) with the grammar's own ranges; untick **Grammar Parameters** to fall back to its declared defaults.
4. **Set the derivation.** **Iterations** is the rewrite depth ($-1$ = the preset's own default), auto-clamped when the predicted word would exceed the **Module Budget**; **Seed** varies stochastic productions; **Angle Override** replaces the default turn angle; **Scale** and **Development** (*Auto* / *Series* / *Final Only*) control layout — *Series* draws each generation as a row, the classic way to read a one-dimensional filament.
5. **Pick the Output.** *Bevelled Curve* gives round tubes (with **Tube Radius**, **Bevel Resolution**, **Round Corners**, and **Taper** → **Tip Radius** for da-Vinci branch thinning); *Skeleton Mesh* gives edges carrying width, colour and Strahler attributes. **Filled Blades** additionally emits `{ . }` polygon captures as leaves and petals.
6. **Add Tropism** to bend every segment toward a fixed **Direction** (gravity or light) with a given **Elasticity**.
7. **Read the report** — the generation count $n$, the number of modules in the derived word, geometry stats, and any note about a clamped depth.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** An **L-system** is a find-and-replace game played on a string, all at once. You start with a short word — maybe a single letter — and a handful of rules of the form "replace $A$ with $AB$". Each round, *every* letter is rewritten simultaneously by its rule, so the word balloons in length and detail: one round, a few rounds, and you have a long, intricate string. Then a **turtle** reads that string as marching orders — a letter means *step forward and draw a segment*, $+$ and $-$ mean *turn left* and *right*, and a bracket means *remember this spot* / *jump back to it*, so the pen can draw a side-branch and return to where it left the trunk. Draw the finished word and out comes a snowflake curve, a fern, or a tree. The "all at once" part is not a technicality: real cells divide simultaneously, and capturing that is exactly why Lindenmayer introduced these systems in 1968. In three dimensions the turtle also *pitches* and *rolls*, so the same idea builds fully spatial trees.

**Rewriting.** Start from an axiom and replace every symbol by its production, all in parallel, once per generation. The classic Koch curve is

$$\text{axiom } F,\qquad F \to F{+}F{-}{-}F{+}F$$

read by a turtle that draws on $F$ and turns on $\pm$. Branching comes from a stack: `[` pushes the turtle's position and heading, `]` pops it, so a subtree is drawn and the turtle returns to the branch point.

**Why the engine is not a one-liner.** This module used to *be* the engine, and it rewrote with a per-character substitution — effectively `"".join(rules.get(c, c) for c in s)`. That makes a **D0L-system**: deterministic, context-free, non-parametric, single-character alphabet, one global angle, one global step.

The limitation was not a shortage of presets. Multi-character module names — `Fl`, `internode`, `seg`, `plant` — were simply unrepresentable, putting every model in chapters 3–5 of *The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants* out of reach. And there is a **theorem**: Prusinkiewicz and Kari showed that arbitrarily large mesotonic and acrotonic structures *cannot* be generated by non-parametric deterministic 0L-systems with subapical branching. So the old engine could not produce a realistically shaped tree at all, whatever angles you chose — a limit of the formalism, not of the tuning.

**What replaced it.** The engine now lives in the `lsystem` subpackage and is parametric, stochastic and context-sensitive, with the full turtle symbol table and growth prediction. In turn:

- **Parametric** — modules carry values, `F(3.5)`, so lengths and angles can be computed rather than fixed.
- **Stochastic** — several productions per symbol with probabilities, so a stand of trees varies instead of being clones.
- **Context-sensitive** — a production can depend on a module's neighbours, which is what lets signals propagate through a structure.

**Output** is emitted as bevelled curves, filled blades, or a skeleton mesh. The operator keeps its original `bl_idname` and its seven preset keys, so saved presets and muscle memory survive the change.
