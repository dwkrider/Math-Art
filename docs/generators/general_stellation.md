# General Stellation

## Overview

Add a stellation of a seed polyhedron (icosahedron, dodecahedron,
cuboctahedron, or rhombic triacontahedron) -- built from the bounded
cells of the seed's face-plane arrangement, grouped into symmetry
shells.

The stellation machinery of [the fifty-nine icosahedra](icosahedron_stellation.md), generalised to an arbitrary convex seed. Extend the seed's face planes, sort the bounded cells they carve out into symmetry shells, and choose which shells to make solid.

Going beyond the icosahedron is not simply a matter of changing a constant. The icosahedron is isohedral — one face orbit, all planes the same distance from the centre, no degenerate intersections — and each of those conveniences fails for a general seed. Four assumptions have to be replaced.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ General Stellation*.
2. **Pick the Seed** — the polyhedron whose face planes are extended: **Icosahedron**, **Dodecahedron**, **Cuboctahedron**, or **Rhombic Triacontahedron**. This is the choice that has no icosahedron-only equivalent; it decides the whole arrangement.
3. **Pick the Stellation.** The second dropdown lists the *verified* named stellations of the chosen seed, and its contents change with the seed:
   - **Icosahedron** — Icosahedron, First stellation, Compound of five octahedra, Compound of ten tetrahedra, Compound of five tetrahedra (chiral), Great icosahedron, Final stellation (echidnahedron).
   - **Dodecahedron** — Dodecahedron and the three Kepler–Poinsot stellations: Small stellated dodecahedron, Great dodecahedron, Great stellated dodecahedron.
   - **Cuboctahedron** — Cuboctahedron, Octahedron, Cube, the compound of cube and octahedron (Wenninger 43), and the final stellation.
   - **Rhombic Triacontahedron** — the seed, the compound of five cubes, the medial and great rhombic triacontahedra, and the final stellation.

   Switching seed resets this to the seed's first (core) entry.
4. **Choose the Style** — *Solid*, *Leonardo (da Vinci)* (**Border**, **Thickness**), *Struts* (**Thickness**), *Ball and Stick* (**Strut Radius**, **Node Radius**), or *Wireframe* — and set **Scale**.
5. **Read the report.** Each build prints the preset's name with its `V=… F=…` counts in the status bar.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** The idea is exactly the one from [the fifty-nine icosahedra](icosahedron_stellation.md): treat each of the seed's flat faces as an endless pane of glass, let all the panes slice space into sealed compartments ("cells"), and let you fill in whichever groups of compartments you like. The catch is that the icosahedron is unusually *tidy* — every face is identical and sits the same distance from the centre — and a lot of shortcuts quietly rely on that tidiness. A cuboctahedron, whose triangles and squares sit at *different* distances, or a rhombic triacontahedron, whose planes cross in messy triple points, break those shortcuts. So the general engine keeps the same three moves — carve the arrangement, group the copies, build the boundary — but replaces four assumptions with methods that don't need the seed to be regular. The engine runs on Python + numpy (no scipy), caches each seed, and finds, for example, 63 cells for the dodecahedron, 119 (in 8 orbits) for the cuboctahedron, and 2003 for the rhombic triacontahedron.

The core strategy, then, is the icosahedron engine's: enumerate the bounded cells of the face-plane arrangement by **sign-vector breadth-first search** — each cell is a vector $s\in\{+1,-1\}^m$ recording which side of each of the $m$ planes it lies on, and crossing one plane flips one sign — with a large bounding cube for the boundedness test; group cells into symmetry orbits ("shells"); and build the outward boundary of any union of orbits. What changes is everything the icosahedron let you assume.

**Per-plane distances.** The planes become

$$\mathbf{n}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}=d_i$$

with a distance $d_i$ *per plane* (the whole set is normalised so the nearest face sits at $d=1$). An isohedral seed has a single $d$; a cuboctahedron has two face-distance classes — its triangles and squares sit at genuinely different distances — so hard-wiring one shared distance would put half the arrangement in the wrong place. Carrying $d_i$ per plane is the price of admitting non-isohedral seeds at all.

**A rank-3 check.** In a general symmetric arrangement, some candidate regions are **degenerate** — flat slivers or lower-dimensional scraps where several planes happen to be coincident or concurrent, meeting along a line or at a point instead of enclosing a volume. These are not solid cells and would corrupt the boundary if kept. Each candidate is therefore tested for *full three-dimensionality*: the engine takes the singular values of its vertex cloud (about the centroid) and demands the smallest one exceed a tolerance, so a region that is really a sheet or a needle — smallest singular value $\approx 0$ — is thrown out rather than assumed solid.

**Convex-hull facet cycling.** The icosahedron engine builds each face by sorting its vertices by angle about the centroid. That fails in general, because a triple-intersection point can lie strictly *inside* a facet rather than on its boundary — angle-sorting would thread the face through that interior point and produce a self-crossing polygon. Instead each facet's boundary is cycled from the **convex hull** of its points (Andrew's monotone-chain scan in the face plane), which cannot make that mistake; it deliberately *keeps* collinear boundary points, because those are shared with the neighbouring face and dropping them would tear a gap in the watertight surface.

**Exact symmetry.** The seed's symmetry group is computed from its vertex set — every rotation and reflection that permutes the vertices among themselves — and then made to act on cells **by permuting the planes**, not by comparing floating-point centroids. Concretely, each symmetry is turned into a permutation of the $m$ plane indices, and two cells share a shell when some plane-permutation carries one sign vector to the other: an exact, integer test resolved by union–find. Matching cells by measured centroid position would misclassify cells that sit at almost the same place — and near-coincidence is the *common* case in a symmetric arrangement, not a rare one.

The pattern in all four: the icosahedron's regularity hid the distinction between "geometrically close" and "combinatorially the same", and a general seed forces you to work with the combinatorics. Once the shells are known, building a chosen stellation is identical to the icosahedron case — union the selected orbits, keep only faces whose across-the-plane neighbour is empty, and emit that outward boundary.
