# Crochet/Coral

![Crochet/Coral](../images/crochet.png)

## Overview

Add a crocheted hyperbolic plane -- a ruffled, negatively
curved surface; graded for crested/cristate coral forms.

The **crocheted hyperbolic plane**, after Daina Taimina's 1997 discovery that the surface [smooth geometry cannot embed](hyperbolic_surface.md) can simply be made out of yarn. Crochet a constant *rate* of increase — one extra stitch every $N$ — and each row's circumference grows exponentially. The fabric cannot lie flat, so it buckles into ruffles, and those ruffles are the hyperbolic plane's excess area having to go somewhere.

It is a genuinely useful model precisely because it gives up on being flat. Hilbert's theorem forbids a complete smooth embedding; a physical fabric evades it by crumpling, and the result is a hyperbolic plane you can hold and measure geodesics on with a ruler.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Crochet/Coral*.
2. **Pick a Preset.** **Wavy → Ruffled → Bendy → Taimina (ball)** run from a gently undulating sheet to a densely folded ball (later presets are slower); **Custom** exposes the raw stitch parameters below.
3. **In Custom, set the curvature and size.** **Increase Ratio N** is the number a crocheter actually chooses: increase one stitch every $N$, with small $N$ giving tight curvature and heavy folding, large $N$ a nearly flat sheet. **Rows** sets how large and how folded the piece grows; **Stitch Size** is the size of one square stitch, and **Max Stitches/Row** caps the exponentially growing count.
4. **Shape the ruffling with controls that apply on top of any preset.** **Cristate Grade** grades the curvature across the sheet: positive flattens the centre and concentrates ruffling at the rim (a crested / cristate form like a brain coral), negative does the reverse. **Lobes** sets how many primary lobes the rim first breaks into.
5. **Choose how it folds.** **Fold By ▸ Built-in Packing** relaxes and self-collides the seeded ruffles internally; **Blender Cloth** hands a flat ruffled sheet to a Cloth modifier with a gather field and (optionally) bakes it. On the packing path, **Coarse-to-Fine** and **Stiffness** trade stitch-scale crumple for larger, smoother waves.
6. **Read the report.** The build prints vertex/face counts (and, in cloth mode, whether the sim was set up or baked). There is no convergence number to read — the ruffles are the settled result of the relaxation itself.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Crochet grows a piece outward one ring at a time, each ring a loop of stitches around the last. If you add the same *number* of stitches every ring, the piece stays flat, like a coaster. But if you add stitches at a steady *rate* — one extra every few, ring after ring — the outer loops need far more length than a flat disc of the same size could offer, and there is simply nowhere flat to put all that fabric. So it waves. Then the waves grow waves, and the edge crimps into the frilly, lettuce-or-kale look of a hyperbolic plane. **Negative curvature** is just the name for "too much room around every point": at each spot the surface has more material than a flat sheet would, and the only way to store the surplus is to fold. The rest of this explains how much extra to add (that sets the curvature) and why the frills come out self-similar — the same ruffle repeating at every scale.

**The mesh is the actual crochet.** No shortcut stands between the model and the craft: each row is a real ring of stitches, and its stitch count grows with the *hyperbolic* circumference of a circle of radius $\rho$,

$$C(\rho)=2\pi R\sinh\!\big(\rho/R\big),$$

every stitch the same fixed size. That $\sinh$ is the entire phenomenon. In the flat plane a circle's circumference grows only linearly, $2\pi\rho$; in the hyperbolic plane it grows *exponentially* with radius. A fabric made of same-size stitches must therefore keep pace with an exponential, which it can only do by gaining stitches at a constant proportional rate — which is precisely what a crocheter's "increase every $N$" instruction is.

**Curvature from the increase rate.** Turn that instruction into geometry: increasing one stitch every $N$ multiplies each row's length by $1+\tfrac1N$, and matching that to the $\sinh$ growth pins the radius of curvature to

$$R=\frac{h}{\ln(1+1/N)}\ \approx\ N h$$

for stitch height $h$. The reading is direct — the surface's curvature scale is essentially $N$ stitches. Small $N$ crochets a tightly folded, very bendy plane (a small $R$, sharp curvature); large $N$ a gently wavy one. The single number a crocheter chooses *is* the curvature, which is what makes crochet such an honest model of the hyperbolic plane.

**Where the ruffles come from.** Now the buckling. When a new row carries more circumference than the ring beneath it can hold at that radius, the surplus has to go out of plane, and a ring of surplus length bows into a fixed number of waves — a wavenumber $m$. As the radius keeps growing the exponential surplus outruns that $m$: the existing waves cannot get any longer without over-stretching, so the fabric splits each wave in two, jumping to $2m$, then $4m$ — a **period-doubling cascade**, wave-count doubling outward like a tree branching. Rather than discover this by luck in the relaxation, each ring is pre-seeded with exactly that cascade, kept phase-coherent so the fine waves sit centred on the coarse ones, with each new wavenumber switched on at the radius where the surplus first demands it.

The special radii where a doubling happens are the surface's **distributed branch points**, and they are the source of the self-similar kale-or-lettuce morphology: because the very same buckling instability recurs at each doubling, the *same* ruffle shape reappears at every scale, a small frill on a larger frill on a larger frill.

**Relaxation.** The seeded sheet only has the right *topology* of folds, not yet their true shape, so it is handed to a position-based solver that nudges every vertex while enforcing one hard rule — each stitch must keep its length (the metric of the hyperbolic plane) — together with a gentle bending term, self-repulsion, and self-collision so the fabric cannot pass through itself. The cascade supplies which folds exist; the relaxation settles where they actually rest. The result is the same physical bargain real crochet strikes in the hand: keep every stitch its size, and let the surplus area buckle wherever it must.
