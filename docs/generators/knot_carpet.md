# Knot Carpet

![Knot Carpet](../images/knot_carpet.png)

## Overview

Add a knot carpet (tileable alternating link of unknots).

Rob Scharein's **knot carpets** (from KnotPlot): "an infinitely complex link consisting of an infinite number of unknots". A doubly periodic field of closed loops, one on every lattice node, each overlapping its neighbours, the whole woven strictly over-and-under into an alternating link.

The point is the inversion. **No loop is knotted on its own** — every component is an unknot, and pulled free of the rest it would relax to a circle. All the complexity lives in how the infinite family interlocks. It is knot theory rendered as wallpaper: the local picture is trivial and only the global one is not.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Knot Carpet*.
2. **Choose the Lattice** — the scaffold the loops sit on. **Square** and **Triangular** are the flat carpets; **Curvilinear** replaces the lattice with a family of smooth curves (Wavy Plaid, Guilloche, Rose, Spiral Net…); and **Sphere**, **Polyhedral**, **Tiling**, **Torus** and **UV Mesh** wrap the same alternating weave onto other surfaces. Each of these reveals its own sub-selector — Sphere Tiling, Solid, Tiling, Torus Tiling, Curve Family or a Target Mesh — so pick the scaffold first and its detail second.
3. **Set the loop and its overlap.** **Symmetry** is the $k$-fold lobe count of each rosette (shown only where it is not derived automatically — the plain square, square-torus and square-UV carpets; on tilings, polyhedra and spheres each medallion follows its tile's neighbour count). **Rosette Amplitude** is the lobe depth (0 gives plain circles), and **Overlap** is the loop radius as a multiple of half the neighbour spacing: it must stay above 1 so neighbouring loops actually cross — the overlap is where every crossing comes from. Tiles X/Y set how many cells are laid out.
4. **Weave it.** **Interlace** turns the flat field of loops into the alternating over/under link; **Interlace Mode** is Flat Knotwork or Woven (3D) with a **Weave Height**; **Style** (Angular or Smooth, with Smoothness) sets the cord drawing. For true rope, **Relax Iterations** above 0 runs a KnotPlot-style bead-and-stick relaxation into 3-D (with Rope Clearance and Weave Gap).
5. **Reach the curve controls** — Amplitude, Frequency, Phase, Warp Strength, Petals/Copies, Arms and Boundary appear only under the **Curvilinear** lattice, where they drive whichever curve family you chose.
6. **Choose the Output.** Ribbon Mesh, Tube (Rope), Centerline Curves, or **Faces (Mosaic)** — the last fills the tiles between the cords and unlocks Tile Color, Include Ribbons, Tile Height and Separate by Color. Color By (Uniform, By Loop, Over/Under), Backing Slab and Relief Height finish the look.
7. **Read the report.** It names the scaffold, the number of woven loops, and the vertex/face totals, so you can confirm the carpet closed up with the loop count you expected.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Lay a grid of identical rings on a table, each big enough to reach into its neighbours so their edges cross. Now go around and, at every crossing, decide which ring passes over and which passes under — and do it consistently, so that as you travel around any single ring you meet over, under, over, under, alternating the whole way. That alternating rule is exactly what a good piece of Celtic knotwork obeys, and it is what makes the whole grid lock into one interwoven fabric. The surprise is that **not one ring is knotted**. Any single ring, lifted away from the others, is just a circle — you could lay it flat without cutting it. All the intricacy is in the *linking*: rings holding rings holding rings, none of them individually tangled. That is the carpet — knot theory that looks like patterned cloth, trivial up close and complicated only as a whole. The rest of this section pins down the lattice, the alternating rule, and why each loop stays unknotted.

**The lattice.** One closed loop is centred on every node of a **square** lattice, basis $(1,0),(0,1)$, or a **triangular** one, basis $(1,0),(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$. Each loop is a $k$-fold rosette $r(\theta)=R\,(1+\text{amp}\cos k\theta)$ about its own centre — a plain circle when the amplitude is 0 — with $R$ set a little over half the nearest-neighbour spacing by the **Overlap** factor, so each loop reaches into its neighbours. That overlap is where the crossings come from: a loop drawn small enough to clear its neighbours gives a field of disjoint circles and no link at all.

**Alternating by construction.** Along each cord, over and under strictly alternate. On a doubly periodic field this is a global consistency condition, not a local choice: it is satisfiable exactly when the crossings can be two-coloured, which the lattice's structure guarantees. Getting it right is what makes the carpet an *alternating* link rather than an arbitrary weave — and alternating links are the class about which knot theory can actually prove things.

**Why every component is unknotted.** Each loop is a simple closed curve in its own plane, perturbed only in $z$ by the weave. A curve whose projection is a simple closed curve — no self-crossings — is unknotted regardless of how it is lifted, because the crossings it participates in are all with *other* loops. So the carpet is a link of unknots whose linking is entirely mutual.

**Substrates beyond the plane.** The same construction runs on other scaffolds: polyhedral and spherical frameworks, tori, uniform tilings and general UV meshes, so the carpet can be wrapped onto a surface while keeping its alternating weave.

**Weaving and output.** The over/under bit becomes a $z$ offset smoothstepped along each strand between crossings, and the result is swept with a proximity-welded, rotation-minimising frame — the same machinery the [rational knot](rational_knot.md) generator reuses. Output as ribbons, tubes, bare curves or filled faces.
