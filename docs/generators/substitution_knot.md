# Substitution Fractal Knot

![Substitution Fractal Knot](../images/substitution_knot.png)

## Overview

Add a Fathauer iterative-substitution fractal knot.

A **fractal knot** built the way Robert Fathauer devised it (Bridges 2007): draw a knot so that some of the gaps in its diagram are similar in shape to the whole diagram, then replace the strand's passage through each gap with a shrunken copy of the entire knot — and repeat.

Because every copy brings its own gaps, the process never terminates. The limit has infinitely many crossings, which makes it a **wild** knot: not equivalent to any diagram with finitely many crossings, and so outside ordinary knot theory altogether. It is a knot within a knot within a knot, and unlike most fractal constructions it stays a single unbroken cord throughout.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Knots & Curves ▸ Substitution Fractal Knot*.
2. **Pick the Base Knot** — the diagram that gets substituted into its own slots. *Trefoil (3 slots)* and *Cinquefoil (5 slots)* are the "bloom" forms, where every lobe of the root sprouts a smaller copy; *Trefoil Clasp (2 slots)* and *Cinquefoil Clasp (4 slots)* open one lobe of the root too, giving a cleaner binary/quaternary branching.
3. **Set the Depth** — the number of substitution passes. $0$ is the plain base knot; each step nests a smaller copy into every slot, and the operator **auto-clamps** the depth if the requested mesh would blow past its point budget.
4. **Shape the nesting.** **Slot Scale** is the child-to-parent size ratio at each level; **Strand Width** is the root strand's thickness (it *telescopes* thinner into deeper copies); **Width Telescope** overrides that with a fixed per-level factor ($0$ = automatic, follow each copy's true geometric scale); **Width Blend** tapers the width smoothly across the joins between levels; **Samples** sets the curve smoothness.
5. **Choose the Output and weave.** *Round Tube* sweeps a rope, *Ribbon* draws a flat band (with **Interlace**: *Woven (3D)* lifts the strand at crossings, *Flat Knotwork* cuts the under-strand; **Relief Height** extrudes the ribbon), and *Centerline Curve* emits the bare path. **Weave Height** is the over/under lift, and **Color By** paints either one hue per **Substitution Level** or a **Uniform** color.
6. **Read the report.** The status line prints the base, the depth actually built (watch for a "depth clamped" warning), the crossing count, and the vertex/face totals.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** The trick is the one behind every fractal: replace a piece of a picture with a shrunken copy of the *whole* picture, then repeat forever. Draw a simple knot — say a trefoil, the pretzel-like knot with three loops — but draw it so that some of the empty gaps in the diagram happen to be the same shape as the whole trefoil. Now, wherever the cord passes straight across one of those gaps, snip it out and splice in a tiny complete trefoil in its place, joining its two loose ends onto the cord you cut. Every tiny trefoil has its own little gaps, so you do it again to each of them, and again, like a set of Russian nesting dolls — except the dolls are all threaded onto one continuous string. Keep going and you get a knot made of knots made of knots, with unimaginably many crossings, yet it is still a single unbroken loop of cord. The mathematics below pins down three things: what the base drawing actually is, how a copy is fitted into a slot without tangling the parent, and how the over/under weave is kept consistent through every splice.

**The base motifs.** The starting diagrams are the standard star projections of the $(2,p)$ torus knots for odd $p$ — the trefoil $3_1$ and the cinquefoil $5_1$ — drawn as the polar curve

$$r = R + a\cos\!\big(\tfrac{p\,\theta}{2}\big),\qquad \theta\in[0,4\pi).$$

The $4\pi$ range is the whole point. The $\cos(p\theta/2)$ term has half the usual angular frequency, so the curve does **not** close after one $2\pi$ turn — it comes back to the *radius* it started at but on the opposite side — and only after a second turn does it truly rejoin its start. That doubling is exactly what braids the strand through itself: the result is a single **unicursal** (drawn-in-one-stroke) loop with $p$ evenly spaced lobes and $p$ crossings, where the two passes at parameters $\theta$ and $\theta+2\pi$ meet. The tip of each lobe is then flattened into a short straight chord — a **slot** — where a copy will later be glued.

**Substitution slots and the splice.** Each slot is a chord whose shape is similar to the diagram's own **gate** (the pair of ends a copy exposes when one lobe is opened up). Fitting a copy into a slot is therefore a single planar **similarity** — a uniform scale, rotation and possible reflection — the unique one carrying the copy's gate ends onto the slot's chord ends. The scale factor of that map equals the slot-chord-to-gate-chord ratio, so **Slot Scale** is literally the size ratio between a child and its parent. The one real choice is direct versus reflected: the code picks whichever lands the copy's body on the side of the chord *away* from the parent's own body — the empty outward side — so a nested copy can never fold back through the strand it hangs from. (The root instance is closed and needs no gate; in the "bloom" presets it keeps a slot on all $p$ lobes, while every spliced copy, having spent one lobe as its gate, branches through the remaining $p-1$.)

**Two invariants the construction must preserve**, and they are what make the method work rather than merely look recursive:

- The diagram stays **valence-4**: every crossing is a point where exactly four strand-ends meet. Because a copy is scaled to fit strictly *inside* its slot, its crossings never land on top of the parent's, so no crossing ever gains extra strands. Every crossing of the substituted diagram is a control vertex the strand passes through exactly twice, and — crucially — the code knows those coincidences *exactly*, without any geometric intersection search, because a similarity preserves the coincidence it inherits from the base motif.
- The diagram stays **alternating**: over and under strictly alternate as you travel along the cord. This is what turns a tangle of crossings into a consistent weave. A closed strand whose crossings are all self-crossings always admits exactly two alternating assignments — the two colours of the checkerboard shading of the diagram's regions — and the code selects one by writing a parity constraint $x_c \oplus x_{c'} = 1$ between each pair of consecutive passages along the strand and solving them with a union-find over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (the same parity engine the Islamic strapwork generator uses). The splice is arranged so the alternation carries unbroken through every join, and the self-test confirms both that the parity system is consistent and that no two neighbouring crossings share a sense.

**Why it is wild.** Each pass substitutes into every slot, so the number of copies — and with it the crossing count — is multiplied at every level: after $n$ iterations the diagram carries on the order of $p^{\,n}$ crossings (exactly $p$ times the instance count the self-test predicts). The limit has *infinitely* many crossings and cannot be reduced to any finite diagram, which is the very definition of a **wild** knot — the same phenomenon as [Antoine's necklace](antoine.md), arriving from the opposite direction: Antoine's set removes structure to infinite depth, this one adds it. In practice the operator stops at a finite **Depth** (auto-clamped to a mesh budget), and as it renders it telescopes the strand width and weave height down by each copy's scale so the deeper, denser knots are drawn with proportionally finer cord — matching Fathauer's original plates.

The [fractal knot](fractal_knot.md) generator reaches a similar coiled, self-similar family by a completely different route — cabling in 3-space rather than substituting in the plane.
