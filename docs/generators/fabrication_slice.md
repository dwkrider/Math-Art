# Slice for Fabrication

![Slice for Fabrication](../images/fabrication_slice.png)

## Overview

Slice the active mesh into flat parts for laser cutting.

Builds an assembled 3-D preview and a nested, real-millimetre
sheet layout, then reports every joint that will not work.

This is a *fabrication style*: it does not add new geometry but turns an object you already have into flat parts a laser cutter or knife plotter can cut, and a person can slot or glue back together. The four constructions are all old — stacked museum cross-sections, John Sharp's crossed-slice sliceforms, radial fins and rings, and ribs threaded on a spine — and everything it emits is in real millimetres, ready to export as SVG or DXF.

### Using it

1. **Select the object to slice first**, so it is the active object, then choose *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Styles ▸ Slice for Fabrication*. The **Object** field is pre-filled with your selection; you can repoint it to any mesh, curve, surface or text object in the file — a curve with a bevel (a knot, say) slices as the tube it draws.
2. **Pick the Technique.** *Stacked Slices* are parallel cross-sections to glue into a stack; *Interlocked Slices* are two crossing families slotted into one another — a sliceform, which uses less material and can flex; *Radial Slices* are half-fins radiating from an axis into a stack of rings, for round shapes; *Ribs along a Curve* are sections taken perpendicular to a curve and threaded on a spine.
3. **Set the size and material** — **Model Size** (the longest dimension of the finished piece, in millimetres, since generators build into a 2 m cube), **Material Thickness** of the sheet stock, and **Kerf**, the width the beam burns away (outlines are offset by half of it so the parts come out to size).
4. **Set the technique-specific slice controls.** Stacked: **Axis**, **Slice Spacing** and alignment **Dowels**. Interlocked: **First / Second Axis** (which must differ) and their **Counts**. Radial: **Fins** and **Rings**. Ribs: the **Ribs** count and the **Curve** to run along (pick *Self* to use the sliced object's own centreline).
5. **Tune the joints** (every technique but Stacked) — **Joint Clearance** (zero is a press fit, more gives the free hinge a paper model needs), **Flexible Material** to allow joints that only assemble by flexing paper or card, **Notch** flaring to ease a large assembly that has racked, and **Tool Diameter** to add dog-bone relief for a router (leave at zero for a laser).
6. **Set the sheet** — **Sheet Width / Height**, **Margin**, and engraved **Label Size** — and choose whether to build the **Assembled Preview**, with an **Explode** slider to pull the previewed slices apart and see how they fit.
7. **What it produces.** Above the original object sits the assembled 3-D preview, each slice a solid plate of the real stock thickness; below it sits the nested sheet layout at true millimetre size. The finished layout is stored on the sliced object, ready to export.
8. **Export.** The panel's **Export SVG / DXF** button (enabled once a layout exists) writes the sheets — one SVG per sheet with the cut/engrave operation carried as stroke colour, and DXF in real millimetres, either one layered file or one file per sheet.
9. **Read the report.** Each run reports the part and sheet count, then every joint that will not work: crossings that cannot be assembled, spans thinner than the stock, slots that could not be cut, parts too big for the sheet, pieces nothing holds, layers that would not section — and, for a rib curve that is not flat, that the ribs must be threaded on a rod because there is no flat spine to slot into.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Cut a loaf of bread into slices and you can stack the slices back into the loaf — that is exactly the old museum trick of building a shape out of stacked cardboard cross-sections. Or picture two combs pushed together at right angles: each has slots cut halfway across, and where they cross they lock. This tool does both. It slices your 3-D model into flat pieces a laser can cut, and wherever two pieces have to cross it cuts a slot halfway into each from opposite edges, so they slide together and stop flush, holding with no glue at all. Everything below turns those two pictures — stacking, and the halfway slot — into exact rules the cutter can follow.

**Scale, decided first and once.** Every generator in this add-on builds its object centred in a 2 m cube. Taken literally against 3 mm stock that is some six hundred slices and a layout no sheet ever made could hold, so **Model Size** is not a nicety but the very first thing the pipeline does: the mesh is scaled so its longest dimension equals the requested real size, and from that point on every number — section offsets, slot widths, sheet coordinates — is millimetres.

**Sectioning.** A single cut is a plane, the set of points

$$\lbrace\,\mathbf x : \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x = d\,\rbrace,$$

with unit normal $\mathbf n$ and signed offset $d$. Intersecting that plane with the model's triangles and stitching the resulting segments end to end gives the closed outlines of one slice. Marching $d$ along an axis in steps produces a *family* of parallel slices; the four techniques differ only in how the planes are arranged and how the pieces are joined.

**The four constructions.** *Stacked* takes parallel sections one material thickness apart. Because a glued stack only reproduces the shape when the planes are exactly that far apart, the slice count is **derived** from height / thickness rather than offered as a knob that could contradict the stock — this is Cavalieri's principle, that equally spaced parallel sections reconstruct the volume in the limit, made physical. Alignment dowels register each slice against its neighbour. *Interlocked* takes two families along different axes; every crossing becomes a joint, giving John Sharp's kinetic sliceform, which collapses flat and moves through a range of shapes. *Radial* must be handled with care: $N$ planes through one common axis all meet along that single line, so no two of them have a pairwise joint to cut. The honest construction is the globe — meridians and parallels — so the fins are clipped to *half*-planes joined to a stack of rings, and a fin must begin beyond the hub radius

$$r_{\mathrm{hub}}=\frac{t}{2\sin(\pi/N)},$$

the radius at which two fins of thickness $t$ separated by angle $2\pi/N$ first clear one another. *Ribs* takes sections perpendicular to a curve, spaced evenly by arclength along it (not by however densely the curve's control points happen to fall); a flat curve gets a real spine plane to slot into, while a curve that is not flat — a knot — gets a threading hole in each rib and is assembled on a rod instead, because there is no planar spine to cut.

**The half-lapped joint.** All four share one rule, stated exactly by Luecking: where two pieces overlap along a shared span, cut each piece from its own rim inward to the **midpoint** of that span, from opposite ends. The two half-depth slots meet in the middle, the pieces pass through one another, and they stop flush — a half-lap that needs no glue. The slot is cut to width $t-\text{clearance}$, so zero clearance is a press fit and a little more opens the free hinge a paper model needs; and because a real beam removes material, every outline is offset outward by half the **kerf** so the finished part comes out to its true size.

**What can and cannot be built.** Nothing here raises an error for awkward geometry — one unusable layer out of sixty should cost that layer, not the whole job — so faults are *counted* and reported instead. A crossing whose two slots cannot both reach through is unassemblable; a shared span thinner than the stock cannot be jointed; a piece that no joint reaches at all is loose and would fall out; a part larger than the sheet cannot be nested. Each of these is tallied and named in the status line, which is the operator's answer to the question "will this actually go together?"

## References

- John Sharp, "Sliceforms: Mathematical Models from Paper Sections", Tarquin Publications, Stradbroke, 1995 -- the book that named the technique and works out the two-family construction.
- John Sharp, "Sliceform Sculptures - a Bridge between Art and Mathematics", Bridges 1998, pp. 275-276 -- the history from Henrici and Brill's models through Gabo, and the kinetic property of the crossed-slice construction.
- Stephen Luecking, "Creating Sliceforms with 3D Modelers", Bridges 2006, pp. 631-638 -- the complementary half-slot rule this module implements, stated in full.
- Yongquan Lu and Erik D. Demaine, "A Pattern Tracing System for Generating Paper Sliceform Artwork", Bridges 2015, pp. 367-370 -- slits spanning alternately the top or bottom half, and the convention of separating cut from score by colour so a laser can map colour to power.
- Jace Miller and Ergun Akleman, "Edge-Based Intersected Polyhedral Paper Sculptures Constructed by Interlocking Slitted Planar Pieces", Bridges 2008, pp. 259-264 -- the same midpoint-slit rule for planes that are not parallel, generalising Hart's slide-togethers.
- George W. Hart, "Laser-Cut Plywood and Cable-Tie Sculptures", Bridges 2015, pp. 77-84 -- fabrication practice for laser-cut plywood sculpture, including bevelling a butt joint by half the angle between the two planes' normals.
- Caroline Bowen, "Cut Colored Paper Sculptures of 3D Contour Plots of the Real and Imaginary Parts of Complex Functions", Bridges 2017, pp. 375-378 -- the stacked technique applied to a heightfield, which is what the Relief Panel generator produces.
- Bonaventura Cavalieri, "Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum nova quadam ratione promota", Bologna, 1635 -- the principle that equally spaced parallel sections reconstruct the volume in the limit, which is what a stacked model is an approximation to.
