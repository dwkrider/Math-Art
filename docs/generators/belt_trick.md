# Dirac Belt Trick

![Dirac Belt Trick](../images/belt_trick.png)

## Overview

The **Dirac belt trick** is a hands‑on demonstration of one of the strangest
facts in geometry: a full 360° turn leaves a belt tangled and stuck, but a
*double* 720° turn can be undone without rotating anything back. A solid spins at
the centre of a spherical cage with belts fastened from each of its faces out to
the cage; the belts twist as it turns, never passing through one another, and
return *exactly* to their start after two turns and not after one. This is the
picture behind why a spin‑½ particle needs 720° to come home.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Odds & Ends ▸ Belt Trick.
2. **Pick the Solid** — this sets how many belts spin: *Two Belts* (the classic
   pair), any Platonic solid, the snub cube, or a geodesic sphere (up to eighty
   belts). More belts, more dramatic tangle.
3. **Drive the Turn.** This is the animation dial: keyframe **Turn** from 0° to
   720° for one full cycle. The belts tangle through the first turn and untangle
   through the second, arriving back where they began only at 720°.
4. **Choose the Spin Axis** (X/Y/Z) — the two belts lie along Z, so spinning
   about Z twists them along their length while X or Y bends and wraps them
   around the solid.
5. **Shape the belts** — **Belt Width** (leave at 0 for the widest that safely
   clears its neighbours), **Reach** (how much of each belt takes part in the
   untangling vs. lies straight), **Smoothing**, and **Thickness**.
6. **Read it** — **Vary Colours** gives each belt its own material so you can
   follow one through its coil; **Cage** and **Solid** toggle the surrounding
   sphere and the central body.

## How it works

Hold a belt with one end pinned to a wall and give the free end one full
turn. The belt is now twisted, and there is no way to flatten it out again
without either turning the end back or threading the whole belt over it. Now do
something that sounds pointless: give the end a *second* full turn, in the same
direction, so it is twisted twice as much. Astonishingly, you can now undo the
whole thing — just loop the slack of the belt up and over the fixed end, and the
double twist melts away, with the end never rotating at all. One turn is stuck;
two turns come free. That difference is not a quirk of belts; it is a fact about
how rotations in three dimensions are connected, and everything in this scene is
built to make it visible.

**The topology in one line.** The rotations of space form the group
$\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and the loops you can trace in it come in exactly two kinds:

$$\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2.$$

A single full turn is a loop that cannot be shrunk to nothing; a double turn is a
loop that can. The belt is a physical witness to that shrinking — its untangling
*is* the contraction of the double‑turn loop.

**Why the belt must remember the turn, not just the orientation.** Here is the
subtle point the construction turns on. You might expect a belt's shape to be a
function of how the cube is currently oriented. It cannot be: the cube looks
identical at 360° and at 0°, so a belt determined by orientation alone would also
be identical there — which would mean the single‑turn loop could be undone, and
it cannot. So the belt state has to depend on the *turn parameter itself*, with a
period of $4\pi$ (720°), and the untangling has to be driven by the very same
parameter that does the winding. Scrubbing one slider both tangles and frees the
belts precisely because of this.

**The frame field.** Attach a little frame (a set of axes) to every point along a
belt. A belt is then a path from the cage, where the frame sits at the identity,
to the solid, where the frame is the solid's current rotation — a path in
$\mathrm{SO}(3)$. Lifted to unit quaternions, with $\psi = \text{turn}/2$ running
over $[0, 2\pi]$ and $s$ the position along the belt, the frame follows Hanson's
double‑twist deformation (his 12.3.2, credited to Hart, Francis and Kauffman),
read with its two parameters swapped — the "candle‑dance" reading of Pengelley
and Ramras, in which **time is the loop parameter and position along the belt is
the stage** of the untangling. Geometrically, at each fixed point along the belt
the turn traces a *circle* on the 2‑sphere of quaternions spanned by the identity,
a fixed sideways axis $m$, and the spin axis $n$; all these circles are tangent to
one another at the identity. At the cube end ($s$ at the solid) the circle is the
great circle through the identity and $n$ — the full double turn about the axis —
while at the cage end it shrinks to the single point at the identity, which is why
that end never moves. Every belt therefore runs around a loop of period $2\pi$ in
$\psi$ (i.e. $4\pi$, 720°, in Turn), and the family of loops from the still cage
to the doubly‑turning cube is exactly the nullhomotopy that the belt trick makes
you watch.

**What you actually see.** The drawn belt is a smooth, penalised fit of that
field's path (the **Smoothing** control), widened to a strip and kept from
creasing, with each solid sized by its circumsphere so the belts always sit the
same way inside the cage. At the half‑way point of the cycle the two belts along
$m$ are straight with a single full twist, and the others are spirals coiled once
around the solid — the moment the tangle is at its worst, just before it comes
undone.

- George K. Francis and Louis H. Kauffman, "Air on the Dirac strings", in The Mathematical Legacy of Wilhelm Magnus, Contemporary Mathematics 169 (1994), 261-276.  The film this construction descends from.
- Alexander E. Holroyd, "The Spinor Linkage - a mechanical implementation of the plate trick", arXiv:2107.01681 (2021).  The arm that precesses at half the cube's rate, and the remark that any number of belts in any directions can share one untangling.
- M. H. A. Newman, "On a string problem of Dirac", Journal of the London Mathematical Society s1-17 (1942), 173-177.  The original several-strings model and the proof that an odd number of turns cannot be undone.
