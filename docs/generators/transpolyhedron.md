# Transpolyhedron

![Transpolyhedron](../images/transpolyhedron.png)

## Overview

Add a transpolyhedron: the transitional form between a
polyhedron and its dual.

Every polyhedron has a **dual** — swap its vertices for faces and its faces for vertices, and a cube becomes an octahedron, a dodecahedron an icosahedron. Haresh Lalvani's **transpolyhedron** (1977) freezes a single frame of the trip *between* a solid and its dual. It is assembled from three pieces at once: every face of the original polyhedron (shrinking toward its centre), every face of the dual (growing in), and one connecting rectangle per edge of the original. A single blend dial slides continuously from one end to the other, so **duality stops being a statement and becomes a motion.**

The twenty seeds built here are George Hart's named examples. Because a solid and its dual produce the *same* transpolyhedron — the two families of faces simply trade which one is growing — the five Platonic solids contribute only three distinct entries, which is exactly the list Hart publishes.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Transpolyhedron*.
2. **Pick the Seed** — the polyhedron to transform, grouped as Platonic, Archimedean, and prisms/antiprisms. Since a solid and its dual give the same result, only one of each dual pair is offered (so, for instance, Cube stands in for the cube–octahedron pair).
3. **Set the Blend** — where along the seed→dual journey to freeze. Near $0$ the seed's faces dominate and the dual's are mere specks; near $1$ the reverse; $0.5$ is the balanced midpoint. Both ends are degenerate (all the pieces collapse together), so the slider stops short of them, at $0.02$–$0.98$.
4. **Keep Colours on** to shade the three face families apart — one material for the seed's faces, one for the dual's, one for the connecting rectangles — so you can see which of the three the construction is assembling; turn it off for a single-material solid.
5. **Choose the output** with **Style** (Solid, Leonardo, Struts, Ball and Stick, Wireframe, Face Segments) and **Scale**.
6. **Read the report.** Each build prints `V=… F=…` broken down as *seed faces + dual faces + rectangles*, so you can confirm the three families are all present in the counts the construction predicts.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Think of a cube and its partner shape, the octahedron: put a dot at the centre of each of the cube's faces and connect them, and you get an octahedron. Every polyhedron has such a partner, its *dual*. Imagine slowly morphing the cube into its octahedron: the cube's square faces shrink toward little points while the octahedron's triangles swell up out of nowhere, and in between the two sets of faces there is a gap. A transpolyhedron is one snapshot of that morph, with the gap neatly filled by small rectangles — one for each edge of the original. Slide the dial and you slide along the morph, from "almost all cube" through a balanced mix to "almost all octahedron."

**Getting the geometry right: canonical form.** The construction needs a well-defined target for each face to shrink toward. So the seed is first put into **canonical form**, the unique size at which all of its edges are tangent to one common sphere (the *midsphere*). In that form each face plane touches the midsphere at a single point, the face's **pole**,

$$p_f = \frac{n_f}{d_f},$$

where $n_f$ is the face's unit normal and $d_f$ its plane's distance from the centre. These poles are precisely the vertices of the dual, so they are the points the seed's faces migrate toward.

**One vertex per flag.** The moving points are indexed by **flags** — incidences $(v, f)$ of a vertex $v$ lying on a face $f$. Each flag contributes one vertex, linearly interpolated between the seed vertex and the face pole:

$$x(v, f) = (1 - t)\,v + t\,p_f .$$

At $t = 0$ every flag of a given vertex collapses onto that vertex, recovering the seed; at $t = 1$ every flag of a given face collapses onto that face's pole, recovering the dual. In between, the flags spread apart and there is room for three families of faces:

- **one per original face** — the flags of its own vertices, i.e. the face shrunk toward its pole;
- **one per original vertex** — the flags of the faces around it taken in cyclic order, which grows into the corresponding dual face;
- **one per original edge** — a quadrilateral over the four flags of that edge's two endpoints and two adjacent faces.

**Why the edge pieces are rectangles.** The edge quad is a **parallelogram for free**, for any seed and any $t$, because the interpolation is affine: for an edge $ab$ shared by faces $f$ and $g$,

$$x(b, f) - x(a, f) = (1 - t)(b - a) = x(b, g) - x(a, g),$$

so opposite sides are equal vectors. It becomes a true **rectangle exactly when the seed is canonical**, because at the midsphere an edge and its dual edge cross at right angles at the tangency point — the two pairs of sides meet the two orthogonal edge directions. So Lalvani's "connecting rectangles" are not something to solve for; they are a *consequence* of using the canonical form.

**Counts and Euler.** The bookkeeping falls out immediately. With one vertex per flag and every edge carrying two flags at each end, the number of vertices is $V = 2E_0$, where $E_0$ is the seed's edge count. The three families give $F = F_0 + V_0 + E_0$ faces. Euler's formula then checks the whole thing:

$$V - E + F = 2E_0 - 3E_0 + (F_0 + V_0 + E_0) = F_0 + V_0 - E_0 = 2,$$

using the seed's own $V_0 - E_0 + F_0 = 2$. The build reports these three sub-counts explicitly, so the identity is visible in every solid.

**Prisms are the one real solve.** Most seeds already have a midsphere or a close relative of one, but a *uniform* prism has no midsphere at all, so it must genuinely be canonicalized — reshaped until a common edge-tangent sphere exists — before its faces have poles to aim at.
