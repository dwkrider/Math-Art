# Focal Surface

![Focal Surface](../images/focal_surface.png)

## Overview

The **focal surface** (the classical "surface of centres" or *centro-surface*) is
where a surface's normal lines focus — the caustic you see as bright curves at
the bottom of a sunlit coffee cup, here drawn in full as a surface rather than a
line. It has two sheets, one per principal curvature, and it is singular exactly
where the source surface is most interesting: it flares to infinity at parabolic
points and its two sheets meet at umbilics. Because curvature is a second
derivative, this generator only works from *exactly-charted* sources, so the
curvatures are analytic rather than noisy mesh estimates.

### Using it

1. **Add it** with Add ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Focal Surface.
2. **Pick the Source** whose centres of curvature you want traced — Ellipsoid,
   Torus, Sphere, Elliptic or Hyperbolic Paraboloid, Catenoid, or Monkey Saddle.
   Each is an exact chart, so its curvatures are computed analytically.
3. **Choose the Sheets** — *Both*, or just the *First* or *Second* sheet, to read
   the two families of centres apart.
4. **Set the Clip Distance.** Near parabolic points one principal curvature goes
   to zero and its centre $1/\kappa$ runs to infinity; this cuts that flare off
   at a finite distance instead of trying to mesh it.
5. **Shape the source** — the ellipsoid's **Semi-axes a/b/c** (keep them distinct
   to keep the four umbilics visible), or the torus's **Ring** and **Tube
   Radius**.
6. **Turn on Source Surface** to build the source alongside its focal sheets, so
   you can read the caustic against the surface that cast it.

## How it works

Stand a pencil on its point on a curved mirror and look at how the reflected
light gathers: the rays don't meet at a single spot but pile up along bright,
cusped curves — a *caustic*. The same thing happens to the straight lines that
stick out perpendicular to a surface. Near any point, those normal lines lean
toward each other and cross at little focusing points, and the collection of all
those focus points is the focal surface. Where the surface curves gently the
focus is far away; where it curves sharply the focus is close; and at the
special points where the surface is locally spherical, the focusing collapses
and the sheets pinch together. Those pinches and cusps are the whole reason to
draw it.

**The two sheets.** At each point $x$ of a surface with unit normal $N$, the two
principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ are the sharpest and gentlest bending
rates, and each defines a centre of curvature a distance $1/\kappa_i$ along the
normal. Letting the point range over the surface sweeps out two sheets,

$$f_i(u, v) = x(u, v) + \frac{N(u, v)}{\kappa_i(u, v)}, \qquad i = 1, 2,$$

one per principal curvature. Together they are the caustic of the surface's
normal congruence — precisely where its normal lines focus.

**Why the singularities are the point.** The map $f_i$ is singular exactly where
the source is special. Where a principal curvature passes through zero (a
*parabolic* point) the centre $1/\kappa_i$ runs off to infinity, so a sheet flares
without bound — which is why the **Clip Distance** exists, to cut that flare
rather than mesh it. Where the two principal curvatures coincide (an *umbilic*)
the two sheets *meet*. The result is a surface laced with cuspidal edges and
swallowtails, the singularity-theory fingerprints of the source's ridges and
umbilics.

**Why exact charts, and what degenerates.** Principal curvatures are second
derivatives, and estimating them from a triangle mesh amplifies noise twice over
— worst precisely where the caustic is most delicate. So each source is an exact
chart whose first and second fundamental forms are written out in closed form and
the shape operator evaluated analytically; the focal surface of a coarse mesh
would be "noise wearing the shape of mathematics." The classical degeneracies all
fall out and are checked by the module's self-test: the **sphere**'s sheets both
collapse to its centre (every point is an umbilic); the **torus**'s sheets
collapse to its centre circle and its axis (both sheets becoming curves — Dupin's
characterisation, one curve-sheet meaning a canal surface, both meaning a
cyclide); the **ellipsoid**'s sheets meet at its four umbilics, Cayley's famous
centro-surface; and a minimal surface like the **catenoid** has
$\kappa_2 = -\kappa_1$, so the source lies exactly midway between its two focal
sheets.

- A. Cayley, "On the centro-surface of an ellipsoid", Trans. Cambridge Phil. Soc. 12 (1873) -- the focal surface of the ellipsoid.

- I. R. Porteous, "Geometric Differentiation" (1994) -- the modern singularity-theory reading of focal sets, umbilics and ridges.
