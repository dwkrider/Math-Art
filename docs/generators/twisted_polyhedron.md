# Twisted Polyhedron

![Twisted Polyhedron](../images/twisted_polyhedron.png)

## Overview

A dual-polyhedron sculpture: an inset polygon sits on every face of a chosen **outer solid**, another on every face of its **dual inner solid**, and a twisted bezier ribbon swirls from each outer-polygon edge to a neighbouring inner-polygon edge, all welded into one closed shell.

The outer solid can be any of the five Platonic solids, all thirteen Archimedean solids (woven to their Catalan duals), or an icosahedral geodesic sphere (woven to its Goldberg dual). Because the whole thing fuses into a single watertight surface, an optional Solidify + Subdivision pair can thicken it and round every corner.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Weaves & Tangles ▸ Twisted Polyhedron*.
2. **Pick the Outer Solid.** This is the main choice: the five Platonic solids weave to their Platonic duals, the thirteen Archimedean solids to their Catalan duals, and the Geodesic Sphere to its Goldberg dual. Switching the solid **loads that solid's tuned default parameters** — few large faces and many small faces want different insets, spins and curvature — so change it first, then adjust from there.
3. **Set the placement and sizes.** **Inner Scale** is the radius of the inner (dual) solid, the outer being fixed at 1; **Outer Size** and **Inner Size** are the inset polygons' sizes as a fraction of their faces; **Inner Spin** and **Outer Spin** turn each polygon about its face normal, which is what makes the ribbons swirl rather than run straight.
4. **Adjust the mode-specific control.** **Frequency** appears only when the Outer Solid is the Geodesic Sphere; it is the geodesic breakdown — higher values give more, smaller triangular faces (and so more, smaller ribbons).
5. **Shape the ribbons.** **Stiffness (Outer/Inner)** set how straight each ribbon leaves its edge; **Middle Width** waists or bulges the ribbon at its centre; **Taper (Outer/Inner)** set how quickly each half reaches that middle width; **Outer/Inner Curvature** dome the face caps toward a sphere; **Ribbon Segments** is the lengthwise resolution.
6. **Choose the output finish.** **Thickness** > 0 adds a Solidify modifier to give the shell a wall; **Smoothing** > 0 adds a Subdivision-Surface modifier to round the corners; **Smooth Shading** sets shading. Each build reports the final vertex and face counts.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Every polyhedron has a partner called its **dual**: mark a dot at the centre of each face, join the dots of neighbouring faces, and the new shape you get has a corner wherever the old one had a face, and a face wherever the old one had a corner. A cube's dual is an octahedron; a dodecahedron's dual is an icosahedron. This generator nests one solid inside its dual and laces them together with twisting straps — it puts a small polygon on each face of the outer solid, a small polygon on each face of the inner one, and runs a ribbon that swirls from every edge of an outer polygon over to a nearby edge of an inner polygon. Spinning the little polygons before connecting them is what makes the straps curl instead of running straight, and because every strap's ends land exactly on the polygon corners, the whole cage fuses into one seamless surface you can then thicken and round. The rest of this section makes each of those steps precise.

**Solid and dual.** The chosen outer solid is built with unit-radius vertices and faces oriented CCW as seen from outside. Its **dual** is computed directly: one dual vertex per face (the unit face centroid), and, per outer vertex, the ring of incident faces ordered CCW — that ring *is* the dual face at the vertex, because the faces meeting at a corner of the original become the corners of one face of the dual. The dual of a Platonic solid is another Platonic; the dual of an Archimedean solid is its Catalan solid; the dual of the icosahedral geodesic sphere is the Goldberg solid of hexagons and twelve pentagons. Reading the vertex degree per vertex from the ring lengths (rather than assuming it constant) is what lets the mixed-degree solids — the geodesic sphere and its Goldberg dual — work alongside the uniform ones. The outer solid is scaled to just fill a 2 m cube ($\max|\text{coord}| = 1$), and the dual is placed at radius **Inner Scale**, so a single family of insets and spins reads well whatever the size of the pieces.

| Outer solid | Inner (dual) | Outer faces | Inner polygon (vertex degree $m$) |
|-------------|--------------|-------------|-----------------------------------|
| Tetrahedron | Tetrahedron (self-dual) | triangles | triangles ($m=3$) |
| Cube | Octahedron | squares | triangles ($m=3$) |
| Octahedron | Cube | triangles | squares ($m=4$) |
| Dodecahedron | Icosahedron | pentagons | triangles ($m=3$) |
| Icosahedron | Dodecahedron | triangles | pentagons ($m=5$) |
| Archimedean | Catalan dual | mixed | equal-sided (= vertex degree) |
| Geodesic | Goldberg | triangles | pentagons & hexagons |

**Inset, spin, cap.** An inset polygon is built on every outer face (scaled toward its centroid by **Outer Size**) and every inner face (by **Inner Size**), then each is spun about its face normal — inner by $+$**Inner Spin** (CCW), outer by $-$**Outer Spin**. That spin is the whole source of the swirl: with zero spin each ribbon would leave its edge pointing more or less straight at its partner, and turning the small polygons offsets the two ends tangentially so the connecting strap has to twist. Each inset polygon is then filled with a **subdivision-friendly cap** that deliberately avoids a high-valence centre pole, because a pole makes Catmull–Clark subdivision pinch or seam the cap: a triangle cannot be tiled by quads without an irregular vertex, so it uses a 3-triangle fan to a single domed valence-3 apex (as clean as a cube corner), while an $n\ge4$ polygon uses an outer band of quads around one central $n$-gon, with no pole at all. The **Outer / Inner Curvature** then blends the cap interior toward the sphere that passes through the polygon's corners ($0$ = flat, $1$ = on that sphere), while the corners themselves stay pinned in place — that pinning is what keeps the ribbon weld exact even as the caps dome.

**Ribbon pairing.** Each outer face fans out one ribbon per edge, and the trick is deciding *which* inner edge each outer edge should reach for. Doing that by nearest distance would flip pairings unpredictably as the polygons spin; instead the pairing is **topological, and therefore rotation-proof**. Outer face $f$'s edge $(v_i \to v_{i+1})$ binds to the inner polygon sitting at the **forward vertex** $v_{i+1}$, at edge index

$$(j + \text{step}) \bmod m,\qquad \text{step} = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{\text{Inner Spin}}{360/m}\right),$$

where $j$ is $f$'s position in the ring of faces around that vertex and $m$ is the vertex degree (which, recall, equals the number of sides of that inner polygon). The geometric meaning is that an inner polygon carrying $m$-fold rotational symmetry looks identical after a turn of $360/m$, so a spin of the polygon can be absorbed by relabelling its edges: `step` counts how many whole such turns the **Inner Spin** amounts to. At `step = 0` each outer edge binds to the dual edge lying directly below it; every additional $360/m$ of spin advances the target by one edge. Because the same integer shift is applied everywhere and $m$-fold symmetry guarantees it stays within the polygon, the map is a clean **bijection** — every inner edge receives exactly one ribbon — for every solid and at every spin angle, with no ribbon left doubled up or unclaimed.

**Twisted ribbon.** Each ribbon is a pair of cubic bezier **rails**, one from each outer-edge endpoint to its paired inner-edge endpoint. A cubic bezier

$$\mathbf B(t) = (1-t)^3\mathbf p_0 + 3(1-t)^2 t\,\mathbf p_1 + 3(1-t)t^2\,\mathbf p_2 + t^3\mathbf p_3$$

starts at $\mathbf p_0$, ends at $\mathbf p_3$, and leaves each end pointing at its neighbouring control point. Here $\mathbf p_1$ and $\mathbf p_2$ are placed *along the edge tangents* of the outer and inner faces, at distances **Stiffness (Outer)** and **Stiffness (Inner)** times the span between the edges — so a higher stiffness makes the ribbon shoot out straight and perpendicular before curving, while a low value lets it peel away immediately. Because the two rails run between two edges that have been spun relative to each other, they wind around one another, and that winding *is* the ribbon's twist. The centreline is the average of the two rails and the twist is the direction between them, but the ribbon's half-**width** is not left to the rails; it follows an eased **width profile** running from the outer half-width $w_0$ to the inner half-width $w_1$ through a middle width $\text{mid}\cdot(w_0+w_1)$. Setting **Middle Width** below $0.5$ therefore pinches a waist thinner than either end, and above $0.5$ bulges it; **Taper (Outer/Inner)** control how sharply each half approaches that middle width ($0$ = a straight linear taper), while the two endpoints stay exact so the weld is never disturbed. The finished ribbon is tessellated across **Ribbon Segments** lengthwise steps into a strip of quads.

**Rail pairing and orientability.** An outer edge has two endpoints and so does its inner partner, and there are two ways to join them — straight across, or crossed. The default `"dist"` choice joins them the **non-crossing** way, swapping the inner endpoints whenever that shortens the total rail length; this gives the cleanest-looking ribbon. The subtlety is global rather than local: on a few solids (the truncated tetrahedron, cuboctahedron and icosidodecahedron) these locally-best choices cannot all be made consistent at once, and the resulting shell is **non-orientable** — it has no coherent inside and outside, which would make a Solidify wall flip through itself. The operator detects this directly by counting *manifold but non-contiguous* edges (edges where the two faces disagree on winding) after merging, and only when that count is nonzero does it rebuild with the always-consistent `"fwd"` pairing. Every solid that can keep the nicer pairing does.

**Welding and modifiers.** The whole point of anchoring every ribbon end on a polygon corner is that the parts are not really separate: all ribbon ends coincide with the outer/inner polygon edge vertices, so the caps and ribbons accumulate into **one welded vertex pool**. A bmesh pass merges the coincident doubles and recalculates face normals to unify the windings, turning the lattice of straps into a single watertight surface — which is exactly the condition that lets subdivision smooth *across* the junctions instead of treating each strap as a loose flap. On that shell, **Thickness** > 0 adds a Solidify modifier to give it a wall, and **Smoothing** > 0 adds a Subdivision-Surface modifier to round the corners; the pole-free caps built earlier are what let that subdivision stay smooth. Switching the **Outer Solid** reloads that solid's tuned defaults (few large faces versus many small ones want different insets, spins and curvature), and the Geodesic Sphere additionally exposes **Frequency**. Each build reports the resulting vertex and face counts.

## References

- Inspired by Bathsheba Grossman's "Quintrino" (and her "Ora" family of dual-symmetry sculptures): strap-like arms swirling around the rotational-symmetry axes of a dodecahedron. This generalizes that idea to any Platonic or Archimedean solid woven to its dual.
- Polyhedron/dual pairing: the dual of a Platonic solid is Platonic; the dual of an Archimedean solid is its Catalan solid.
