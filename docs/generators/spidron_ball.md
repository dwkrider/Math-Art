# Spidroball

![Spidroball](../images/spidron_ball.png)

## Overview

Add a spidronised solid: every face replaced by a spiral
nest of triangles.

A **spidroball** is a polyhedron whose every face has been replaced by a spidron nest — a spiral of ever-shrinking triangles winding to the face's centre — and then lifted into relief so the spirals stand proud and interlock. Dániel Erdély, Amina Bühler-Allen and Marc Pelletier named the family; Paul Nylander's dodecahedral version, five spiral arms to a face, is its widely reproduced picture, and the rhombic triacontahedron assembled from thirty spidron pairs is the one the press showed. This operator uses van Ballegooijen–Gailiunas–Erdély's generalisation of spidronisation to any polygon (skew ones included), so it decorates seven different seed solids.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Polyhedra ▸ Spidroball*.
2. **Pick the Solid.** The seed whose faces are spidronised: *Tetrahedron*, *Cube*, *Octahedron*, *Dodecahedron* (the classical spidroball, five arms a face), *Icosahedron*, *Truncated Icosahedron* (pentagons and hexagons, so five- and six-armed nests mixed), or *Rhombic Triacontahedron* (thirty rhombi — the first spidroball shown).
3. **Set the spiral shape.** **Rings** is how many times the spiral step repeats on each face (Nylander's ball uses eight); **Ring Scale** is how much each ring shrinks toward the face centre (two-thirds is Nylander's value, larger makes a shallower, denser vortex).
4. **Lift it into relief.** **Relief** raises alternate boundary points out of the face plane before spiralling, so the nests stand out of the solid; the default $0.106$ makes a pentagon's skew boundary exactly regular, which is Nylander's ball. **Relief Style** decides how: *Woven* displaces shared boundary points radially so neighbouring faces meet exactly and the ball reads as one interlocked surface, while *Per Face* lifts each face along its own normal, leaving separate raised decorations (a hedgehog rather than a weave).
5. **Tune the twist and winding.** **Twist Style** *Advance* turns each ring by one node step of the boundary plus **Twist**, which advances the corrugation by a node per ring and weaves the arms over the folds — the broad-petalled Nylander look; *Fixed* turns by exactly Twist for a shallower rosette. **Chirality** winds every face the same way (*Clockwise*/*Anticlockwise*) or *Alternate*, the opposite-neighbours pairing an assembly of these solids needs.
6. **Choose the output and read the report.** **Color** assigns materials by joined arms, arm, ring, face, triangle shape, chirality or uniformly, capped by **Colors**; **Smooth Shading** and **Sharp Creases** keep each arm smooth while the arm-to-arm folds stay crisp; **Open Centres** leaves each face's central hole open. The report prints vertex, face and crease counts — and warns when the chosen Colors cannot avoid colour repeats on that solid (stating how many it needs), or when *Alternate* chirality is impossible because the face graph has an odd cycle.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Start with a solid — say a dodecahedron. On one of its flat faces, draw a copy of that face shrunk a bit and spun a bit, then draw the copy inside *that*, and so on: a nest of shrinking, turning polygons spiralling into the middle, with the ring between each pair filled by triangles. That is a spidron nest, and you glue one onto every face. So far it is flat decoration. The magic is in pushing it out of the surface: lift the corners of each face up and the dips down, and the spiral arms of neighbouring faces climb over the shared edges and lace together into a spiky, woven ball. Which points you push, and in which direction, is the whole difference between a beautiful interlocked ball and a heap of loose spikes — and that is what the maths below pins down.

**Spidronising a face.** The core operation (van Ballegooijen–Gailiunas–Erdély) takes a face polygon and repeatedly maps it toward the face centre $C$: scale by a factor $s$ toward $C$ and rotate by a twist $\tau$ about the axis through $C$ along the face normal. Writing $R_\tau$ for that rotation, one ring's boundary becomes

$$P^{(k+1)} = s\,R_\tau\big(P^{(k)} - C\big) + C,$$

and the annulus between consecutive rings is triangulated two triangles to a side, exactly as in the flat figure. Because each step is the *same* scale-and-rotate, every vertex's images lie on a **logarithmic spiral** — successive radii in the constant ratio $s$ — which is why the arms curl inward so smoothly. This works for *skew* polygons too, and that is what makes spidronising the faces of a solid possible at all.

**Relief: which points move, and where.** With **Relief** $e = 0$ each face is decorated flat in its own plane. Raising $e$ first refines the boundary to the face's own vertices plus its edge midpoints (an n-sided face becomes a 2n-point boundary so the up/down alternation can close), then displaces those points. The two **Relief Styles** differ in the displacement direction, and the difference is decisive:

- **Woven** moves points **radially**, from the solid's centre: it pulls the solid's vertices *in* by $(1-e)$ and pushes its edge midpoints *out* by $(1+e)$. Because a shared point's new position then depends only on the point and the centre, *every* face that meets there computes the same position — so the nests join seamlessly and the ball reads as one continuous corrugated surface.
- **Per Face** moves each face's boundary along **its own normal**. A solid's vertex belongs to three faces, so its three copies fly apart in three directions and the surface tears into shards.

Woven raises a ridge along every edge (the midpoints go out) rather than a spike at every vertex, and the arms of the two faces sharing that edge sweep up over the ridge together — which is exactly what makes the surface look woven.

**The relief that makes it regular.** There is one special relief for an n-gon face: the value that makes its lifted skew 2n-gon boundary *regular* — all $2n$ points equidistant from the face axis. Seen from that axis the pulled-in vertices sit at the circumradius $r_c$ and the pushed-out midpoints at the apothem $r_a = r_c\cos(\pi/n)$, and equalising $(1-e)\,r_c = (1+e)\,r_a$ gives

$$e = \frac{1 - \cos(\pi/n)}{1 + \cos(\pi/n)} = \tan^2\!\frac{\pi}{2n}.$$

For a pentagon that is $\tan^2(\pi/10) = 0.105573$, and the resulting skew decagon is node-for-node the boundary of Nylander's dodeca-spidroball — reached here by a *derived* number, not a fitted one.

**The twist that weaves the petals.** A woven boundary's corrugation has period *two* nodes (up, down, up, down…), so rotating a ring by one whole node step maps the boundary onto its parity mirror — raised points land where lowered ones were. **Twist Style: Advance** builds in exactly that full step for a face of $m$ boundary points, plus the user's excess:

$$\tau = \frac{2\pi}{m} + \text{Twist}.$$

Each ring therefore advances the corrugation phase by one node, an arm climbs over the adjacent fold as it spirals in, and the outermost triangles stay broad and flat — the deep five-arm vortices and wide petals of Nylander's renders. A small *fixed* twist never flips the parity and yields only a shallow rosette; that is **Twist Style: Fixed**, $\tau = \text{Twist}$. (With Advance, scale $2/3$, eight rings, the derived relief and uniform chirality, this reproduces Nylander's published ball vertex-for-vertex.)

**Chirality and its obstruction.** Each face's spiral can wind clockwise or anticlockwise. Aggregating these solids requires *alternating* chirality across every shared face — a proper two-colouring of the face-adjacency graph. That is possible exactly when the solid's dual has a bipartite edge graph, and among the seven seeds **only the octahedron qualifies** (its dual is the cube, all quadrilaterals); every other dual has triangular or pentagonal faces, hence an odd cycle, so *Alternate* is a best effort and the operator says so. This is the same fact the Bridges paper states as "a polyhedron with an odd number of identical faces needs two different spidronised forms."

**Colouring whole petals.** The default *Joined Arms* mode treats a **petal** — the flap over one edge of the solid, made of the two arms either side of that edge's ridge, one from each of the two faces sharing it — as a single coloured unit, and forbids a face from repeating a colour among its petals. That is a graph-colouring problem: a face's edges form a clique, so a solid whose largest face has $s$ sides needs at least $s$ colours (five for the dodecahedron, seven for the truncated icosahedron). A DSATUR-plus-restarts colouring finds that optimum, and **Colors** is a ceiling it stays under.
