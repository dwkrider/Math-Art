# Relief Panel

![Relief Panel](../images/relief_panel.png)

## Overview

Add a relief panel whose height is a pattern field.

A relief panel is a surface whose height is a scalar field $h(x,y)$ — a plate carved by mathematics rather than assembled from parts. The field is built **compositionally**, from pattern, orientation, warp and transfer, so that the organic and the geometric can be mixed rather than chosen between: a drumhead mode pushed through a smooth domain warp and a crest-sharpening transfer becomes drapery without ceasing to be a drumhead mode.

The design idea the generator turns on is that ridges which fan, split and sweep across a panel are an **orientation** phenomenon, not an amplitude one. A wave with a single global direction cannot produce them however its amplitude is modulated — the direction field has to vary.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Relief Panel*.
2. **Start from a Load Preset** if you want a finished look fast. The presets — Drapery, Dunes, Pond, Terrain, Chladni Plate, Ocean, Quasicrystal and some two dozen more — each fill in every control below, so they are starting points rather than separate modes: switching to **Custom** afterwards keeps whatever the preset just set instead of snapping back to defaults.
3. **Pick the Pattern** — the base field. This is the biggest choice: a wave train, a fractal (fBm or Weierstrass–Mandelbrot), a circular ripple, a solved drumhead or plate mode, Worley cells, a Gabor weave, a quasicrystal, a Turing reaction–diffusion skin, a wallpaper-group function, an elliptic function, or a tiling (Truchet, Seigaiha). **The panel redraws its controls for whichever pattern you choose** — Wavelength/Angle for waves, Hurst/Octaves for fractals, Regime/Steps for Turing, Mode m/n for membranes, and so on — so most sliders in the Options table only appear for the pattern they belong to.
4. **Steer it with Orientation & Warp**, the load-bearing stage. **Orientation** is the *direction field* the pattern is evaluated along — Constant, Radial, Tangent, Spiral, Curl (flow) or Gradient — and it is what makes ridges fan and split rather than run parallel; Field Scale and Swirl appear for the swirling fields. **Warp** then displaces the coordinates before evaluation, which is what turns a regular field into flowing cloth. (Warp is switched off automatically while Tiling is on, since a warp cannot be made periodic.)
5. **Shape the panel and its surface.** Choose the outline (Rect, Disc, Ellipse, Rounded Rect), Width, Aspect, Resolution and Border; then a **Profile** transfer — Ridges and Valleys sharpen the zero set into crests or creases, Gamma/S-Curve bias light and shade, Terrace and Clamp step or flatten the height.
6. **Choose the output.** Set Relief Depth, pick a **Form** (Slab for a watertight panel, Sheet for an open surface), and a **Fit** (Footprint, 2 m Cube or literal metres). Turn on **Pierce** to open the panel right through wherever the field is low — a relief becomes a screen — or set **Tiling** so copies abut invisibly.
7. **Read the report.** The generator prints the sample grid and vertex/face counts, and *warns* when something will not come out as intended: that the panel does **not** tile (with the measured joint step and curvature, where 1.0 would be invisible), or that the wavelength spans too few samples and the pattern will alias — raise Resolution or Wavelength.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture a flat sheet of soft clay and a rule that, for every point on it, tells you how far to push that point up or down. That rule is the whole panel: give it a wavy rule and you get corrugations, a fractal rule and you get rough terrain, a drumhead's vibration pattern and you get its ripples frozen in relief. The clever part is a second, separate rule for *which way* the pattern runs at each spot. Corrugated iron has ridges that all point the same way; the folds of a hanging curtain fan out, gather, and occasionally split into two — and the difference is not how *tall* the ridges are but how their *direction* turns from place to place. This generator keeps those two rules apart on purpose: one picks the pattern, another steers it, a third can bend the whole sheet before it is read, and a last one reshapes the finished heights. Below, each of those becomes a piece of math.

**The pipeline.** Height is assembled in four composed stages rather than chosen from a list:

$$h(x,y) \;=\; T\Big(\,P\big(W(x,y);\ \Theta(x,y)\big)\Big)$$

- **Pattern $P$** — the base field: a wave, a drumhead mode, a cellular or Gabor noise, a harmonic, a quasicrystal, a reaction–diffusion (Turing) field, a lattice. This is the raw texture, evaluated at each point of the domain.
- **Orientation $\Theta$** — a *direction field* the pattern is evaluated along. It supplies, at every point, the local heading the pattern should follow, and it is the load-bearing idea developed below.
- **Warp $W$** — a smooth deformation of the domain applied *before* evaluation, so the pattern is read at displaced coordinates. Because it moves where the pattern is sampled rather than what the pattern is, it bends and gathers without changing the pattern's character.
- **Transfer $T$** — a remapping of the resulting height: absolute value to fold the sheet into creases, ridge and terrace curves to sharpen crests, gamma and S-curves to bias where the surface reads as light or shade.

The reason these are stages and not a menu is that they **compose**: $T\circ P$ is a different surface for every $\Theta$ and $W$ slid underneath it. The same drumhead mode is drapery, corduroy or a rippled pond depending only on what is wrapped around it — which is why a "pattern" here is not a preset but one factor in a product.

**Why orientation is the crux.** Take the simplest pattern, a plane wave,

$$h=\cos\!\big(k\,(x\cos\alpha + y\sin\alpha)\big),$$

whose argument is the dot product of the position with a fixed unit heading $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$. With a single global $\alpha$ the crests are the parallel straight lines where that dot product is constant, and **no amplitude modulation can bend them**: writing $A(x,y)\cos(k\,\dots)$ only fades the same straight ridges in and out, because the *places* where $\cos$ peaks are fixed by the phase, and amplitude never touches the phase. To move a crest you must move the phase. So let the heading itself vary with position and read the wave off an accumulated phase $\phi$,

$$h=\cos\!\big(k\,\phi(x,y)\big),\qquad \nabla\phi \parallel \big(\cos\alpha(x,y),\ \sin\alpha(x,y)\big),$$

where the second condition says the phase climbs fastest along the local heading — its gradient is parallel to the direction field. The crests are now the curved level sets $\phi=\text{const}$, and their geometry is exactly the geometry of the field: **where the directions converge, the level sets crowd and the ridges merge; where they diverge, the ridges fan; and where the field has a singular point** — a spot no consistent single direction can be assigned — **the level sets cannot close up smoothly and a ridge splits in two.** That split is a dislocation in the wave pattern, the direct analogue of a defect in a crystal lattice or a fingerprint's ridge ending. Recovering $\phi$ from a prescribed direction field is an integration problem (the field need not be an exact gradient), solved here in the spectral least-squares sense of Frankot–Chellappa. The upshot is that fanning, splitting and sweeping are properties of the orientation field's *topology*, not of amplitude, and that is why they cannot be imitated by turning a wave up and down.

**Curves and thickness.** Once $h(x,y)$ is fixed it is sampled on the panel grid, the transfer's folds are cut in as sharp creases, and the sheet is thickened into a slab (or left an open sheet). The result is a closed, printable solid rather than a displaced plane — a height field made into an object.

For the same idea on closed surfaces, where there is no rectangle to be a function of, see [Solid Relief](relief_solid.md).
