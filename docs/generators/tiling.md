# Tiling

## Overview

Add a uniform Euclidean tiling.

A **uniform tiling** covers the plane edge-to-edge with regular polygons and is vertex-transitive: every vertex looks the same as every other. There are exactly **11** — the 3 regular tilings, by triangles, squares or hexagons, and the 8 Archimedean (semiregular) ones that mix polygon types. Kepler drew them systematically in *Harmonices Mundi* in 1619, four centuries before the modern classification.

Their **duals**, the 8 Laves tilings, are the other half of the family: not vertex- but *tile*-transitive, built from a single repeated tile that need not be regular. This generator produces all 19, with the Laves tilings computed as genuine duals rather than transcribed, so only the 11 base tilings carry hand-placed coordinates.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling.** The menu lists all 19 by name and **vertex configuration** — the polygons meeting at a vertex, read around it. The 3 regular members are *Triangular (3.3.3.3.3.3)*, *Square (4.4.4.4)* and *Hexagonal (6.6.6)*; the 8 Archimedean members mix polygon types (e.g. *Rhombitrihexagonal (3.4.6.4)*); and the 8 **Laves** members, labelled *(dual …)*, are the tile-transitive duals of the Archimedean ones.
3. **Set the patch size** with **Cells X** and **Cells Y** — how many copies of the tiling's translational unit cell to lay down along each lattice direction (1–40 each).
4. **Choose the colouring** with **Color By**: *By Sides* gives each polygon a material by its side count (triangles, squares, hexagons… each their own colour), *By Tile Type* colours by the tile's orbit within the unit cell, and *Uniform* is a single material.
5. **Shape the output.** **Margin** insets every tile toward its centroid, opening grout lines between tiles; **Relief Height** at 0 keeps a flat 2-D mesh and above 0 extrudes each tile into a prism; **Trim Boundary** clips the ragged edge to a clean rectangle (the patch is over-built and then cut, so skew lattices still square off); **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own object under an empty, so tiles can be edited individually.
6. **Read the report.** The header line prints the chosen tiling with its vertex and face counts (or, when separated, the number of tiles) — a quick check that the patch built at the size you asked for.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Imagine tiling a floor with regular tiles — all triangles, all squares, all hexagons, or a mixture — laid edge to edge with no gaps and no overlaps. Now add one rule: every corner in the floor must look the same, the same tiles crowded around it in the same order. It turns out there are only **eleven** ways to do this across the whole plane. Three use a single shape; the other eight mix two or three regular shapes. There is a companion trick: put a dot in the middle of each tile, join dots whose tiles touch, and a *second* pattern appears — its tiles all identical to one another but no longer regular. That gives eight more, the "duals". This generator draws all nineteen.

**Why only eleven.** The rule "every corner looks the same" is really a rule about angles. The polygons crowded around a vertex must exactly fill the $2\pi$ of a full turn — no gap, no overlap. A regular $n$-gon has interior angle $\pi(n-2)/n$, so if $n_1,\dots,n_k$-gons meet at the vertex,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{n_i-2}{n_i}=2 .$$

This is a Diophantine condition — a whole-number equation — and it is surprisingly restrictive: only 17 solutions exist as multisets, 21 once the distinct cyclic *orderings* around the vertex are counted separately. But arithmetic at one vertex is not enough. A vertex figure can balance its angles perfectly and still refuse to propagate: $(3,7,42)$ and $(4,5,20)$ both satisfy the equation yet tile nothing, because the arrangement they force at one vertex cannot be repeated consistently at its neighbours. Demanding the *same* configuration at **every** vertex — global consistency, not just local balance — cuts the 21 down to 11. Each survivor is named by its **vertex configuration**, the cyclic list of polygons around a vertex: $3^6$ for triangles, $4^4$ for squares, $6^3$ for hexagons, and mixed ones like $3.6.3.6$ (the trihexagonal tiling) or $4.8^2$ (truncated square). The name *is* the local picture, which is exactly what makes it a complete label for a vertex-transitive tiling.

**Building one.** Because a uniform tiling is periodic, it is fully described by one repeating block. Each tiling is stored as a translational **unit cell**: lattice vectors $b_1,b_2$ plus a short list of regular polygons of edge length 1 placed inside the cell. The whole patch is the cell stamped out along the lattice,

$$P=\bigcup_{i,j} T(i\,b_1+j\,b_2)\,(\text{cell tiles}),$$

where $T(v)$ is translation by $v$. Tiles that a shared cell boundary would duplicate are welded together when the mesh merges, so the seam between cells is invisible. The economy here is that a regular polygon of unit edge is pinned down by just its centre, its circumradius $R = 1/(2\sin(\pi/n))$ and one starting angle — so each tiling needs only a handful of hand-entered numbers, and the rest is generated.

**The Laves duals.** Dualising a tiling swaps the roles of vertices and faces: drop a new vertex at the centroid of every polygon, and join the centroids of all polygons that meet at a common old vertex. The ring of face-centres around one vertex closes up into a single new tile. Concretely, for each interior vertex $v$ of the Archimedean patch, gather the incident faces, take their centroids, and order them by angle about $v$ so the new tile's boundary does not self-cross:

$$\text{dual tile}(v)=\Big[\,\operatorname{centroid}(f)\ :\ v\in f\,\Big]\ \text{sorted by}\ \operatorname{atan2}(c_y-v_y,\;c_x-v_x).$$

Only *interior* vertices qualify. A vertex on the patch boundary is missing some of its faces, so its centroid ring is incomplete and would yield a truncated, wrong tile — which is precisely why the generator builds a patch larger than requested and trims the fringe afterwards. Geometrically the swap explains the counts: because a uniform tiling is *vertex*-transitive, its dual is *tile*-transitive (one tile repeated), and because the dual tile need not be regular, the Laves tilings escape the "regular polygons only" straitjacket. The duals of the 3 regular tilings are regular again — the square tiling is self-dual, triangles and hexagons trade places — so the Laves family adds only 8 genuinely new members rather than 11.

## References

- Johannes Kepler, "Harmonices Mundi" (1619) -- first systematic depiction of the regular and Archimedean (semiregular) tilings by regular polygons.
- Branko Grunbaum & G. C. Shephard, "Tilings and Patterns" (W. H. Freeman, 1987) -- modern classification of the uniform tilings and their Laves (dual) tilings.
