# Fractal Tiling

![Fractal Tiling](../images/fractal_tiling.png)

## Overview

Add a Fathauer fractal tiling patch.

Robert Fathauer's **fractal tilings** — "f-tilings" — are edge-to-edge tilings by a single prototile appearing in progressively smaller similar copies. The interesting inversion is where the fractal lives: the **interior** is a clean, gap-free tiling at every depth, and only the **limit boundary** is fractal, crinkling into self-similar lobes with singular wrap-around points.

That is the reverse of the usual arrangement. A Koch snowflake has a fractal boundary around a trivial interior; a Penrose tiling has an intricate interior with a boundary that is merely where you stopped. An f-tiling is genuinely both: a perfectly ordinary tiling whose edge cannot be reached in finitely many steps.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Fractals ▸ Fractal Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling** — the prototile and its symmetry. The three **Kite** rosettes (6-, 8- and 12-fold) start from a regular polygon of right kites and fan a ring of children onto every exposed tip; the **Triangle Segment** ($s=6,8,12$) and **Trapezoid Segment** ($u=6,10$) families instead grow a back-to-back "pod" of polygon-segment prototiles. Higher order shrinks faster and crinkles finer.
3. **Set Iterations** — the growth depth. Each generation is a similar copy scaled by the tile's own fixed ratio; deep growth is capped so the mesh stays manageable.
4. **Choose Color By** — *By Level* gives every generation its own material, making the shrinking halo of scales legible; *Uniform* is a single material.
5. **Shape the output.** *Margin* insets each tile toward its centroid, opening grout lines between tiles; *Relief Height* $>0$ extrudes the flat 2-D patch into 3-D relief.
6. **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own mesh object (parented to an empty) so tiles can be edited individually.
7. **Read the report** — vertex and face counts for a single mesh, or the tile count when the tiles are separated.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture tiling a floor with identical kite-shaped tiles, fanned out around a centre like the petals of a flower. When you reach the ragged edge, instead of stopping you open a second box of the *same* kites but smaller, and tuck them into the gaps around every point; then a third box, smaller still, and so on forever. The floor stays perfectly covered at every stage — no gaps, no overlaps — yet its outer edge never settles down: each new ring of little tiles adds another layer of crinkle, so in the limit the boundary is an infinitely wrinkled coastline while the inside remains a plain, tidy tiling. That upside-down arrangement — smooth interior, fractal edge — is the whole point of a Fathauer f-tiling. Everything below is the bookkeeping that makes each small tile fit its parent *exactly*, which is precisely what keeps the interior gap-free.

**The prototile — a right kite.** For an even rotational order $k$ the tile is a kite with apex angle $A=360°/k$ between its two **long** edges, two square ($90°$) side corners, and a sharp tip of $180°-A$ between its two **short** edges. Its short-to-long ratio is not a free choice — the two right angles force it — and it works out to

$$s=\frac{\text{short}}{\text{long}}=\tan\!\left(\frac{180°}{k}\right).$$

That one number is the seed of everything: it is both the factor each generation shrinks by *and* the reason the shrunk tiles fit. Geometrically it is pinned the moment you demand the side corners be right angles, so the tile already "knows" its own recursion ratio.

**Generation 0 — the rosette.** Place $k$ kites apex-to-apex around a centre, each long edge glued to a neighbour's. Their union is a regular $k$-gon — a hexagon for $k=6$ — with the kite tips at its corners and the $90°$ corners at its side midpoints. This is the fully-tiled starting patch, and every edge on its boundary is a **short** edge: the only kind that is ever left exposed.

**The recursion, and why it closes exactly.** At a lone exposed tip the material already present subtends the tip angle $180°-A$, so the *free* angle outside it is

$$360° - (180° - A) = 180° + A = \left(\tfrac{k}{2}+1\right)A.$$

Because that is a whole-number multiple of $A$, a **fan of $n=k/2+1$ children**, each scaled by $s$ with its apex pinned to the tip, sweeps the free angle with no gap and no overlap. This is exactly why $k$ must be even: $k/2+1$ has to be an integer, which is why the encyclopedia lists no odd-order kite f-tiling.

**The edges match too.** A child's long edge is $s\times$ the parent's long edge, which by the definition of $s$ is *precisely* the parent's short-edge length. So the two outermost children in each fan lay a long edge endpoint-to-endpoint along the parent's exposed short edges, while neighbours inside a fan pair long edges with each other. Where two fans from adjacent tips meet, their $90°$ corners add up to a straight $180°$; and where two tips actually coincide (first over the seed polygon's side midpoints) only $2A$ of angle remains free, filled by a fan of just two. Every interior vertex therefore closes to exactly $360°$, and anything left open lies on the outer boundary. Each of these coincidences traces back to the single relation $s=\tan(180°/k)$ — perturb the ratio and the tiling tears.

**Segment prototiles.** The same growth also runs on two families cut from a regular polygon by a chord (Fathauer, Bridges 2000). A chord spanning two edges of an $n$-gon leaves an isosceles **triangle** with two unit short edges and a long edge $2\cos(\pi/s)$, ratio $r_s=1/(2\cos(\pi/s))$, which closes only for even $s$; spanning three edges leaves an isosceles **trapezoid** with three unit short edges and a long base $1+2\cos(2\pi/u)$, ratio $r_u=1/(1+2\cos(2\pi/u))$, which closes only for $u\equiv 2\pmod 4$. Here generation 0 is a "pod" — two prototiles back-to-back on their long edge — and each later generation glues one $r$-scaled child, long edge first, onto every still-exposed short edge. The $u=6$ trapezoid pod is the exception in the set: its limit boundary stays a perfect regular hexagon, the one shipped kind that is *not* fractal.

**Where the fractal lives.** Each generation adds a halo of tiles $s$ (or $r$) times smaller. Since the ratio is below $1$ the total extent converges to a finite region — but the boundary gains a finer crinkle at every level and, in the limit, becomes a fractal curve, with singular points where infinitely many generations pile up (Fathauer's own renders show them as tiny white dots — uncovered pockets that shrink to points). The interior, by the exact-fit argument above, is a clean edge-to-edge tiling at every finite depth.
