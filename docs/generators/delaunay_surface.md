# Delaunay Surface

## Overview

Add a Delaunay surface: a surface of revolution of constant
mean curvature, traced by the focus of a rolling conic.

The **Delaunay surfaces** are the surfaces of revolution with constant mean curvature — the shapes a liquid bridge takes between two rings. Delaunay classified them completely in 1841 and, remarkably, gave a purely mechanical recipe for drawing them: **roll a conic along a line without slipping, and the path traced by one focus is the meridian.**

Which conic you roll picks the surface, and the whole family falls out of one construction:

| Rolled conic | Surface |
| --- | --- |
| Ellipse | **Unduloid** — a periodic chain of bulges |
| Circle | **Cylinder** (degenerate ellipse) |
| Ellipse, $e\to1$ | **Sphere** — a bead chain of tangent spheres |
| Parabola | **Catenoid** — the minimal, $H=0$ limit |
| Hyperbola | **Nodoid** — self-intersecting loops |

Beyond the classical five, the operator also builds three CMC surfaces that **no rolling conic reaches**: the **bubbleton** (a Delaunay surface with bubbles grafted on at unchanged mean curvature), the **Wente torus** (the first closed CMC torus, Wente's counterexample to Hopf's conjecture), and the **elastic torus** (a constrained-Willmore torus of revolution that is CMC in the round 3-sphere). These are the same one family — "CMC surface of revolution" — continued past what Delaunay's construction alone can draw, which is why they share a menu entry.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ Delaunay Surface*.
2. **Pick the Surface.** The classical five (Unduloid, Nodoid, Sphere Chain, Cylinder, Catenoid) are the rolling-conic surfaces; the three extras (Bubbleton, Wente Torus, Elastic Torus) continue the same CMC family past what the roulette can draw.
3. **Set the Neck Radius** — the radius of the narrowest parallel, the one real shape knob for the rolling-conic surfaces. The unduloid's bulge is then $2-\text{neck}$, so a small neck gives a beaded chain of near-spheres and neck $=1$ is the plain cylinder; the nodoid's bulge is $2+\text{neck}$; the catenoid reads it as its waist.
4. **Dial the mode-specific controls** that appear only for the exotic surfaces — the Bubbleton's Lobes/Covers (the resonance that closes each bubble) and its second-bubble options, the Wente Torus's Closure $l$ and Lobes, or the Elastic Torus's Lobes/Winding/Shape. Every combination still has constant mean curvature; these just choose *which* CMC surface.
5. **Read the report.** The exotic modes print a per-build check — the Wente and elastic tori report their measured mean curvature (and its deviation) and the elastic torus its Willmore energy against the closed-form value — so you can see the surface really is the CMC one it claims to be.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** A *surface of revolution* is what you get by drawing a curve and spinning it around an axis — a vase, a wine glass. *Constant mean curvature* (CMC) is the condition a soap film or a liquid bridge between two rings settles into: the surface is equally taut everywhere, curving the same amount, on average, at every point. Delaunay's 1841 discovery is that the profile curve you spin can be drawn **mechanically**: roll a conic — an ellipse, say — along a straight line like a coin rolling along a ruler, and follow one *focus* of that ellipse. The gently waving path the focus leaves behind is exactly the curve to revolve, and different conics (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) give the different surfaces in the table above. Everything below trades that rolling picture for the **equation the profile satisfies**, because an equation is what the code can integrate exactly and what makes the bulges, necks and self-crossings quantitative.

Not the 1841 roulette, then, but its modern **first integral** — exact, short, and numerically well behaved.

**Setting up.** Write the meridian in arclength $s$ as $(r(s), z(s))$ with tangent angle $\psi$, so $r'=\cos\psi$ and $z'=\sin\psi$. For a surface of revolution the two principal curvatures are $\psi'$ along the meridian and $\sin\psi/r$ along the parallel, so constant mean curvature $H$ means

$$\psi' + \frac{\sin\psi}{r} = 2H .$$

**The integral.** Multiply by $r$ and the left side turns out to be a total derivative:

$$\frac{d}{ds}\big(r\sin\psi\big)=\cos\psi\,\big(\sin\psi + r\psi'\big)=2Hrr'=H\,\frac{d}{ds}\big(r^2\big),$$

so the whole system collapses to

$$r\sin\psi = Hr^2 + c,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad \sin\psi = Hr + \frac{c}{r}.$$

Every Delaunay surface is one pair $(H, c)$, with $c$ the **Delaunay weight** — the flux through a parallel — and Delaunay's classification is nothing more than the sign of $c$.

**Bounded by the roots.** Fixing $H=\tfrac12$, so every mode shares one mean curvature and the family really is one family, gives $\sin\psi = r/2 + c/r$. Since $|\sin\psi|\le 1$, the radius is confined between the roots of

$$\tfrac{1}{2}r^2 \mp r + c = 0,$$

which is why an unduloid bulges and necks between two fixed radii instead of running away — the turning points are where $|\sin\psi|=1$ and the meridian doubles back.

**Why this exists alongside the relaxation route.** The [CMC / capillary generator](cmc_capillary.md) reaches CMC surfaces by constrained area minimisation. That is genuine CMC, but it cannot produce the **nodoids** — a minimiser is never self-intersecting — and it cannot be aimed at a prescribed weight. Delaunay's construction is exact, covers the whole family including the nodoids, and takes $c$ as an input.

Having both matters more than having either: the closed form and the relaxation are independent derivations of the same surfaces, so each checks the other.

### Bubbletons — grafting bubbles by Darboux transform

A **bubbleton** is a Delaunay surface with one or more bubbles grafted on **without disturbing its mean curvature**: away from its bubbles it is exactly the cylinder (or unduloid, or nodoid) it came from, and on a bounded stretch it carries a chain of lobes. Sterling and Wente named them; they are the **solitons of the sinh-Gordon equation** that governs CMC surfaces.

Rather than the usual loop-group dressing (which needs a numerical Iwasawa factorisation at every point), the code uses Cho–Leschke–Ogata (2022), who give the parallel sections of the flat connection **in closed form** (their Theorem 3.1), after which the **Darboux transform is purely algebraic** (their Theorem 2.2) — one quaternion per grid point, inverted and added, with no ODE integration. The Delaunay surface is written conformally as $f(x,y) = i\,p(x) + j\,q(x)\,e^{-iy}$ (so the $i$-axis is the axis of revolution) with, for necksize $r\in(-\infty,\tfrac12]$ and $M = 1 - (1 - \tfrac{1}{1-r})^2$,

$$q = (1-r)\,\mathrm{dn}\big((1-r)x,\,M\big),\qquad p' = q^2 + r(1-r),$$

where $r=\tfrac12$ is the cylinder, $0<r<\tfrac12$ the unduloids, and $r<0$ the nodoids. A spectral parameter $\mu$ is chosen at a **resonance point** so the transform closes up: with $a=(\mu+1/\mu)/2$ the eigenvalue split of the $y$-system is $t=\sqrt{1+2r(1-r)(a-1)}$, and the resonance condition is simply $t=n/m$ for coprime integers $n$ (**lobes**) and $m$ (**covers**). The parallel sections bring in the elliptic integral of the **third** kind, $\Pi$. A **second** bubble is grafted by **Bianchi permutability** — this is the *Double bubbleton (unduloid)* preset, two grafted bubbles at once.

### The Wente torus — a closed CMC surface

Hopf conjectured (1951) that every closed CMC surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a round sphere; Wente disproved it in 1986 by building a CMC **torus**. The construction here follows Walter's explicit description. In isothermic curvature-line coordinates with the Hopf differential normalised to 1, the Gauss equation reduces to **sinh-Gordon**,

$$\Delta F + 4H\sinh(F) = 0,$$

whose principal curvatures are $H(1\pm e^{-F})$ — mean curvature $H$ by construction. Walter's separable ansatz solves it in Jacobi $\mathrm{cn}$,

$$F = 4\,\mathrm{artanh}\!\big(\gamma\,\mathrm{cn}_k(\alpha x)\;\bar\gamma\,\mathrm{cn}_{\bar k}(\bar\alpha y)\big),$$

with $k=\sin\theta$, $\gamma=\sqrt{\tan\theta}$ and the analogous barred quantities. What makes it a **torus** rather than a strip is closure: over one $x$-period the surface is carried to itself by a rigid motion, and that motion must be a **pure rotation** (this fixes $\bar\theta \approx 65.35°$) whose angle $\Omega$ is a **rational multiple of $2\pi$**. As $\theta$ runs over its interval $\Omega$ sweeps $0$ to $360°$, so each rational $l/n = 1 + \Omega/360$ in the open interval $(1,2)$ is hit exactly once — Walter's closure condition (6.A). The operator exposes exactly this fraction as **Closure l** and **Lobes** $n$.

### The elastic torus — the spherical member ($S^3$)

Delaunay's rolling conics exhaust the CMC surfaces of revolution of **Euclidean** space, and none of them is compact (the sphere chain merely touches itself). The CMC surfaces of revolution of the **spherical** space form *do* close up into compact tori, and they are reached by a completely different route (Heller 2013): rotate a closed $n$-lobed **elastic** curve of the hyperbolic plane — a critical point of $\int \kappa^2\,ds$ — about the boundary axis of the upper half-plane. The resulting torus is **constrained Willmore** and has **constant mean curvature in the round 3-sphere**. The first-integral machinery above plays no role here: $\sin\psi = Hr + c/r$ presumes $H$ constant in $\mathbb{R}^3$, which these tori are not — their meridian obeys the elastica equation

$$\kappa'' + \tfrac12\kappa^3 + (\mu-1)\,\kappa = 0$$

in $H^2$ instead. The mathematics (Weierstrass closed forms, closure, gauges) lives in `constrained_willmore_generator`; this operator is only its revolution front-end, because "CMC surface of revolution" is one family and should be one menu entry. Each build reports the measured $H$ in $S^3$ (and its deviation) and the Willmore energy against Heller's closed form, as a per-build check.
