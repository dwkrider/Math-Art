# k-Uniform Tiling

![k-Uniform Tiling](../images/kuniform.png)

## Overview

Add a 2-uniform Euclidean tiling.

A **2-uniform tiling** covers the plane edge-to-edge with regular polygons, but its vertices fall into exactly **two** transitivity classes instead of one — a single step less regular than the 11 [uniform tilings](tiling.md). There are 20 of them, enumerated by Krötenheerdt in 1969.

They occupy an appealing middle ground: regular enough to look deliberate, irregular enough to be surprising. Many of the handsomest are *layered* — periodic stacks of two 1-uniform strips, such as a row of squares alternating with a double band of triangles — and those layered families are what this generator builds.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ k-Uniform Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling.** The six layered members are named by their **two vertex figures** — the pair of distinct configurations that make the tiling 2-uniform. *3.3.3.3.3.3 / 3.3.3.4.4 (a)* and *(b)* are a square row against a double or triple triangle band; *4.4.4.4 / 3.3.3.4.4 (a)* and *(b)* stack a double or triple square row on a single triangle band; *3.6.3.6 / 3.3.3.3.6* rides a triangle band on a hexagon-and-triangle strip; and *3.6.3.6 / 3.3.6.6* sets hexagons in aligned columns with triangle-rhombus gaps.
3. **Set the patch size** with **Cells X** and **Cells Y** — copies of the layered unit cell along each lattice direction (1–40 each).
4. **Choose the colouring** with **Color By**: *By Sides* colours each polygon by side count, *By Tile Type* by its orbit within the cell, *Uniform* a single material.
5. **Shape the output.** **Margin** opens grout lines by insetting each tile; **Relief Height** extrudes flat tiles into prisms above 0; **Trim Boundary** clips the patch to a clean rectangle; **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own object.
6. **Read the report.** The header prints the tiling's name with its vertex and face counts (or tile count when separated), confirming the patch built as requested.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Picture the regular tilings as ribbons: a ribbon of squares, a ribbon of triangles, a ribbon of hexagons. Each ribbon on its own is perfectly regular — every corner along it looks the same. Now stack two *different* ribbons in a repeating pattern, say a row of squares on top of a double band of triangles. Deep inside a band, corners still look uniform; but right along the seam where square meets triangle, the corners are a *different* kind of place, and no symmetry of the whole pattern can slide a seam-corner onto an interior-corner. So the finished floor has exactly **two** kinds of corner instead of one — that is what "2-uniform" means, and stacking regular ribbons is exactly how this generator makes them.

**What "k-uniform" counts.** Every vertex of an edge-to-edge tiling by regular polygons still has to fill a full turn, so it obeys the same angle condition as the uniform case,

$$\sum_i \frac{n_i-2}{n_i}=2,$$

and each individual vertex is therefore one of the 21 admissible configurations. The number $k$ measures something different: not what the vertices *look* like but how the pattern's symmetry group *sorts* them. Two vertices are in the same **orbit** if some symmetry of the tiling carries one exactly onto the other. A 1-uniform tiling has a single orbit — the group is transitive, every vertex reachable from every other. A 2-uniform tiling has two orbits: there are genuinely two kinds of place to stand, and no symmetry maps between them. The subtlety worth holding onto is that this is a statement about the *group*, not the *appearance* — two vertices can share the very same configuration and still fall in different orbits, because sameness of local picture does not imply a symmetry connecting them.

**Layered cells.** The members built here realise those two orbits as the *interior* and the *seam* of a stack of 1-uniform strips. Take a row of squares (locally $4^4$) above a double band of triangles (locally $3^6$): each strip is uniform in isolation, but the seam vertices become inequivalent to the interior ones, and the stack is 2-uniform. As with any periodic tiling, one translational unit cell captures everything — lattice vectors $b_1,b_2$ and a list of unit-edge regular polygons — replicated along the lattice,

$$P=\bigcup_{i,j} T(i\,b_1+j\,b_2)\,(\text{cell tiles}).$$

The lattice vectors are dictated by how tall the strips are: a triangle band of unit edge adds $\sqrt3/2$ of height per row, a square band adds $1$, so a cell of one square row over two triangle rows repeats vertically with $b_2=(0,\,1+\sqrt3)$ — and the horizontal component of $b_2$ is chosen so the strips register, i.e. the seams of one copy land on the seams of the next.

**Coverage, not appearance, is the test.** A hand-derived cell can look flawless and still hide a defect: a hairline sliver left uncovered, or two tiles overlapping by a whisker at a seam. Neither shows at a glance, so the check is numerical, not visual. Each cell is coverage-verified by sampling a $35\times35$ grid of points across the patch, every point nudged by a small irrational offset ($+0.0137$ in $x$, $+0.0071$ in $y$) so no sample can land exactly on an edge and give an ambiguous answer. Each point must lie in **exactly one** tile: a point in none is a gap, a point in two or more is an overlap, and both count as defects that must be zero. A second check canonicalises the cyclic side-count sequence around each interior vertex and counts the distinct figures — a genuine 2-uniform tiling returns exactly two. Together these certify the *tiling*, which is what a cell entered by hand actually needs, rather than certifying the picture.
