# D-Form

![D-Form](../images/dform.png)

## Overview

Add a D-form: a solid glued from two flat equal-perimeter sheets.

A **D-form** is made by cutting two flat pieces whose outlines have the *same perimeter* and gluing them edge to edge. They cannot lie flat once joined, so the join pops into a closed solid — curved everywhere, yet stretched nowhere. It is paper across its whole surface, and **all** of its Gaussian curvature lives on the single seam.

Tony Wills invented them and John Sharp brought them to the mathematical-art community. Demaine and O'Rourke proved the crucial fact: the resulting solid is the **convex hull of its own seam curve**, which is what makes the shape well posed at all — the two outlines and the point at which they are joined determine it uniquely.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Surfaces ▸ D-Form*.
2. **Pick the Mode.** **Seam (two sheets)** is the classic D-form, solved for; **Anti (holes joined)** is Sharp's anti-D-form — two annuli glued along their *holes* into an open saddle collar carrying $-4\pi$; **Truncated (sliced polyhedron)** builds one exactly by rounding a polyhedron's edges into a developable band (no solver); **Folded Vesica Piscis** folds two overlapping discs, a closed-form relative with interior creases.
3. **Choose the two outlines** (Seam / Anti). **Piece A** and **Piece B** each pick a curve — Ellipse, Superellipse, Egg / Oval, Rounded Polygon or Cassinian Oval — with an **A / B Aspect** and any curve-specific knob (Superellipse n, Egg Bias, Sides + Corner Radius, Cassini b/a). Their perimeters are matched for you, so you never balance them by hand.
4. **Work the shape controls.** **Join Offset** is the one that matters most: it slides where the two outlines first meet, redistributing curvature around the seam and often remaking the form entirely — without ever changing the total. **Flip Second Piece** glues the second outline reversed. **Seam Segments** sets the seam resolution and **Solver Quality** the iteration budget (raise it if the report shows high strain).
5. **Mode-specific controls.** Anti adds the **Hole A / B** outlines (these are the glued pair) and **Hole Size**; Truncated adds **Seed Polyhedron**, **Cut Edges**, **Truncation Rounds** and **Cut Depth**; the vesica adds **Disc Offset u** and **Apex Height**.
6. **Outputs and checks.** **Flat Development** also lays out the exact pieces to cut; **Sharp Seam** marks the crease (a D-form is smooth everywhere but the seam); **Seam Curvature Attribute** paints the per-vertex angle defect. The report prints strain %, the measured seam curvature against its $4\pi$ ideal, and the iteration count — with per-mode extras (Anti: fold percentile and sheet crossings; Truncated: face planarity, valence-3 conical, genus; Vesica: max and circular-crease heights).

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Cut two flat shapes out of card whose edges are the *same length* all the way round, then tape them together rim to rim, the way the front and back of a cushion are sewn. A cloth cushion would just lie flat and wrinkle — but stiff card cannot wrinkle and cannot stretch, so the only way it can close up is to bulge outward into a smooth, rounded solid. That puffed shape is a **D-form**. Every part of its surface is still flat card you could peel off and lay down again; the *only* place it truly bends is along the taped seam, which is where all of the roundness has been forced to collect.

**Why it must pop out.** Each piece is flat, so its interior has zero Gaussian curvature, and gluing does not stretch it — the pieces stay *developable*. Yet a closed surface must carry total curvature $4\pi$ by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. With the interiors contributing nothing, **all** $4\pi$ is concentrated on the seam, and a surface with its curvature on a curve cannot lie flat there: the join is forced up into space. Equal perimeter is what lets the two rims be matched point for point — the point at arclength $s$ on one outline is glued to the point at arclength $s$ on the other — so the seam is well defined all the way round.

**The shape is the hull of the seam.** Demaine and O'Rourke proved the solid is exactly the **convex hull of its seam curve**: no point of the paper can poke outside the smallest convex body containing the seam. The generator turns that theorem into an algorithm. It triangulates the two flat pieces, glues them along the matched seam, and then **settles** the mesh by alternating three demands until they agree: *isometry* (every edge keeps its flat length, so the paper never stretches), *hull projection* (any vertex that has drifted outside the convex hull of the current seam is clipped back in — the theorem used as a constraint), and a gentle *bending / convexity* push that inflates the sheet outward so it finds the plump solution rather than the flat, doubly-covered pillow, which is isometric too but degenerate. The hull itself is never built: a convex body is the intersection of its supporting half-spaces, so clipping against a few hundred directions on the sphere approximates it cheaply.

**The join point is the interesting control.** Rotating one outline before gluing — starting the arclength pairing somewhere else — cannot add or remove curvature. Gauss–Bonnet pins the total at $4\pi$ whatever you do. It only **slides the curvature around the seam**, moving where the solid is sharply bent and where it is nearly flat. Same two pieces, same total, a wholly different object. The **Seam Curvature** colour attribute paints the per-vertex angle defect $\mu$, which is zero off the seam and sums to $4\pi$ along it, so the redistribution is literally visible as you move the join.

**The other modes** change the closure rather than the outlines. The **anti-D-form** glues two annuli along their *holes* with the outer rims left free; those free rims let Gauss–Bonnet balance a seam that now carries $-4\pi$, making it a saddle collar with no convexity theory to lean on, so the solver instead works seam-first with a bending energy. The **truncated** mode is exact and needs no solver: it rounds a convex polyhedron's edges (a Chaikin-like corner cut) until the cut band reads as a developable strip, leaving a valence-3 conical mesh that can be given real thickness. The **folded vesica piscis** folds two overlapping discs in closed form into cylindrical and conical patches joined by curved creases — a relative of a D-form (one sheet, interior creases, not convex) rather than one.
