# Aperiodic Tiling

![Aperiodic Tiling](../images/aperiodic.png)

## Overview

Add an aperiodic tiling patch: a finite disc of a plane tiling that repeats locally everywhere yet never as a whole.

An **aperiodic** tiling covers the plane with a small set of tiles that *cannot* be arranged periodically. Every finite patch recurs infinitely often, yet no translation ever maps the whole tiling to itself — order without repetition. Penrose found his two-tile sets in the 1970s, and the question they answer is genuinely surprising: a shape can force non-periodicity, so "tiles the plane" and "tiles the plane periodically" are different properties. The 2023 "einstein" monotiles (the hat and the spectre) push it further still — a *single* tile that admits only aperiodic tilings — and this one operator builds all of them.

The physical sequel is better still. Penrose tilings show sharp diffraction peaks with **five-fold** symmetry, which the crystallographic restriction theorem forbids in any periodic crystal. When Dan Shechtman measured exactly that in a real alloy in 1982 he was widely disbelieved; quasicrystals earned him the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

### Using it

1. **Add it** from *Add ▸ Mesh ▸ Math Art ▸ Patterns ▸ Aperiodic Tiling*.
2. **Pick the Tiling.** The two Penrose sets are **Penrose Rhombs (P3)** (thick and thin rhombs) and **Penrose Kite & Dart (P2)**; **Ammann-Beenker (Octagonal)** is the eight-fold square-and-rhomb tiling. The rest are the 2023 single-tile einsteins and their continuous family: **Hat**, **Turtle**, **Chevron** and **Comet** are named members of Tile(a,b), **Custom Tile(a,b)** exposes the whole family through one angle, and **Spectre (Chiral Monotile)** is the equilateral member that tiles with no reflections at all.
3. **Set Generations** — the deflation (or, for Ammann-Beenker, grid) depth. Each Penrose step multiplies the tile count by roughly $\varphi^2\approx2.6$, so the patch grows fast; the cap is 7.
4. **Dial the mode-specific controls** that appear only for some tilings (the operator's UI hides the rest):
   - **P2 Seed** — only for Penrose Kite & Dart: the central patch the deflation grows from, **Sun (Five Kites)**, **Star (Five Darts)** or **Cartwheel**.
   - **Phase** and **Asymmetry** — only for Ammann-Beenker: Phase slides through the family of octagonal tilings; Asymmetry breaks the 8-fold symmetry (0 = the symmetric tiling).
   - **Kite Semi-Angle** — only for Custom Tile(a,b): the angle $A$ that sets the two edge lengths $a=\cos A$, $b=\sin A$ (60° = Hat, 30° = Turtle, 45° = Spectre).
   - **Curved Edges** — only for Spectre: replace each straight edge with the standard S-curve, which upgrades the weakly-chiral straight tile to the *strictly* chiral monotile.
5. **Choose Color By.** **By Tile Type** colours each tile kind (thin/thick, kite/dart, square/rhomb, reflected/mystic); **By Cluster** and **By Supertile Level** expose the substitution hierarchy and apply to the einstein monotiles only; **Uniform** is one material.
6. **Set the output.** **Margin** insets each tile to leave grout lines, **Relief Height** extrudes flat tiles into 3D, **Trim Boundary** clips the ragged disc to a clean rectangle, and **Separate Tiles** emits each tile as its own object. The operator's info line reports the vertex/face count (or tile count when separated), a quick check the patch built.

## How it works

**In plain terms.** Think of a rubber stamp that, instead of printing a shape, prints a little *scene* of smaller shapes in its place. Start with a handful of tiles, stamp every one of them — each is replaced by a shrunken cluster of tiles — and repeat. The patch spreads outward while the tiles get finer, and in the limit the whole plane is covered. The trick is that the shrink factor is an *irrational* number (the golden ratio for the Penrose tilings), and that single fact is what makes the finished pattern cover the plane while never repeating exactly: a repeating wallpaper would need a whole number of each tile in its unit cell, and an irrational ratio simply cannot be a whole-number ratio. The Penrose and einstein tilings below are built this way; Ammann-Beenker uses a different, equally exact recipe (slicing a grid) explained at the end.

**Deflation.** The Penrose families are built by *substitution*: start from a small seed patch, replace every tile by a scaled-down group of tiles, and repeat. The patch grows and the tiles shrink, and in the limit the whole plane is covered. Crucially the subdivision rule is not applied to the rhombs directly but to the **Robinson triangles** they are cut from, because those subdivide cleanly — the rhombs are reassembled only at the end, by merging triangle pairs across their shared base edge.

**Penrose P3 (thick and thin rhombs).** The two Robinson triangles are the acute *golden triangle* (apex $36^\circ$) and the obtuse *golden gnomon* (apex $108^\circ$), so named because in both the ratio of long side to short is the golden ratio

$$\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}=2\cos\frac{\pi}{5}.$$

That $\varphi$ is baked into the subdivision geometrically: each cut point is placed a fraction $1/\varphi$ of the way along a leg, which is exactly why the children come out $\varphi$ times smaller and stay golden triangles. Each deflation step subdivides every triangle into those children — a thin triangle into one thin plus one thick, a thick into two thick plus one thin — starting from a ten-triangle "sun" seed with five-fold symmetry. The resulting edge-to-edge triangle mesh is then merged back along shared base edges into the thin ($36^\circ/144^\circ$) and thick ($72^\circ/108^\circ$) rhombs.

**Penrose P2 (kite and dart).** The same machinery on its own half-tiles: the half-kite (an acute golden triangle, $36$–$72$–$72$) and the half-dart (a golden gnomon, $36$–$36$–$108$). A half-kite becomes two half-kites plus a half-dart, a half-dart becomes one of each, every child $\varphi$ times smaller; keeping track of which leg is each half-tile's *symmetry axis* is what lets mirror halves be re-joined afterward. Those pairs merge across their axis into the full **kite**, a convex quadrilateral with angles $72/72/144/72$, and the **dart**, an arrowhead with angles $72/36/216/36$ and one reflex vertex. Both tiles use only two edge lengths, in golden ratio. The starting patch is selectable — the **Sun** (five kites meeting at the centre), the **Star** (five darts, its dual), or Conway's **Cartwheel** (the decagonal hub that sits at the heart of *every* kite-and-dart tiling, obtained as the sun grown by two more rings).

**Why $\varphi$ forces aperiodicity.** After $n$ deflations the ratio of thick to thin tiles (equivalently kites to darts) tends to $\varphi$, and $\varphi$ is irrational. A periodic tiling would have to repeat a finite unit cell containing a whole number of each tile, giving a *rational* ratio. So no periodic arrangement of these tiles exists — the irrationality of the golden ratio is the obstruction, and the module's self-test confirms the measured kite:dart ratio really does approach $\varphi$.

**The einstein monotiles (hat, turtle, chevron, comet, spectre).** In 2023 a *single* aperiodic tile — an "einstein", from the German *ein Stein*, "one stone" — was finally found. Its home is the same substitution idea, but now the objects being stamped are **metatiles**: named clusters of hats (labelled H, T, P and F) that inflate on a fixed rule, each larger H/T/P/F built from a specific arrangement of the previous generation's clusters. The hat itself is a 13-sided polygon of eight kites cut from the $[3.4.6.4]$ "kite lattice"; because one hat inside every H metatile is a *mirror image*, the finished tiling is forced to include reflected hats — asymptotically about one in seven, which the self-test checks. All of **Hat**, **Turtle**, **Chevron** and **Comet** share one 14-edge combinatorial outline: eight edges of length $a$ and six of length $b$, in fixed directions that are multiples of $30^\circ$, so a member is fixed by the single pair $(a,b)$. The hat is $\text{Tile}(1,\sqrt3)$ (where two collinear $a$-edges merge to give its familiar 13-vertex shape), the turtle $\text{Tile}(\sqrt3,1)$, and the degenerate chevron $\text{Tile}(0,1)$ and comet $\text{Tile}(1,0)$ collapse one edge family to zero length. Rather than re-deriving the substitution for each shape, the code runs the exact hat substitution *once* to fix the combinatorics — which tile mates which, and each tile's rotation or reflection — then re-lays the parametrized $\text{Tile}(a,b)$ edge-to-edge by a **developing map**, walking outward from one seed tile and matching shared edges as it goes.

**The spectre.** The equilateral member $\text{Tile}(1,1)$ is special: it tiles by rotations and translations *only*. But with straight edges it is merely *weakly* chiral — reflected copies, if allowed, could still form periodic tilings. Replacing every edge with a point-symmetric **S-curve** (a shallow cubic Bézier that bulges to opposite sides of the edge midpoint) removes that loophole: a reflected copy's edges no longer nest with the originals, so the curved **Spectre** is a *strictly* chiral monotile, tiling the plane with a single handedness and no reflections at all. It is grown by its own nine-metatile substitution (Gamma through Psi), with the Gamma "mystic" cluster carrying a bonded pair of spectres.

**Ammann–Beenker.** The eight-fold tiling of squares and $45^\circ$ rhombs is *not* built by substitution here but by de Bruijn's **multigrid dual** method — the same construction his References-listed paper gives for Penrose. Take four families of equally spaced parallel lines ("grids") whose normals point at $0^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ, 135^\circ$, each family shifted by a generic offset $\gamma_j$ chosen so no three lines are ever concurrent. Every intersection of two grid lines becomes one tile, whose four corners are the images of the four grid cells meeting there; perpendicular families ($90^\circ$ apart) produce a **square**, adjacent families ($45^\circ$ apart) a **rhomb**. Sliding all four offsets together (the **Phase** control) walks through the whole family of octagonal tilings, and spreading them apart (**Asymmetry**) breaks the 8-fold symmetry. Building it as a grid dual, rather than by the silver-ratio $1+\sqrt2$ inflation, lets every patch be coverage-verified directly.

- N. G. de Bruijn, "Algebraic theory of Penrose's non-periodic tilings of the plane" (1981) -- the multigrid / pentagrid dual method used here for Ammann-Beenker.
- Robert Ammann and F. P. M. Beenker -- the octagonal Ammann-Beenker tiling (Beenker report, 1982).
- David Smith, Joseph Samuel Myers, Craig S. Kaplan & Chaim Goodman-Strauss, "An aperiodic monotile" and "A chiral aperiodic monotile" (2023) -- the "hat" and "spectre" einsteins, the continuous Tile(a,b) family that interpolates hat, turtle, chevron and comet, and the curved-edge Spectre that is strictly chiral.
